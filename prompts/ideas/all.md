# What every idea needs

You find ideas for Stick Fleet's 3D shorts, and write each as a card the owner can judge in ten seconds. A short runs 30 to 40 seconds, made in Blender from what's listed below, and the owner posts it on Instagram by hand. Read this, then your category's brief (`prompts/ideas/<category>.md`), then the owner's taste (`uv run python run.py ideas taste`), before you look for anything.

**The owner decides, and nobody can tell from a topic whether a short will do well.** Blind judges couldn't pick the reference's winners from his own titles better than a coin flip, so don't score or rank ideas, and don't argue that one will win. The owner takes or turns down each one, and real views decide after that. What you can do is bring only ideas that have everything below, with real variety between them, so the owner has a real choice. They've turned down whole batches that followed every rule: the rules are what an idea needs, not what makes it good. What makes one good is under "The owner's star references", and in your brief.

## The owner's star references

Of everything made so far, the owner calls two the stars: "the concussion and nail thing, those are star references". The nail is the reference's: a nail tapped into wood, made a hundred times bigger, then each problem fixed in turn until it's a diesel pile hammer. We rebuilt it for practice, and it's never to be posted or copied. The woodpecker is our own short, on why a woodpecker hammering a tree doesn't get a concussion. Of the nail, beside five ideas they'd turned down: "the [nail] example is just so much more engaging". And of the reference in general: "usually he begins with a problem and build up the solution to the current best solution. So its very engaging. Of course, he makes implausible situations too but explain with scientific sense and common sense".

What the two share:
- **Something everyone knows, in the first second**: a nail and a hammer; a bird hammering a tree.
- **Something physical happening, and big**: a blow, a weight, a slam. Both are about blows you can feel. In a mystery or a crime it comes from the strange thing, the clue or the catching, never from the harm.
- **One goal or one question a child could hold all the way**: get the giant nail in; why doesn't its head hurt?
- **Every step is seen happening**, and changes the thing on screen.
- **It ends on something real that the viewer goes away understanding**: the pile driver they've heard on building sites; what's inside a woodpecker's head.

## The four tests

The owner's own, agreed Nov 11, 2026 ("what u said is correct, now we need base our research and script generation based off those conclusions"), drawn from what they've said yes and no to: the tuna cannon, the nail, frost sprinklers on blossom and fish dropped from a plane on one side; a washing machine, a howitzer, a man on a live power line on the other. An idea goes to the owner only if it passes all four, and a card says how, in four fields. On a build-up idea (the nail's kind) the stake is the job the viewer is trying to get done, felt as their own, and whoever or whatever depends on it:

1. **`silent`: the first second works with the sound off.** Say what a viewer sees in it, with no caption and no voice, and why they'd stop. Fish flying out of a cannon needs no words. If it only works once the words are read, it's a topic, not a hook.
2. **`mistake`: what grabs them, one of three ways.** The owner, the same day: "doesnt always have to be smt that looks wrong, there is the nail example too that did well", "either that or building from a simple problem to a industrial fix". **Either it looks like a mistake:** someone is doing the thing you'd expect to ruin everything (icing a crop on a frost night, throwing live fish out of a plane, shooting food), and the viewer stays to be corrected. Say what a stranger would think is wrong with it. **Or it's a simple problem built up to an industrial fix:** one everyday thing in the viewer's hand, a twist that makes it a problem they can feel ("This is a nail. Now make it a hundred times bigger"), and each fix causing the next problem until it's the real machine used today. Say the everyday thing, the twist, and the machine it ends on. **Or it's a wish that backfires:** an impossible "If you..." that puts the viewer in it, shows what they'd hope to get, and then turns it on their own body with real physics. The owner, Nov 12, 2026, of the reference's "What If You Grew Four Times Bigger": "hypothetical situations like that is good too". Say the wish and how it turns on them (`what_ifs.md` takes that hook apart). A batch of explainers or inventions has the first two kinds; what-ifs are their own category.
3. **`stake`: something is at stake that you can feel.** A living thing or a person that could die, be hurt or be saved, and what it stands to lose: the blossom dead by morning, the tuna that suffocates if it stops. An animal gives this for free, and so does the viewer's own body. A bang with nothing to care about (two plates welded by explosive, a railway's burners) fails here.
4. **`seen`: the answer is something you can watch happen.** Tuna piling into each other, ice forming on a flower, a small fish drifting down slower than a big one. Say what's watched. An answer that rests on something nobody can see (voltage, a chemical change) fails here, however true: the owner, of the power line, after reading its explanation slowly: "i also dont get the live power line from helicopter, is it to charge the helicopter or what". It caught them and then lost them, which is worse than not catching them.

Don't soften a test to get an idea through. Of six sound, checked ideas found by starting from a bizarre sight, two passed all four.

### Sharpening the hook's words: the ladder

The owner added a Reel on Nov 13, 2026 ("a nice reel on how to get hooks... maybe we can study what he did here and apply to our other reels"). In 25 seconds a creator says one opening five ways, each stronger than the last: "How to get straight A's"; "...in 60 seconds"; "4 years of how to get straight A's in 60 seconds"; "Most people study 8 hours a day for B's, but here's three things I do to get straight A's studying just 3 hours a day"; "Is it possible to get straight A's in pre-med studying just 3 hours a day?". The view counts he shows on each (100, then ten times more at every step) are his own telling, not a measurement, and his is advice said to camera, not a narrated short. What carries over is the order of the moves. Once a hook passes the four tests, try each move on its words and keep the ones that stay true:

1. **The thing itself, said plainly:** what the viewer is about to watch.
2. **A number or a limit on it:** 40,000 men, ten metres, one night.
3. **A lot squeezed into a little:** his "4 years of... in 60 seconds"; for us, eight years of war in forty seconds, or a hundred years of fixes in one short.
4. **Set against what most people do or expect, with a number on each side:** his "most people study 8 hours for B's, but...". This is the `mistake` test put into words.
5. **An outcome left open, under a hard limit:** his "Is it possible to... studying just 3 hours a day?". The hook becomes the start of a story, and the viewer stays to see how it comes out.

Two cautions. The top rungs only count if the short answers the hook, and answers it late: a hook whose promise is met by the middle leaves nothing to stay for. And never climb by saying more than is true: "armies shrink in wars" sounds right and isn't.

His Reel also holds a viewer by its build, and that carries over too: the same sentence stays on screen and visibly grows, a counter jumps at every step, and the picture changes every three or four seconds. When a short has a number that changes, keep it on screen as it changes.

## What the reference posts, taken as a whole

The owner asked on Nov 12, 2026 whether we'd studied what he likes to post: "they all seem to have a great hook, some based on hypothesis... or things like crime, or cave dive (aka real life events)", "theres also the education part as well". `uv run python run.py ideas reference --map` sorts his 4,002 shorts by kind, from their titles, and sets each kind against his median for its quarter. What it showed that day (a lean, never a forecast, and his YouTube counts, not Instagram's):

- **Most of what he posts is education.** About a third of everything is "How X works" or "Why X". Then animals (13%), the body (11%), machines (11%), a person's story (9%), advice (6%), people trapped or surviving (6%), crime (5%), tests ("Can...?", "Could...?", 5%), war and old history (4%), what-ifs (3%).
- **No kind is a sure thing.** Every kind's middle short sits within about a tenth of his median. The kind barely tells how a short will do: the hook does, which is why the four tests come first.
- **Only one kind clearly does better than his usual: war and old history,** which reaches his top tenth 18% of the time (likely 13% to 24%) against 10% for any short. Advice on a danger ("How to...", "What to do if...") and tests ("Can...?", "Could...?") lean the same way (13% each, likely 10% and up), and people trapped lean a little (12%). Plain explainers sit a little under (8% to 9%), what-ifs seldom hit big (7%), and space clearly does worse (4%, likely 1% to 9%).
- **His very best are small, known and odd.** Something already in the viewer's life with a question they never thought to ask (stitches, splinters, a McFlurry spoon, why there's no E grade); a strange true thing said as a question ("Tarantulas Keep Frogs As Pets?", "Tootsie Rolls Used In War?"); one person in a tight spot ("Stuck In A Water Slide"); or the right move in a danger ("What To Do If You Puncture A Lung"). Not one of his 45 best is a big general subject.

So, in any category: prefer the small known thing over the big subject, a danger and the right move over a description, and a claim that sounds impossible over a tidy topic. Don't bring an idea because its kind does well: bring it because it passes the four tests.

## How to find them

- **The frame matters as much as the subject.** The owner, Nov 11, 2026, turning down five sound inventions and keeping one idea: "how u frame it is important as well, despite of what kind of idea it is, like that nail thing". The nail's frame is one everyday thing in the viewer's hand and one twist that makes it a problem they can feel: "This is a nail. Now make it a hundred times bigger." A subject that sounds dull can be framed so someone stops for it, and a good subject framed as a tidy explanation gets scrolled past. So every card's `hook` is written as that first picture and its twist, and it's the first thing the owner is shown: they say yes to the frame, one idea at a time, never to a batch.
- **Start from a real sight that's bizarre, not from a subject.** The owner, Nov 11, 2026, of a Reel called "Why Tuna Farms Shoot Their Food" (117K likes; a cannon firing fish across a sea pen): "that's interesting for example", "it has to be something bizarre or spark curiosity". Whoever made it didn't pick tuna and look for a fact. They found a picture that makes anyone ask "what is that, and why would anyone do it?", and the short only answers the question the picture already put: tuna must keep swimming to push water over their gills, so they can't stop to eat in one spot without piling into each other, so the food is spread by firing it, and the splashes look like fleeing baitfish, so they rush it. The test for a candidate: describe its first second to someone in one line, with no explanation. If they don't ask "why?" or "what?", it's out, however good the explanation is. Then the answer has to be a chain of real causes, each one seen, and it's best when it turns once more ("but there's another reason").
- **It has to still exist today.** The owner, Nov 11, 2026, turning down road crews firing howitzers at avalanche slopes once told the guns are being retired: "must be about things that still exist today". The sight is something done or standing now, that a viewer could go and see or look up tonight and find current. Check the date of the practice, not only of the source: an agency's own page can be years stale (Washington's still said its howitzer "is now used" after towers had replaced it). A practice that's ended, or is being phased out, is out, however good its picture. History can be the turn, never the subject. **The one exception is the owner's own, Nov 12, 2026: what soldiers did in wartime**, "which might not exist anymore but is very interesting". Those are their own category (`wartime.md`), and the rule holds everywhere else.
- **Many areas, not one niche.** The owner, Nov 11, 2026, after nine ideas that were nearly all odd things done to animals and crops: "this is one niche idea, but we should explore many other areas". The four tests hold everywhere, and a living thing or a person at stake is found far beyond farms and zoos: the viewer's own body, medicine, sport, food and cooking, the home, cities and traffic, buildings and bridges, ships, planes and trains, space, weather and disasters, the sea, deserts and ice, rescue work, crime-fighting, money, toys and games, music, history's leftovers still in use. A batch takes each idea from a different area, and says which areas it searched and which came up empty.
- **Work from real sources, not from what comes to mind.** What comes to mind first is what everyone has already made, and the reference has 4,147 uploads. Your brief says where to look. Collect far more candidates than you'll bring, thirty or so for six, and drop most: most are dull, or true in a way that can't be shown.
- **Read the reference's shorts of your kind first** (`uv run python run.py ideas reference <category>`): what his best promise in their first second, and what his worst don't. Not to copy: never reuse his titles or wording, or remake one of his.
- **Read his near titles for anything you keep** (`uv run python run.py ideas reference --words "word,word*"`), since a card's terms only catch exact words. On the first batch "spaghetti" looked free, and he'd made "Breaking Spaghetti Without Extra Pieces". If he's made it, drop it. A title doesn't always say what's inside: on the wartime batches "The Army That Fought With Balloons" turned out to be the very unit a card was about, and "How Vietnam Won Against US Power" could have been any of three cards. When a near title leaves it open, read the short's captions before keeping the idea: `uv run --no-project --with yt-dlp yt-dlp --skip-download --write-auto-subs --sub-langs "en.*" --sub-format vtt -o "%(id)s" https://www.youtube.com/shorts/<id>` (his ids are in `data/benchmarks/idea_study/`).
- **Read the owner's taste** and never bring back what they turned down, nor another idea with the same flaw, nor what they liked as made.

## What every card needs

- `hook`: the first line heard. Either a moment already under way that makes no sense yet, under 14 words, or a setup that puts the viewer in it, up to about 25: "If you..." or, as the nail opens, "This is a nail... Now make it a hundred times bigger." Nobody stops for a topic: they stop for a thing happening.
- `first_picture`: what's on screen in the first second, big in the middle: one thing a viewer would know.
- `ending`: the last thing seen and said, as the short ends. With the hook and the first picture, it's what the owner judges by.
- **It's straightforward, for anyone, even with the sound off**: one goal or one question all the way, each step something seen going wrong or working, one thing followed as it changes, few places and few new things. Nothing does its job unseen: when a machine does something, the viewer sees how.
- **It's true, and you can say how you know.** `source` names where it's known from, and `source_url` is a link someone could check, to the primary source when there is one: a paper, a court record, an official inquiry, a museum, a patent, a national agency. A claim found only on a list isn't checked: find what the list cites. If it's one small study, or a theory and not a finding, say so. The easiest mistake is a step that sounds right and isn't the source's: on the first batch a checker found that a rhino "breathes better since neither lung is underneath" was the writer's own idea, and the study said nobody's sure. If you've reasoned a step yourself, say so, or find it. A hook is a claim too.
- **The kit can show it.** `needs` lists what has to be made: people (invented, never a real person's likeness, except that a meme names a public figure on an invented face, by `memes.md`'s rules) and what they hold; real 3D models of animals, objects, machines and anatomy; shapes built from measurements; liquids, and air as moving balls; forces as glows and rings; x-rays and cutaways; labels, ticks and crosses on the picture. People hold a stance and react: they can't walk across a room, fight or act out a scene, so the story is told through what's placed, moved and struck around them. No crowds. Nothing graphic: no wounds, blood or bodies, and no harm to children or animals. (One category differs, by the owner's word of Nov 12, 2026: `survival.md` documents real deaths and shows the dead as still, simple figures. It still shows no wounds or blood.)
- `terms`: the 1 to 3 words that name what it's about. Code refuses a card if one of his titles has all of them, or if the owner's already answered an idea with the same terms. Make them the real subject ("straw", not "water"). When the only title of his with your subject makes a different promise (a story of someone trapped in a washing machine, beside how one came to be built), it isn't his idea: add the word that names your angle, and say in your reply which title it was, so the owner can judge.
- `like`: 1 to 3 of his shorts that make the same kind of promise, by their exact titles from `run.py ideas reference`. The check prints how each did against his median, as evidence for the owner, not a forecast. Choose them for being alike, not for doing well.
- `shape`: which outline shape it's built on (`prompts/character/outline.md`), from your brief's list.

## The file and the check

Write `{"category": "<category>", "cards": [...]}` to `data/ideas/<category>/cards.json`: every card with a `topic` (one line, under 100 characters), what every card needs above, and your brief's own fields. Run `uv run python run.py ideas check <category>` and fix what it says until it passes. Passing says a card can be used, not that it's good. Then a fresh fact-checker with web search tests the claims, since whoever wrote a card doesn't grade it, and the owner takes or turns down what's left. Their answers go in the taste file, word for word.
