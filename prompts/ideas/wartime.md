# Wartime

An idea here is one strange thing soldiers really did in a war, which looks wrong until the viewer sees why it worked: a trick, a trap, a disguise, a fix made in the field. The owner asked for this on Nov 12, 2026, of the reference's Reel "Why Soldiers Put Grenades In Glass Cups": "this is interesting too because it describes what happened or happens during war time, which might not exist anymore but is very interesting. Or something like traps used in Vietnam wars is pretty cool".

**So here, and only here, an idea needn't still exist today.** `all.md` turns down anything that's ended or being retired. In this category the history is the subject, by the owner's own word.

**Strategies first.** The same day, after hearing the first six ideas (a smokeless field kitchen, markers left on traps, a fake head raised for a sniper, fire along a foggy runway, a bridge dropped by parachute, a river dammed to free gunboats), the owner said: "I think people would be more interested in strategies used in war times like in WW2, there was chemical warfare right? there must be a lot of strategies in that area, or in the Japan China war, how Mao was hiding while the other party fought Japan and came to finish up the fight because the other side was wounded, or the booby traps used in Vietnam war, or how Japan used to fly planes into ships etc, idk". So the heart of this category is a strategy, in a war people have heard of: what one side was up against, the move it made, and why that worked or how it was beaten. A clever object, like the glass on the door, is one kind of idea here. The plan behind a campaign is the kind the owner asks for most.

**A person, and what their move teaches.** On Nov 14, 2026 the owner called the finished short on Mao and Chiang Kai-shek "actually pretty good", and for its caption wanted "how someone weak can become strong", then "Why sometimes strategy matters more than strength ?" and "in chinese theres a saying like before you are yeye, you are sun zi" (先当孙子，后当爷: first the grandson, then the grandfather). The agent's reading, not their words: the strategy ideas they like best have a named person on the weaker side, who makes a move that looks like giving in or running away, and wins by it; and each leaves the viewer one lesson they can use in their own life. So a strategy card:
- names the people its record names (`people`: who, on which side, and what each wanted), since a true story is about someone, never about forces;
- says what the move teaches, in one line said to the viewer (`lesson`), true to what happened and no firmer than the record;
- carries a saying that fits, in any language, where one exists, tested at its source like any other claim: who said it, where it's first written, and whether it's really about this.

Any century's war can be a card this way, if a viewer knows the name (Napoleon, Hannibal) or the story can be told so they don't need to. Ancient stories are often known only from a historian writing centuries later: say so in `how_known`, and tell them as "as ... tells it".

## The hook the owner showed, taken apart

The Reel runs 27 seconds, about 70 words, and loops. It had 13.3K likes when the owner recorded it. Never reuse its title, its wording or its subject: this is how it's built, not something to remake.

**What's said, with the times:**
- 0.0 s: "It's put into a glass cup before the pin is pulled," (done by 3.5 s)
- 4.0 s: "and the walls would hold the lever in place, preventing it from blowing up."
- 8.2 s: "Now some say this was invented by Soviet soldiers, who would place the glass on top of a slightly open door."
- 15.1 s: "Whenever enemy soldiers tried to go in, the glass would tip over, instantly shattering and releasing the lever."
- 21.7 s: "This would light the fuze and turn a simple grenade into a booby trap."
- It stops there and cuts to the first frame, so the last thing named, "a simple grenade", is what the first word, "It", points at.

**What's seen:**
- 0.0 s: a soldier already lowering a grenade into a drinking glass, in bright daylight. Nothing sets it up. The Reel's title asks the question, so the voice can start on the answer.
- 1.5 to 3.5 s: close on two hands: the grenade goes into the glass, the ring is pulled.
- 4 to 6.5 s: closer still, inside the glass: its wall glows green where it presses the lever against the grenade. The one part that matters is the only thing coloured.
- 7 to 8.5 s: the soldier wipes his brow. One second of relief, on the only face the short dwells on.
- 8.5 to 11 s: on "some say", a different room and a different army: one soldier hands the glass to another.
- 12 to 15 s: a hand stands the glass on top of a door left ajar.
- 16 to 18 s: through a window, men coming. The door moves and the glass tips.
- 19 to 23 s: from the floor: the glass falls and breaks on the tiles, the lever springs off, smoke rises.
- 24 to 26.5 s: the men run out with a blast behind them. Ours stops before this (see "The lines that always hold").

**How the hook is built:**
1. **Two things that don't belong together, already together:** a grenade and a drinking glass. The picture and the title ask "why would anyone?", so the first words are the answer's first step, not a setup.
2. **How it works comes first,** in two clauses and eight seconds, shown from inside with the one part that matters lit.
3. **Only then who did it and what for.** "Some say" is him hedging what isn't firmly known, in two words.
4. **Then the use, as a chain where each link is seen and causes the next:** the door, the tip, the shatter, the lever, the fuze.
5. **It ends by naming what the thing has become** ("a booby trap"), with no summing up, and loops.
6. **Every clause is a step or a cause:** "before", "and", "preventing", "whenever", "instantly", "and", "this would".

**Against the owner's four tests** (`all.md`): it works silent (a soldier lowering a grenade into a glass); it looks like a mistake (a live grenade in the one thing sure to break); lives are at stake; and the answer is watched (the wall holding the lever, the glass tipping, the lever flying off).

**What his other war shorts show** (`uv run python run.py ideas reference wartime`; a lean, never a forecast): 76 of his 4,002 shorts have a war word in the title. They sit at his median (1.02 times) and reach his top tenth a little more often than his shorts in general (10 of 76). The ones that did best put something in a war that doesn't belong there, or ask about something that looks like a mistake: sweets used in a war, a military dog's broken tooth, footprints disguised, a plane that made ice cream, bullets boiled. A trap set for soldiers did four times his median, while his plain "how this trap works" shorts (a snake pit, a deadfall, a bear trap) are among his worst. A trap alone isn't a hook: who it was set for, and why it fooled them, is. Also poor: a weapon's workings told straight (a smoke grenade), and a soldier's life story with nothing to watch.

## What makes one

- **A strategy, in a war people know.** The big ones first: the two world wars, the war in China, the Pacific, Vietnam. A viewer should know the war from one word, and leave knowing why one side did the thing they'd only half heard of.
- **It looks wrong** (`mistake`, the first of its three ways): a move a stranger would call stupid, dangerous or pointless (every army carrying a weapon it never uses, an army that keeps away from the big battles, a plane flown into a ship), in a picture of two things that don't belong together. The four tests hold here as everywhere.
- **Told level, and no firmer than the historians.** A strategy is often a reading of events, and readings are argued over. Where historians disagree, the card says who holds which view and what the record shows, in `how_known`, and the hook never states one side's reading as plain fact. Nobody's the hero or the villain, and no nation is mocked.
- **The problem they had** (`problem`): what they were up against, as a job the viewer can feel in a line (get across, stay hidden, know who's coming, keep a thing working in mud). The strange thing is their answer to it.
- **How it worked, in 3 to 6 steps, each seen** (`steps`): the mechanism first, then the use, each step causing the next.
- **Which war, which side, when** (`war`). Any war and any side, told level: the story is the cleverness, and nobody is mocked or made the villain.
- **How firmly it's known** (`how_known`): wartime stories are full of legends, and many famous ones are false. Say whether it's documented (a manual of the time, a museum's object, an archive photograph, a unit history) or only told (soldiers' accounts, "some say"), and who says so. A card built on a told story says so in its hook or its second line, as he does, and never states it as fact. If nothing firmer than a list or a forum has it, drop it.
- **What's left of it today** (`left_today`): a museum piece, a descendant still in use, a word that came from it, or nothing. It needn't exist. But a short that can end on something the viewer could go and see lands harder, so find it if it's there.
- **How the harm stays off the screen** (`shown_as`): see below.

## The lines that always hold

- **Nothing graphic.** A trap is seen working on a thing, never a person: a door, a boot print, a helmet on a stick, an empty doorway, then a flash or smoke. No bodies, wounds or blood, nobody thrown or struck. If it can only be told by showing someone hurt, it's out.
- **History, not a how-to.** What it was and why it worked, the way a museum shows it. Never what an explosive is made of, never sizes or materials for building anything that hurts, never a step a viewer could follow to make one.
- **The cleverness that isn't a weapon is the richest ground:** hiding, fooling, signalling, listening, finding the way, getting food and water, getting across. Look there before traps.
- **Invented stand-ins,** never a real person's likeness. A unit or a person can be named where the record names them.
- **A war still being fought is left alone.** The owner said "what happened or happens": things armies do today in training or in peace are fine.

The owner agreed to these lines on Nov 12, 2026, shown the first two and this last one as the agent's own: "yea i agree".

## What the kit can show

His short has men running and a blast. Ours can't and won't: people hold a stance and react, there are no crowds, and nothing graphic (`all.md`). So a wartime short here is told through hands and things:
- **a strategy on a map table**, the war room's own way of showing one: blocks for armies, an arrow that grows, a region that changes colour, a model ship or plane moved by a hand. It needs no crowd, and it keeps harm at the distance of a model;
- the object close up, cut open or as an x-ray, with the one part that matters lit (his green glass wall is our glow);
- one or two soldiers who stand, hold and look, in the kit of their army and year;
- a door, a path, a trench wall, a riverbank: one place, and what's placed and moved in it.

## Where to look

- Museums' collections online, where an object's page often has its story and a photograph: the Imperial War Museums, the National WWII Museum, the Australian War Memorial, the National Army Museum, the Smithsonian, and for Vietnam the museums and preserved tunnels there.
- Manuals and training films of the time, now in public archives (they say what troops were told to look out for), unit histories, and veterans' recorded accounts.
- Military historians' books on field improvisation, deception units, tunnels, signals and camouflage.
- **The owner's own leads.** Each is a lead to test at its source, not an idea yet, and what's noted beside it is the agent's starting point, to be checked like anything else:
  - *Poison gas.* On the battlefield it was mostly the First World War's weapon. In the Second, the armies stockpiled it and carried masks, and almost none was used on Europe's battlefields, while Japan did use it in China. The strategy may be why it was held back.
  - *The war in China, 1937 to 1945.* The owner's reading, that the Communists kept their strength while the Nationalist armies fought Japan's, and then won the civil war against a worn-out rival, is argued by many historians and disputed by others. It's a card only if it can be told with who says what.
  - *Traps in the Vietnam War.* The reference has made the spike pit and the cartridge trap, and a trap seen working on a person is outside the lines. What the traps were for, as a strategy, is the way in.
  - *Planes flown into ships.* Why Japan chose it, and how the fleet it was aimed at answered. A plane and a ship are things, but men died in both: told on models, at a distance.
