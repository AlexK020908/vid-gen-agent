# What ifs

An idea here is a hypothetical that puts the viewer in it ("If you grew four times bigger..."), shows the thing they'd wish for, and then shows what real physics and biology would do to them. The owner asked for this on Nov 12, 2026, of the reference's Reel "What If You Grew Four Times Bigger": "hypothetical situations like that is good too". The reference himself, in the owner's words: "he makes implausible situations too but explain with scientific sense and common sense".

## The hook the owner showed, taken apart

The Reel had 111K likes on Instagram and did 1.7 times his median on YouTube. It runs 37 seconds, about 105 words, and loops. Never reuse its title, its wording or its subject: this is how it's built, not something to remake.

**What's said, with the times:**
- 0.0 s: "If you grew four times bigger," (five words, done by 2.0 s)
- 2.2 s: "you might think you could leap over houses or shove cars out of your way,"
- 7.0 s: "but it would actually take much longer for your nerve signals to travel through your body, so you would react slower and move like you were in slow motion."
- 17.0 s: "And when you scale up like that, your body doesn't just get four times heavier, it gets over 60 times heavier, because your volume increases in every direction,"
- 28.0 s: "but your bones don't scale the same way, meaning taking a few steps could fracture your bones, and your spine could collapse under your own weight."
- It stops there, on the worst thing, and the picture cuts straight back to the first frame.

**What's seen:**
- 0.0 s: a man already growing in an ordinary street, with ordinary people at his feet being knocked aside. Nothing sets it up. By 1.2 s he is taller than the street lamp, and by 2 s the camera has risen over the roofs with him.
- 2.5 to 6 s: he leaps over a house and shoves a car aside. The wish is shown really happening, for four seconds, before anything goes wrong.
- 6 s: he picks up a stop sign. At 12.5 s it's stuck in his hand and his face reacts late: the slow nerves are a thing that happens to him, set up six seconds earlier.
- 7 to 12 s: his body as an x-ray, nerves glowing, the signal crawling along the arm.
- 17 to 23 s: a "4x" on a sign beside him becomes a huge green "64x".
- 26 s: a box round him labelled VOLUME, arrows growing three ways, a crowd at his feet for scale.
- 29 s on: his skeleton, bones cracking as he steps, the spine folding, and he's down in the street.

**How the hook is built:**
1. **"If you" plus one exact number.** "Four times bigger", not "a giant". The number comes back as the answer: 4 becomes 64.
2. **"You might think" shows the wish first.** The viewer gets the fun they stopped for, on screen, before the turn.
3. **"But actually" is the turn, at 7.0 s.** From here every line is a cost to the viewer's own body.
4. **Two costs, each from a real law, each seen, worst last:** slower (funny), then broken (grim). No third idea, no summing up, no "follow for more".
5. **Every clause is a cause:** "but", "so", "and when", "because", "but", "meaning". After the hook it is two long sentences.
6. **The title is the hook as a question.**

**Against the owner's four tests** (`all.md`): it works silent (a man outgrowing his street); the wish is the mistake (what you'd want is what would ruin you); what's at stake is the viewer's own body; and the answer is watched (the signal in the nerve, the late flinch, 4x turning to 64x, the bones).

**What his other what-ifs show** (`uv run python run.py ideas reference --words "what if"`; a lean, never a forecast): 58 shorts with "What If" in the title sit a little above his median (1.14 times) but reach his top tenth less often than his shorts in general (3 of 58). The ones that did best put the viewer's own body in an impossible place or change it (dug through the Earth, swallowed a live fish, muscles replaced with gears, born on the Moon). The ones that did worst have no body in them and nothing to watch ("could undo anything", "a bird loved a fish"). His "What To Do If You..." shorts, a danger the viewer is in and the right move, did better still (a punctured lung, lost in a cave, power lines on your car).

## What makes one

- **It happens to "you", and it's one change with one exact number** (`number`): four times bigger, a tenth of your weight, ten times the air pressure. Say the number and what real quantity it drives (height 4 times, so weight 64 times).
- **The wish comes first** (`wish`): what a viewer would hope to get from it, in a picture the kit can show, held for a few seconds.
- **Then real costs, 2 to 4 of them, worst last** (`costs`): each is one real law doing one thing to the viewer's body or to what they're holding, each seen happening, each with where it's known from. Each should surprise: if a viewer would guess it, it's out.
- **The wish is the mistake** (`mistake`): say what the viewer would want, and how it turns on them.
- **It leaves the viewer understanding something real** (`real_thing`): the real animal, machine or fact the law explains (why an elephant's legs are so thick, why an ant can fall from any height). This is how a what-if meets the owner's rule that an idea is about things that exist today. That reading is the agent's, not yet the owner's.
- **It's true as far as it goes, and no firmer.** A what-if rests on a real law applied to something that can't happen, so the card says which part is the law (with its source) and which part is our own sum. "Could fracture" is a claim: find what bone can really take before writing it. The fact-checker tests the law and the sum.

## What the first batch got wrong

The fact-checker found every sum on the first six cards arithmetically right and passed none of them. So, before a card is written:
- **Try the obvious way round the limit.** "You still couldn't shove a parked car" is true only of a level push: push upward, or lift one end, and the car's weight comes onto your shoes. A viewer says "just lift it" at once. Say the condition the limit holds under ("a flat shove"), and make the way round it the ending where it leads to the `real_thing`.
- **Check the input is the quantity the sum needs,** and say what it was measured on: the outside of a windpipe isn't the airway; young animals aren't adults; a rate measured in a cool room includes keeping warm.
- **A range in is a range out.** Where the inputs are a range, the cost and its badge say "roughly" and give the range, and one paper's coefficient is never paired with another's area.
- **"Every" and "never" need a source that says every and never.** One counter-example on the card's own page (a warm fish, an eyeless jellyfish) breaks the ending.
- **The cost's verb is no stronger than the sum:** "overheat in minutes", not "cook".
- **No real person, even unnamed, if a suit or a logo would make it them,** and no animal eaten on screen.
- **The kit's people hold a stance:** a what-if that needs a body running or swimming says so under `needs`, as the owner's call.

## What the kit can show

The reference fills a street with running people and breaks a body on screen. Ours can't and won't: no crowds, and nothing graphic (`all.md`). So a what-if here is told on one body and what's beside it for size:
- the body as an x-ray or a skeleton gone to glass, with what the law does shown as a glow, a crack of light, a slow-moving signal, never a wound;
- a number that grows on the picture, and a familiar thing beside the body for size (a door, a car, a house);
- a real hand doing something, filmed close (the touch screen short's look, phase 49).
If the costs can only be shown by hurting someone on screen, it's out.

## Where to look

- The laws that scale strangely: the square-cube law, surface against volume (heat, drying out, falling), pressure with depth and height, the speed of nerves and of sound, what blood and lungs can do. Biomechanics textbooks and papers on scaling in animals are full of worked cases.
- Questions people really ask: `run.py ideas show asked`, and the "what if" columns that answer with real numbers. A lead isn't an idea.
- Real animals at the extremes are the proof, and often the `real_thing`: the biggest and smallest that walk, fly, dive or fall.
- Read his what-ifs first and drop anything he's made (`run.py ideas reference --words "word,word*"`): he has dozens, and the check refuses a card whose terms match one.
