# Inventions

An idea here is a chain of fixes that ends on a real machine. The viewer builds it in their head, as "you", each fix a real part, and is told at the end what they've built, by its real name. The owner wants this wherever the science leads to something people really built: "we need to connect to an actual invention, and say 'congratulations you built this'... similar to the hammer on nail video".

## What the nail does that the turned-down ideas didn't

The owner turned down five inventions that followed every rule in the outline prompt: the straw (ending on the barometer, then a submersible pump, then a force pump), a safety elevator, a mine's steam engine, a hydraulic ram and a pendulum clock. Each was a chain of real fixes to a moving machine that solved its first problem. Set beside the nail, and beside the reference's house drain (a toilet flushed into a hole in the yard, fixed part by part into a sewer line, built the same way), here's what they lacked:

1. **It starts from the simplest version of something everyone has done with their own hands**: tap a nail into wood; flush a toilet. Not a moment from history ("in the 1850s...") or a job the viewer's never had (draining a flooded mine, telling the time at night, getting water uphill to a house).
2. **One twist makes it a problem you can see**: make it a hundred times bigger; there's nowhere for it to go. Then one goal, plain from the picture, all the way: get the giant nail into the ground.
3. **Each problem is one anyone would see coming, and it's seen going wrong**: the hammer's too heavy to swing; the whole street can smell it; one mistake with the explosives and people die. Funny, gross or dangerous beats clever.
4. **Each fix is a leap a viewer could almost have had**, and the bigger the leap the better: don't swing it, drop it; split it in two and set off explosives between; use a car engine. Not a part only an engineer would know. Each is added to the same machine, and named as it goes in.
5. **Four to six fixes, each fixing what the last one broke**, never a new subject. The nail never stops being the nail. In a `you_invent` every fix stays in the machine; in a `ladder`, a real attempt that failed can be a step, said as one and seen failing, if it's how people really got there.
6. **It ends on a machine the viewer has seen or heard and never understood**: the pile driver thumping on a building site; the pipes under their own house. The payoff is "so that's what that is". Not a machine nobody sees any more.
7. **It ends moving, in a cycle you can say in five words**: "fall, press, inject, ignite, rise". Never a still arrangement: of the force pump's weights stacked on a lid, "there is no motion?".
8. **It solves the problem the first second opens with** (the owner, in capitals, for all videos): the barometer didn't help anyone drink, and "how is the straw related to barometer tho".
9. **The force behind it can be seen**: a weight falling, a blow, a spring, a flame, a gear turning. The straw rested on air pressing, which nobody can see, so every step needed explaining: "how does the pump pump water? ... im confused".

## Where to look

Work backwards from the machine to the everyday act:
- **Machines people see and hear working but don't understand**: on building sites, roads, farms, docks, railways and in factories; in town; in their own house, car and pocket. `seen_it` says where.
- For each, **the everyday act it does at a giant scale, or does for you**, which is where the short starts: hammering for the pile driver, and likewise digging, lifting, cutting, throwing, pushing, sweeping, mixing, drilling, lighting a fire, locking a door.
- **How it was really arrived at**, step by step, from its history, its patents and its parts, so that every fix is a real part or the real reason it works. A step that's only plausible isn't a step.
- Sources: the machine's own article on Wikipedia and its history, with what they cite; museums of science, engineering and industry; makers' explanations of how their machines work; patents (Google Patents); engineering textbooks.
- **War and weapons** reach his top tenth a little more often than other subjects (about 17% against 10%, a weak sign that held in both halves of a test), so a machine of war is worth a look, with nothing graphic.

## The card

Besides what every card needs (`all.md`):
- `everyday`: the simple thing everyone has done or seen, that the first second shows.
- `goal`: the one goal, in a few words.
- `steps`: 4 to 7, each what goes wrong, seen, then the fix, with the real part named as it goes in: "Too heavy to swing, so you drop a block from a height: the ram."
- `invention`: the real machine's name, as the last line says it.
- `seen_it`: where the viewer has seen or heard it working today.
- `cycle`: how it moves, in about five words.
- `inside`: how what's hidden is shown: a cut through it, an x-ray, a part made glass.
- `source` and `source_url`: where its history and working are known from.
