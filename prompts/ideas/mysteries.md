# Mysteries

An idea here is a mysterious event re-staged in 3D: the strange thing seen first, then the clues seen one at a time, then the answer shown happening, the way it really did. The owner asked for this ("we can also simulate ... mysterious events"), and chose old real mysteries and invented ones. The re-staging is the promise: nobody was there to see it, and the viewer gets to watch it happen.

## What makes one

1. **The strange thing is seen in the first second, and it's physical**: something found where it can't be, something moved with nobody there, a room locked from the inside. Not a story about a feeling or a rumour. Open on the thing, not the place: a far-off place is only somewhere, and the thing is what stops a thumb.
2. **Every clue is something seen**: a mark, a time on a clock, a thing out of place, what the weather did that night. Not a witness's say-so, since nobody can see what a witness thought.
3. **The obvious guesses are ruled out on the picture, one by one,** and then the answer is shown happening: this is where a 3D short beats a talking head.
4. **The answer rests on how the world works**: ice, wind, tides, sound, light, rot, a mistake anyone could make. That's what the kit shows best, and it's what most of his "explained" shorts do.
5. **One place, and few people**: the kit can't show crowds, and people hold a stance rather than act, so the story is told through what's placed, moved and struck around them.
6. **An old real mystery is historical**: fifty years ago or more, documented in the record (newspapers of the time, official inquiries, papers that studied it). If it's unsolved, the answer is the best-supported explanation, said exactly as firmly as its sources, and `solved` says so.
7. **The cause is nature, an accident or a mistake.** A case with someone to blame is a crime, and its brief is `crimes.md`.
8. **An invented case plays fair**: every clue the answer needs is seen before the answer, and the answer rests on something true (ice melts, a candle burns at a steady rate), named in `source`. His invented stories do a little better than his median (`(fiction)` in his titles, 1.5x in the middle over 27 shorts, with far more comments), a lean worth knowing.

## Where to look

- Wikipedia's lists of unexplained events, ghost ships, disappearances and explained phenomena, with what they cite; official inquiries; the papers or investigations that finally explained one.
- For invented cases: start from a true thing a clue can rest on, then build the case round it. Read the reference's `(fiction)` and `(Murder Mystery)` shorts first (`run.py ideas reference mysteries`), and never reuse one.

## Real people

- People are invented 3D stand-ins, never a real person's likeness. A real person can be named where the record names them.
- Never name or hint that anyone did wrong unless a court found them guilty.
- Nothing graphic. A death is told, not shown: no bodies, wounds or blood. No case where a child was harmed or threatened. The big physical moment the star references have (`all.md`) comes from the strange thing or the answer, never from harm. Leaving out who was hurt is fine; saying nobody was hurt when people were isn't.
- No method someone could copy to hurt anyone.

## The card

Besides what every card needs (`all.md`):
- `kind`: `real` or `invented`.
- `strange`: what's strange, as the first second shows it.
- `clues`: 3 to 6, in order, each what's seen and what it rules out or points to.
- `answer`: what happened, as it's shown happening.
- `solved`: "solved", with by whom and when; "best theory", with what's still argued; or "invented".
- `people`: who's in it, as roles ("the ship's cook"), and who's named.
- `shown_as`: how anything hurtful is shown without being graphic, or "nothing hurtful".
