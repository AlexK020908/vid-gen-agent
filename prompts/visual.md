Write each visual prompt as one short paragraph, 20 to 40 words, describing a single frame:

- Who is in it and what they're doing, at the moment the narration describes. The main character is always "the stick figure", and cast members go by their cast names ("Mrs. Okafor"). The pipeline gives the artist a reference picture of each of them, so don't describe their bodies, faces or clothes; what they carry, put on or have wrong with them (a backpack, body armor, a bandaged hand) is fine, and it must agree with the scene's looks. When the main character isn't in the frame, don't mention them: the picture then leaves them out. Anyone else (the infected, a crowd, a passer-by) gets a short description: how many, how close, what they're doing.
- Where: the scene's place is drawn from its own reference picture, so don't describe its layout again. Say which part of it the camera sees and from where (at the window, by the barricaded front door, looking down the hallway), the time of day and the weather. When the character arrives somewhere, show enough of the place that the viewer knows where they are.
- Who is where: in plain picture terms, who is in front and who behind, left or right, and which side of every door, fence, window or barricade each person is on ("Dale and his men outside the fence on the sidewalk in front; the stick figure behind the locked gate on the porch steps"). Never just "across the gate", "your side" or "inside": the artist can't tell which side that is.
- How things work: when the line has something built, blocked or used (a steel plate bolted over the stairs, a rope down the wall), show it doing its job, and say where it's fixed and to what. A thing that blocks a way is drawn where the way was, so the way is gone from the picture: a plate sealing the stairs means no steps are visible, only steel where they were, bolted into the walls on both sides ("from the landing: the top of the staircase closed off by a steel plate bolted upright between the walls where the stairs went down, fists hammering its far side"). A plate lying on the floor beside the stairs, or standing free with the stairs still open beside it, blocks nothing, and the viewer sees that at once. When the place's own viewpoint can't show the thing doing its job (a plate across a staircase, seen from the landing it protects), go close instead: a close-up of the bolt going through the plate's edge into the wall, with fingers curling over the top edge, says "sealed" better than any wide shot that shows the stairs beside it. Everything in the frame has to be physically possible and agree with the narration.
- Damage by what still holds: a barrier that's been hit but still keeps people out is described by what it still does, not by its damage ("the iron gate still shut and latched, its bars only bowed a little inward where the SUV's nose presses on them from inside"). The artist draws "the bent gate" or "the smashed door" as wrecked open, with a gap a person could step through, and the picture then says the barrier failed when the story says it held. Damage that opens a way is said only when the way is open in the story.
- Framing: the scene's `shot`, said first ("Wide shot: ...", "Close-up: ..."), and the prompt shows what that framing can: the whole place and where everyone is in a wide shot, the character and what they're doing in a medium shot, one face, hand or object in a close-up.
- The mood in a word or two (tense, calm, bleak, hopeful), and the light in every prompt: the time of day and where the light comes from (midnight, one flashlight beam; grey dawn through the window; lamplight). The place's reference picture is drawn by day, so a prompt that doesn't say the light gets daylight, even at midnight.
- One moment follows the last: when a scene continues the one before it in the same place, the artist is also given that scene's picture, so say what has changed since it (who moved where, what's now in whose hands) rather than describing the whole frame again.

The pipeline adds the channel's art style ({style_prompt}), so don't describe line work, colors or style. No words, signs, numbers or labels in the picture. When the line has a threat (zombies, animals, a storm, fire), put it in the frame where a viewer spots it at a glance: how many, how close, what they're doing. Never gory. One frame, one moment: no sequences, split panels or speech bubbles. For a clip scene, also say in a few words what moves.
