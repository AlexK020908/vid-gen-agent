# Outline

You plan one vertical 3D short for {channel_name} (premise: {scenario_family}). It runs 30 to 60 seconds and makes one surprising true point, shown happening rather than described. A person or an animal is in the picture from the first second, and something happens to them.

- `shape`: how this short is built. Choose the one the topic suits best, and build the beats on it. People can hold a stance and react, but they can't walk or act, so choose one the picture can carry:
  - `true_story`: something that really happened. Start at its strangest moment, before the viewer knows why, and let the reason come after. It rests on what's placed and struck around people who hold still.
  - `absurd_test`: a silly or impossible setup, answered with real physics or real numbers, and then what would go wrong next.
  - `why_it_happens`: something familiar, and the reason behind it that nobody would guess.
  - `thought_experiment`: do something simple, in your head or with your hands, and it proves the idea or leads to the real thing that works this way.
  - `fake_invention`: a made-up product shown working, built on a real principle, and then it goes comically wrong. The short says plainly that it's made up, and the `fact` is the real principle.
  - `fix_in_steps`: a problem that gets worse, then the real fix step by step, up to its clever last step.
  - `ladder`: a problem, the obvious fix and why it fails, a better fix and why that fails, up to the real thing used today, named at the end.
  - `you_invent`: the viewer builds a real invention in their head. It starts from something everyday, or a simple thing "you" want to do, and each step is a problem the last fix caused and the fix "you" think of next. Every fix is a real part of the real thing, or the real reason it works, and gets its real name as it goes in. It ends by telling the viewer what they've just built, by its real name. **What they build is a machine that moves**, working in a cycle the viewer can follow and something people really use, never a still arrangement (the owner, of a lid with weights stacked on it: "force pump just stack weights on water and thats it? there is no motion?"). **What they build has to solve the problem the short opens with**: the pile hammer drives the pile the short starts out trying to drive. Learning something on the way and building a different thing with it (a straw too tall to drink through, ending on a barometer) leaves the opening problem unsolved, so the viewer asks what the start had to do with the end. The `fact` says what that real thing is and where it's known from. Choose it whenever the science leads to something people really built: the owner wants a short to land on a real invention where one exists.
  - `mystery`: something strange that really happened, or an invented case. The strange thing is seen first; then the clues, each something seen that rules a guess out or points the way; then the answer, shown happening the way it did. Unsolved, it's the best-supported explanation, said as firmly as its sources and no more. An invented case shows every clue its answer needs before the answer, and the answer rests on something true. Nobody's named or hinted at as having done wrong unless a court found them guilty, and a death is told, never shown.
- `hook`: the first thing the viewer hears, and it doesn't give the answer away. Either a moment already happening that makes no sense yet, under 14 words ("He glued a coin to the pavement and sat down to watch."), or a setup that puts the viewer in it, up to about 25 words: "If you..." ("If you filled a balloon with water and held it over a candle, it wouldn't pop."), or, as the pile hammer opens, something everyday and then the twist ("This is a nail... Now make it a hundred times bigger."). A question on its own only opens a `why_it_happens` short, when no moment or setup would do.
- `fact`: the true claim the short rests on, in one or two sentences, and where it's known from: a study with its journal and year, a textbook, a national agency. If you can't name a source you'd stand behind, it isn't a fact, so choose another angle or say plainly what's uncertain. No myths and no made-up numbers.
- `beats`: 3 to 6 steps, each one thing happening that moves the explanation on. What's strange comes first and why comes after it, so hold the reason back as long as the viewer can stand. Build up to the answer: what you'd expect, why that can't be it, and then what's really going on. When it's time to explain, go inside: the eyeball cut open with light flooding through it, the jaw seen as an x-ray, the air in a pipe as moving balls. A viewer should be able to tell a friend how it works afterwards, from what they saw. Give each number something to be seen on: a scale, a gauge, a thing beside it for size. A person is only in a beat to do something or react: a hand doing the job, a face for a moment, and no one standing by to watch. A `title` of a few words, and a `summary` saying what's seen and what it shows. Think of what can be seen: a person, what they hold, a real animal or object, a force shown as a glow or shock rings, one slow-motion moment that the whole short leads to.
- **Straightforward, for anyone, even with the sound off.** The channel explains things to people who've never studied any of it, and many watch on mute. So the short chases one thing all the way: one goal "you" have (get the pile into the ground) or one question (why doesn't the bird get hurt), and every beat is a step toward it. Each step is something seen going wrong (it won't go in, the water stops at ten metres, the tube won't fit in the house) or something seen working, never an idea the viewer has to take on trust: if a step can only be said and not seen happening ("the air weighs as much as ten metres of water", shown as two columns on a balance), find one that can be seen, or leave it out. **Nothing does its job unseen**: when a step leans on a machine (a pump, a motor), the viewer sees how it does it, so prefer a thing whose working is plain (a lid pressed down, a plunger, a lever) to a box that hides it, or show where what it moves goes (the air going into the pump and out of its exhaust). The owner, of a pump that "empties the straw of air": "how does the pump empty the straw of air? im confused". Stay with the one thing the short's about as it changes: the nail that becomes the pile, the straw that becomes the barometer, the same bird. Few places and few new things, since each new one is something more to work out: bring in a new object or place only where the story can't go on without it.
- `turn`: what comes after the answer, in a sentence: one more true consequence, usually stranger or worse, that the last beat shows. Don't stop at the answer just because it's been given. If the short really is stronger ending there, write "none:" and why.
- `ending`: the last thing said, which lands the point or the turn. Stop as soon as it has landed: no summing up, and no "follow for more". In a `you_invent` short it congratulates the viewer on what they've built, by its real name.

Force and pain are shown with glows and rings, never as wounds or blood. Everyone in it is invented. Never use another creator's characters, wording or look, and never a real person.
