# Outline

You plan one vertical 3D short for {channel_name} (premise: {scenario_family}). It runs 30 to 40 seconds, never over 45, and makes one surprising true point, shown happening rather than described. On a channel whose premise is memes it makes one joke instead, as a `parody` (below), built from what the owner asked for. A person or an animal is in the picture from the first second, and something happens to them.

- `shape`: how this short is built. Choose the one the topic suits best, and build the beats on it. People can hold a stance and react, but they can't walk or act, so choose one the picture can carry:
  - `true_story`: something that really happened. Start at its strangest moment, before the viewer knows why, and let the reason come after. It rests on what's placed and struck around people who hold still. **It's about someone, never about forces.** The owner, Nov 13, 2026, of a wartime script told as "the Communists" and "China's government" on a map: "this seems really abstract no?", and then told it back themselves: Mao nearly beaten, the other party fighting Japan "while he healed", and then "mao killed the wounded tiger". So before the beats, write the story as you'd tell it to a friend, in three plain sentences: who's in trouble, the turn, how it ends. Build the beats on those. The people the record names are named from the hook on, each beat is something one of them does or loses, and one image the story earns (the wounded tiger) is worth more than three careful clauses. It stays no firmer than true: say what each of them did, and where the why is argued, the picture says so.
  - `absurd_test`: a silly or impossible setup, answered with real physics or real numbers, and then what would go wrong next.
  - `why_it_happens`: something familiar, and the reason behind it that nobody would guess. It opens on the viewer's own question, asked straight to them as they'd ask it themselves ("Have you ever wondered why you can't put a fork in the microwave?"), over the picture of it happening. Then a few plain words on how the familiar thing works (a microwave fills with waves that shake the water in food, and that heats it), and the reason is built on those, so it has something to stand on. The owner, Nov 15, 2026: "give a few words on how microwaves work, and then connect to why fork cant be put into microwave".
  - `thought_experiment`: do something simple, in your head or with your hands, and it proves the idea or leads to the real thing that works this way.
  - `fake_invention`: a made-up product shown working, built on a real principle, and then it goes comically wrong. The short says plainly that it's made up, and the `fact` is the real principle.
  - `fix_in_steps`: a problem that gets worse, then the real fix step by step, up to its clever last step.
  - `ladder`: a problem, the obvious fix and why it fails, a better fix and why that fails, up to the real thing used today, named at the end.
  - `you_invent`: the viewer builds a real invention in their head. It starts from something everyday, or a simple thing "you" want to do, and each step is a problem the last fix caused and the fix "you" think of next. Every fix is a real part of the real thing, or the real reason it works, and gets its real name as it goes in. It ends by telling the viewer what they've just built, by its real name. **Nothing it can't work without is left out**: if the real thing fails without a part (a touch screen's rows and columns never touch, and charge crosses a thin gap between them), that part is one of the steps; parts that only add detail can stay out. **What they build is a machine that moves**, working in a cycle the viewer can follow and something people really use, never a still arrangement (the owner, of a lid with weights stacked on it: "force pump just stack weights on water and thats it? there is no motion?"). **What they build has to solve the problem the short opens with**: the pile hammer drives the pile the short starts out trying to drive. Learning something on the way and building a different thing with it (a straw too tall to drink through, ending on a barometer) leaves the opening problem unsolved, so the viewer asks what the start had to do with the end. The `fact` says what that real thing is and where it's known from. Choose it whenever the science leads to something people really built: the owner wants a short to land on a real invention where one exists.
  - `parody`: only on a channel whose premise is memes. Someone the viewer knows, dropped into a famous story or an impossible what-if and told deadpan, the way a true story is told. Every step is something they're really known for, seen doing a job in the story, and it ends on a twist line that turns the goal around. It's plainly made up, and the `fact` is what they're known for and where that's known from (`prompts/ideas/memes.md` has the rules about real people).
  - `mystery`: something strange that really happened, or an invented case. The strange thing is seen first; then the clues, each something seen that rules a guess out or points the way; then the answer, shown happening the way it did. Unsolved, it's the best-supported explanation, said as firmly as its sources and no more. An invented case shows every clue its answer needs before the answer, and the answer rests on something true. Nobody's named or hinted at as having done wrong unless a court found them guilty, and a death is told, never shown.
- `hook`: the first thing the viewer hears, and it doesn't give the answer away. Either a moment already happening that makes no sense yet, under 14 words ("He glued a coin to the pavement and sat down to watch."), or a setup that puts the viewer in it, up to about 25 words: "If you..." ("If you filled a balloon with water and held it over a candle, it wouldn't pop."), or, as the pile hammer opens, something everyday and then the twist ("This is a nail... Now make it a hundred times bigger."). A `why_it_happens` short opens on the viewer's own question instead (the owner, Nov 15, 2026, of a fork short that opened on a statement: "it doesnt start with a question, 'have you ever wondered why you can't put a fork into a microwave?'"), and its picture does the stopping: the strange thing happening, big. No other shape opens on a question. **It passes the four tests** (`prompts/ideas/all.md`, the owner's own): its first second stops a viewer with the sound off; it looks like a mistake, someone doing what you'd expect to ruin everything; a living thing or a person stands to lose something a viewer can feel, and the hook's words or its picture say what; and the answer the short builds to is something watched happening, never something only said. The idea's card says how it passes each (`silent`, `mistake`, `stake`, `seen`): build the hook and the first beat on those, and keep what's at stake in sight until it's saved or lost at the end. **Then sharpen its words** (the ladder in `prompts/ideas/all.md`): give it a number or a limit, squeeze a lot into a little, set it against what most people expect, and best of all leave an outcome open under a hard limit, one the short answers near its end and never by its middle. Keep only what stays true.
- `fact`: the true claim the short rests on, in one or two sentences, and where it's known from: a study with its journal and year, a textbook, a national agency. If you can't name a source you'd stand behind, it isn't a fact, so choose another angle or say plainly what's uncertain. No myths and no made-up numbers.
- `beats`: 3 to 6 steps, each one thing happening that moves the explanation on. What's strange comes first and why comes after it, so hold the reason back as long as the viewer can stand. Build up to the answer: what you'd expect, why that can't be it, and then what's really going on. When it's time to explain, go inside: the eyeball cut open with light flooding through it, the jaw seen as an x-ray, the air in a pipe as moving balls. A viewer should be able to tell a friend how it works afterwards, from what they saw. Give each number something to be seen on: a scale, a gauge, a thing beside it for size. A person is only in a beat to do something or react: a hand doing the job, a face for a moment, and no one standing by to watch. A `title` of a few words, and a `summary` saying what's seen and what it shows. Think of what can be seen: a person, what they hold, a real animal or object, a force shown as a glow or shock rings, one slow-motion moment that the whole short leads to.
- **Straightforward, for anyone, even with the sound off.** The channel explains things to people who've never studied any of it, and many watch on mute. So the short chases one thing all the way: one goal "you" have (get the pile into the ground) or one question (why doesn't the bird get hurt), and every beat is a step toward it. Each step is something seen going wrong (it won't go in, the water stops at ten metres, the tube won't fit in the house) or something seen working, never an idea the viewer has to take on trust: if a step can only be said and not seen happening ("the air weighs as much as ten metres of water", shown as two columns on a balance), find one that can be seen, or leave it out. **Nothing does its job unseen**: when a step leans on a machine (a pump, a motor), the viewer sees how it does it, so prefer a thing whose working is plain (a lid pressed down, a plunger, a lever) to a box that hides it, or show where what it moves goes (the air going into the pump and out of its exhaust). The owner, of a pump that "empties the straw of air": "how does the pump empty the straw of air? im confused". Stay with the one thing the short's about as it changes: the nail that becomes the pile, the straw that becomes the barometer, the same bird. Few places and few new things, since each new one is something more to work out: bring in a new object or place only where the story can't go on without it. **One thing, all the way.** The short is about the one thing its question or goal names, and nothing else does that job beside it: a second example of the same trouble (a twist tie that burns too, beside the sparking fork) or of the way out (a rack in another oven that doesn't spark) is a second short, even when the idea's card has it. The owner, Nov 15, 2026: "we only talk about a fork too, dont talk about the thin metal tie", and of the cut that had both: "its feels like its all over the place".
- **Short, and never a stretch with nothing to wait for.** The reference's shorts run about 31 seconds, and nine in ten of his are under 43. The owner's first two Reels ran 60 and 85 seconds, and most viewers had left by 30 (Nov 13, 2026: "make sure we have good hook throughout the video and dont make it too long"). So the short carries one goal and **at most three rounds of problem and fix**, or three clues: choose the three the answer can't do without, and leave the rest out rather than hurrying all of them. Every beat ends with something still unsettled that the next beat settles, so say in each `summary` what the viewer is left waiting to see. The best picture the short has, after the hook's, comes in the first ten seconds, never saved for the end. And the answer isn't only in the last five seconds: by the middle the viewer has seen it start to work.
- `turn`: what comes after the answer, in a sentence: one more true consequence, usually stranger or worse, that the last beat shows. It happens to the thing the short is about (the fork's sparks can start a fire), never to a new one brought in for it. Don't stop at the answer just because it's been given. If the short really is stronger ending there, write "none:" and why.
- `ending`: the last thing said, which lands the point or the turn. Stop as soon as it has landed: no summing up, and no "follow for more". Where it can be done honestly, the last line or its picture hands back to the first (the nail again, now driven; the glass again, now answering the finger), so the short can be watched round again. In a `you_invent` short it congratulates the viewer on what they've built, by its real name. Just before that, the thing they built can prove itself against the real one, doing its job (the owner, Nov 17, 2026, of a fighter jet made of a man pooping: "in the end scene, have him take out a real jet, and fly away"). **A true story ends on something the viewer can use** (the owner, Nov 13, 2026, of a wartime short: "connect it to irl lessons, so in moment of ... think of this ... basically a take away. ofc keeping all the hooks"): one plain lesson that the story itself showed, said to the viewer as "So when you're..., think of this: ...", in the story's own words. It's one line, never a moral the story didn't show and never a second ending. If the story is still argued over, the lesson comes from what both sides agree happened, and the last picture says that it's argued.

Force and pain are shown with glows and rings, never as wounds or blood. Everyone in it is invented. Never use another creator's characters, wording or look, and never a real person. A true story names the real people its record names, in the words and on tags, and never draws them.
