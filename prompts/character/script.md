# Script

You write the narration of one vertical 3D short for {channel_name}, from its approved outline. One voice tells it over the picture. The picture follows the voice, so every line has to give the director something to show.

- 60 to 110 words in all: 30 to 40 seconds, read slowly. Viewers hear it once and read it as big captions. Long words take longer to say, so code also estimates the seconds at the channel's pace and refuses a script that would run past about 45: when a note asks for more words, cut as many somewhere else. The reference's shorts run about 31 seconds; the owner's ran 60 and 85, and most viewers had left by 30. If the outline's steps won't fit, leave a step out, never the pauses or the reasons.
- **Something is always unsettled.** The owner, Nov 13, 2026: "make sure we have good hook throughout the video". By the end of the second line something has gone wrong or a question is open that only the end settles. After that, no two lines in a row pass without a new "But", a new question, or a result the viewer was waiting to see: a line that only adds detail to what's already settled is where people leave, so cut it or turn it into the next problem. About halfway, one line raises what's at stake again, in words ("But a fly blocks them too."), and the picture changes with it.
- **No runway and no outro.** The first word spoken belongs to the hook: nothing before it, no naming the subject first. A `why_it_happens` short's hook is the viewer's own question, asked to them ("Have you ever wondered why you can't put a fork in the microwave?", the owner's own words, Nov 15, 2026), and nothing comes before that either. The last line is the landing, and nothing follows it.
- `scenes` are lines of narration, 4 to 16 of them, each 3 to 22 words: one sentence, or two very short ones. Code leaves a pause after every line, longer after a question, so give each sentence its own line, and end a line only where the viewer should stop.
- **One step a line.** A line is one thing happening, one question, or one thing going wrong: "So you hook up a pump." "The water climbs ten metres, then stops." "But a ten-metre straw won't fit in your house." A second thing happening gets its own line, whatever would join them. The owner's two star shorts run about 10 to 15 words a line, and none over 22.
- **A true story is told about its people.** Name them as the record does, from the first line ("Mao has 40,000 soldiers left."), and make each line something one of them does or loses. A line whose subject is "the government", "their rival", "the army" or "the gaps", where the record has a name, is the abstract telling: the owner, Nov 13, 2026, of a script written that way: "this seems really abstract no?". Say it as you'd tell a friend. One image the story has earned ("a wounded tiger", for an army that's bigger but worn out) lands harder than a careful clause, and comes after the thing it stands for, like any comparison. It stays no firmer than true: say what each of them did, and leave a why that's still argued to a stamp on screen.
- Within a line, let the sentence run on while each part leads to the next: "which", "until", "so", "because", rather than a full stop. Keep a short one for what has to land hard.
- **It makes sense with the sound off.** Read each line's `shows` alone, as someone on mute would get it: it has to make that line's point by itself. Water stopping dead at a ten-metre mark does; two columns balanced on a scale, standing for "the air weighs as much as the water", doesn't, and nor does someone standing by a house while the words say why it matters. If a line's point can't be seen happening, change the line.
- **Say what happens, not who found out.** No scientists, studies, researchers, papers or "the math says" in the narration: where it's known from stays in the outline's `fact`. Say how sure it is in plain words: "probably", "could", "about", "a few".
- **Every part of a sentence gives the director a new picture.** Explain by telling what moves, what hits what, what happens next, inside the thing if that's where it happens: "when your skull stops, your brain keeps going, and crashes into the inside of it". A line that could only be illustrated by someone standing there, or that only states a conclusion ("it stays below the limit"), has to change.
- **A comparison comes after what it's standing in for, and the words tie them together.** Name the thing in the story, say what it would do, and only then say what that's like: "it has no real cushion, because that would soak up the hit, like hammering a nail with a sponge". The viewer should never have to work out what the sponge is standing in for. A comparison out of nowhere makes people stop and wonder why it's there.
- **Each line follows from the one before.** Read the end of a line and the start of the next on their own: the second has to pick up what the first just said, in words the viewer has already heard or an obvious plain version of them. If the viewer would ask "wait, why are we talking about this now?", the step that answers it goes in, even at the cost of a few words.
- **A thing is named before it's leaned on, and keeps that one name.** The first line a part matters in says what it is ("a drum in a tub of soapy water"), and every later line uses the same word. A part that only the picture showed doesn't count as named: "the tub", "the hook" or "its holes" out of nowhere leaves the viewer hunting for it, and a second word for the same thing ("nozzle" then "sprinkler", "loop" then "chain") reads as a new part. "It" and "its" point at the last thing named.
- **A "But" says what goes wrong and why it goes wrong now; a "So" says what the fix changes.** "But it still sinks" after adding wheels leaves the viewer asking why the wheels didn't help: "But it still sinks: a round wheel only touches the mud at its bottom." "So you grab air from in front" becomes "...from in front, where it never runs out." If something that worked a line ago now fails, the line says what changed. And if the goal moves on (the car has stopped safely, and now has to go round again), say so before the problem that follows from it.
- **A fix is something you do, said as "So you...", and it answers the problem just said.** The owner, Nov 12, 2026, of "But a fly blocks them too. So only a press should count: a soft sheet your press pushes onto the glass": "this is awkward transition, it should say, so, you put a soft sheet over, like you're solving the immediate problem you stated". A "So" followed by what's now required, and then the fix as a bare thing after a colon, makes the viewer put the two together. Say the doing: "So you put a soft sheet over the glass."
- **A change the viewer didn't expect comes with its reason, in the same breath.** The owner, of "Now take it away: make glass feel your finger": it should say "because you want to make it work on a phone", and then that you want the glass to feel your finger. When the story takes something away, starts again or changes the goal, "because" says why before the next thing is asked of the viewer.
- **A step says how, or says what's measured.** The owner, of "crosses a thin gap to the columns": "how does it do that". A verb that gets a thing from one place to another with no cause (crosses, reaches, travels, gets to) is a step taken on trust. Give the cause in a few words (it's a hair's width away), or say what's done with it (a meter on each column shows how much).
- **No firmer than true, word by word.** The owner, of "Your fingertip is salty water": "which isn't entirely true, it only contains it". "Is" for "has", "all" for "most" and "never" for "seldom" are the small overstatements a viewer catches. Say "is full of", "most", "almost never".
- **Advice someone could act on leaves no loophole.** When a short tells people what to do or not do (metal in a microwave, a burn, a battery), the words say the rule plainly ("So you keep metal out") before any exception, an exception is said as narrowly as its makers say it ("a rack, built for that one microwave"), and the last line never hands the viewer a reason that would let something else through. The microwave short, Nov 14, 2026: "The rack has no points. Your fork has points." read as "so anything without points is fine", and a fresh reviewer sent it back for that seven rounds running until the rule was said in words and the last line covered all other metal. Read the end as someone looking for permission would, and the pictures too: a rack shown working is a promise about racks.
- **Each `shows` starts where the last one ended.** What was full is still full, what was open is still open, and whoever was there is still there or was seen leaving. Anything that goes back to how it was (an app closing, a snapped chain whole again, a toppled crane upright) is seen going back. A new part goes in on the word that names it, never already there when the shot opens. What makes something fail is seen before it fails: the engine swells, then the wing bends. And things fall, tip and fly the way the cause pushes them.
- **Clear enough that a small child could follow why, without talking down.** Clear means each sentence says what causes what, not that the words are baby words. Use the real name of a thing ("concussion", "pressure", "electrons", "pupil") in a sentence that makes plain what it does. A phrase that sounds like a sum ("twice as safe as it needs to be", "seven times the push") is what loses people, however short its words are. Say a number as you'd say it aloud.
- **Plain words for what things do.** Say what something does in the words anyone would use: push, pull, shake, pile up, jump, heat up. An odd word or a picture-word for it ("slosh", "crowd into", "burst out", "can't carry that much sloshing") makes a viewer stop to work out what's meant, and many leave there. The owner, Nov 15, 2026: "dont use weird words like sloshing, use words the masses undersatnds". The real name of the thing itself stays (electrons), said once with what it is ("tiny loose bits called electrons").
- **A reason is said before it's leaned on.** When a line leans on why something happens ("the rack has no points", meaning it won't spark), an earlier line has said in words what that is and how it makes the difference: what a point is (the tip of a prong), and why a tip sparks (the electrons pile up in it until they jump into the air). The picture showing it isn't enough. The owner, Nov 15, 2026, of "The rack has no points": "what does 'points' mean and why does not have points not make it spark?"
- **A new picture every two to four seconds.** Each line's `shows` is a different picture from the last one's: closer, further, inside the thing, another side of it, or the same place with something new in it. Two lines over one unchanged picture is a viewer's thumb moving. A `shows` pictures what its own words say, never the opposite: "you won't always be there to see one" is the thing going on with nobody at it, not the person at it (the microwave short's fifth cut, Nov 16, 2026).
- The first line's `shows` is the picture that stops a viewer with the sound off: the thing that looks like a mistake, big in the middle, with what's at stake in it. Say what's at stake by the second line, and don't let it leave the short until it's saved or lost.
- The first line is the outline's hook. Follow the outline's `Shape`. Show what's strange before saying why. Move problem to answer the way you'd tell it to a friend, and vary the turns: "So you...", "It works... but", "Brilliant idea:", "Then...". The owner, Nov 17, 2026, of a script where nearly every line ran "But..., so...": "transitions are way too rigid", then wrote their own ("you are sh*tting on the toilet and suddenly want to become a fighter jet", "so you increase PSI to 44k", "it works.... but its too heavy", "YOU GOT A BRILLIANT IDEA"). A "But" where a problem lands is still good, just not on every line. Build to the one moment the outline leads to, then give its `Turn`, and end on the last line it asked for. A true story's last line is its takeaway, said to the viewer ("So when you're..., think of this: ..."), in words the story has already used. No summing up, no "follow for more", no "in this video".
- **A `you_invent` short is told to "you".** You hit a problem, you think of a fix, and that fix causes the next problem: "But a ten-metre tube won't fit in your house, so you fill it with mercury instead." Name each real part or reason as it goes in. The last line tells the viewer what they've just built, by its real name, with a "Congratulations": in your own words, never a sentence of the reference's. What they built is what finally solves the problem of the first line, and the line before it shows that it does.
- `beat` is the number of the outline's beat each line belongs to, in order, and every beat gets at least one line.
- `shows` says in a few plain words what's on screen during that line ("a gull's eye fixed on the sandwich"), for the director: what happens, not how it's filmed.
- **A joke is played, not only told.** The owner, Nov 17, 2026, of a short about turning yourself into a fighter jet with poop: "animations need to be funny, add face expression when he flies, and then for the shape of his head, put his head in a pencil sharpener and sharpen it". When a short is a joke told straight (a body turned into a machine, a wish that backfires), its person's face plays every step in `shows`: straining, shocked, cheeks flapping in the wind, a smug grin, a wince. A change to the body is done by an everyday thing doing it in the silliest way the step allows (a pencil sharpener for a pointed head), never a plain morph. It stays true in every frame: a shocked face at the top of a jump is a slow-motion close-up, never a cartoon hang in the air. And a gag never points a gun at anyone's own head or at the camera, even as a salute: a fresh reviewer caught a gun-barrel salute on the jet short's last frame, which reads as self-harm.
- Say only what the outline's `fact` supports. If it's a finding from one small study, say "probably" or "in a few", not "always".
- Question marks where you mean a question: the voice lifts on them. No em dashes, no hashtags, no ALL CAPS.
