# Why tower cranes are left free to spin when a hurricane is coming

## How it goes

**People think:** That before a storm you'd lock everything: brakes on, the arm tied down so it can't swing over the street.

**Truth:** A crane held still has to take whatever the wind throws at the whole side of its long arm, and that can bring down the arm or the whole crane. So when a crane is left for a storm, the brake that stops it turning is released. The long arm catches more wind than the short one behind the tower, so the wind swings the top round like a weathervane until the arm points downwind, and it keeps turning as the wind shifts. Crane people call it weathervaning, and Florida law now orders it before every hurricane.

**Inside:** The crane seen from straight above, wind as moving balls streaming across. First locked, side-on: the balls pile up against the whole length of the arm and a bending glow climbs the tower, with a cross. Then the brake shown releasing at the turning ring: the balls push the long arm round until it trails downwind, most of them now slipping past, the glow fading, with a tick. Then the stream of balls changes direction and the arm follows.

1. The top of a tower crane turns on a ring: a long arm in front, and a short arm with the counterweights behind.
2. Locked in place, the long arm can end up side-on to the wind, and the wind's push on all that length loads the tower: Britain's safety regulator warns it can bring down the arm or the whole crane.
3. So before the storm the crew grease the turning ring, cut the power and release the turning brake. Nobody stays in the cab.
4. The wind pushes harder on the long arm than on the short one, so the top swings round until the long arm trails downwind.
5. When the wind changes direction, the arm swings again and follows it, all night, by itself.

**The turn:** It only works if nothing stops it turning, which is why the same law orders the ring greased and the power off: a brake left on by mistake turns the crane back into a wall. And it has a limit. In Hurricane Milton in October 2024 a tower crane in St. Petersburg, Florida, came down onto an office building anyway. That fall is why Florida wrote the rule, in 2025: every tower crane set to weathervane no later than 24 hours before a hurricane.

**The ending:** Morning: the sky clearing, the crane still standing over the wet city with its arm pointing the way the wind went. Said: it wasn't left loose by mistake. Locked, it has to fight the wind. Free, it turns its back and lets the wind go by.

## Where it's known from

Florida Statutes, section 489.1132 (2025; created by chapter 2025-190, Senate Bill 180), read on the Florida Senate's site, which shows it is today's rule: 'no later than 24 hours before the impacts of the hurricane are anticipated to begin' hoisting equipment must be secured; 'Tower cranes must be set in the weathervane position'; 'Tower crane turntables must be lubricated before the event'; 'All power at the base of tower cranes must be disconnected'. Why: Britain's Health and Safety Executive, safety bulletin FOD 2-2014 (20 Feb 2014), on luffing-jib tower cranes: left unattended they 'must be in free slew with the jib at a safe out-of-service radius'; with the slew brake on or the jib parked wrongly the crane may be unable to weathervane, with 'very high loadings being placed on the crane' and possible 'collapse of the jib or the whole crane': https://www.hse.gov.uk/safetybulletins/luffing-jib-tower-cranes.htm How it turns: the equipment-hire firm Dozr, 16 Oct 2020 (a trade explainer, not an engineering source): weathervaning is 'releasing the slew brake or parking brake'; 'The front jib is longer and heavier, meaning that it will move in the direction of the wind', which reduces the pressure on the tower; cranes are 'built to withstand winds up to 100mph' when procedures are followed. Still done, and the Milton crane: iHeart's Miami station, 1 Jun 2026: a crane collapse during Hurricane Milton in 2024 led Florida to pass the rules, in June 2025, and the Florida Building Commission has set out guidelines for securing tower cranes before a storm: https://throwbackmiami.iheart.com/content/2026-06-01-new-law-mandates-building-crane-safety-guidelines-during-hurricanes/ Seen only in search summaries, not opened: a Queensland WorkSafe alert of January 2026 (a weathervaning crane withstands higher winds than a locked one; swinging over neighbours' property is the safe practice) which refused to open; a University of Florida interim report of February 2026 saying weathervaning may not be enough in strong hurricanes (a PDF that could not be read); that the Milton crane fell in St. Petersburg onto an office building (a news headline in search results; the iHeart piece says only 'a crane collapse during Hurricane Milton'). Our own wording: 'like a weathervane until the arm points downwind' and wind 'slipping past' (the sources say it moves in the direction of the wind and the load is reduced); that the long arm catches more wind than the short one (Dozr says longer and heavier); 'a brake left on turns the crane back into a wall'; that nobody stays in the cab. The HSE bulletin is about one kind of tower crane; the Florida rule covers all tower cranes.
