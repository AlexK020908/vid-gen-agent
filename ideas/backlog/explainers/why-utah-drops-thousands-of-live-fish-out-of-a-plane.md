# Why Utah drops thousands of live fish out of a plane

## The first second

**Heard:** A plane skims a mountain lake and dumps thousands of live fish out.

**Seen:** A small plane low over a mountain lake, a long white cloud of water and tiny fish pouring out from under its belly.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A plane low over a lake with a cloud of water and tiny live fish pouring out of its belly.
- **What grabs them:** Dropping a live animal from a plane should kill it.
- **What's at stake:** Thousands of live young fish, falling from 150 feet.
- **The answer you watch:** One small fish and one big trout dropped side by side: the small one drifts down slowly and swims off, the big one hits hard.

## How it goes

**People think:** It's a stunt, or the fish must die when they hit: a fall like that onto water would kill anything.

**Truth:** It's how Utah's wildlife agency stocks more than 300 mountain lakes a year that no road reaches. Only fish 1 to 3 inches long are dropped, because for something that small the air slows the fall, so they land gently. The agency's biologists have netted lakes minutes after a drop and report high survival, and say bigger fish would not survive as well.

**Inside:** The plane's belly cut open to show the tank's seven chambers and one door opening; then the fall slowed right down: one fingerling beside a big trout dropped from the same height, air as moving balls piling up under each, a speed badge on each with no numbers, the small one's reading slower and the big one's faster.

1. The lakes sit high in the mountains with no road to them, so a hatchery truck can't get there.
2. So the young fish, 1 to 3 inches long, go into a tank in the plane's belly: more than 10,000 of them a flight, in seven chambers with hundreds of pounds of water.
3. The pilot comes in 50 to 150 feet above the lake and opens one chamber, and fish and water pour out together.
4. A fish that small weighs almost nothing for the amount of air it has to push through, so the air slows its drop and it flutters down instead of plunging.
5. It lands, and swims off. A bigger fish falls harder, which is why only the small ones go by air.

**The turn:** It isn't the desperate option. The agency says dropping them from a plane would still be the cheaper way even if every one of those lakes had a road, and it has done it since at least 1956. Before the planes, by its own account in press reports, the fish went up in metal milk cans tied to pack horses.

**The ending:** The fingerlings hit the water in a patter of small splashes and dart away, while the plane banks toward the next lake with six chambers still shut. Said: a fish this small falls slowly enough that nearly all of them survive. That's the whole trick, and it's why only the small ones fly.

## Where it's known from

Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, Aerial fish stocking in Utah (the agency's own page): fish 1 to 3 inches, 'the air slows their drop', larger fish would survive less well, 50 to 150 feet above the water, over 10,000 2.5-inch fish and up to 84 pounds of fish a flight, seven chambers, hundreds of pounds of water, more than 300 lakes a year, since as early as 1956, cheaper than a truck even where a road exists, biologists surveying within minutes of a drop. The page gives no survival percentage: the '95%' in news reports (CNN 2018 and others) is the agency quoted by the press. Pack horses and milk cans: Fox News, 13 Jul 2021, quoting the agency, not its current page. That a small body reaches a low falling speed because of its weight against its surface is ordinary physics, stated by us, not by the agency.
