# Why fruit growers spray their blossom with water on a freezing night

## The first second

**Heard:** It's freezing tonight, and this farmer is spraying his apple blossom with water.

**Seen:** An apple tree in blossom at night, every branch and flower cased in clear ice with icicles hanging, and a sprinkler still spraying water over it.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A blossoming apple tree at night, every flower cased in ice, a sprinkler still pouring water over it.
- **What grabs them:** Ice is what kills blossom, and he's making more of it on the coldest night of spring.
- **What's at stake:** The blossom, and the year's apples with it: dead by morning if the air reaches about -2 C.
- **The answer you watch:** Each drop freezing onto the flower with a small warm glow; a thermometer inside the ice holding at 0 while the one in the air falls; the unsprayed tree brown by morning.

## How it goes

**People think:** Ice is what kills a plant, so wetting it on a freezing night is the worst thing you could do. Or, at a guess, the ice must wrap the flower like a blanket.

**Truth:** Water gives out heat as it turns to ice. As long as fresh water keeps landing and freezing on the flower, that heat holds the flower at about 0 C, while the air around it drops to the temperatures that kill open apple blossom (about -2 C starts killing it, about -4 C kills most of it).

**Inside:** A cut through one flower in its shell of ice: a wet film on the outside, each drop that freezes giving off a small warm glow into the flower; a thermometer badge inside the ice holding at 0 while a second badge in the air falls to -4; then the sprinkler stops, the wet film steams away as small balls leaving the surface, and the inside badge drops below the air's.

1. On a clear, still spring night the air in the orchard falls below freezing, and open apple blossom starts to die at about -2 C.
2. The grower turns on sprinklers over the trees, and water lands on every flower.
3. As each bit of water freezes it gives out heat: 334 kilojoules for every kilogram, straight into the flower it's freezing on.
4. While there's still wet water on top of the ice, the flower inside stays at about 0 C, while the air around it goes on getting colder.
5. So the sprinklers must keep running all night: clear ice with water dripping off it means enough water is arriving, and milky white ice means too little.

**The turn:** Do it badly and it's worse than doing nothing. Kilo for kilo, water that evaporates takes away about seven and a half times the heat that freezing gives out, so if the sprinklers stop early or spray too little, the wet flower falls well below the air's temperature and dies sooner than a dry one. And the ice gets heavy enough to snap branches.

**The ending:** Morning sun on the orchard: the ice melts and drips off, and the blossom under it is white and whole, beside an unsprayed tree whose blossom has gone brown. Said: the ice was never a blanket. The freezing was the heater.

## Where it's known from

FAO, Frost Protection: fundamentals, practice and economics (Snyder and de Melo-Abreu, 2005), chapter on active protection, Sprinklers: 334.5 kJ released per kg of water freezing at 0 C against 2501 kJ/kg removed by evaporation; coated plant parts stay at about 0 C while a liquid-ice mixture remains; wet tissue can fall well below air temperature; inadequate rates in windy conditions 'can cause more damage than if the sprinklers are not used'; clear ice against milky ice; ice weight can snap branches; don't stop until the wet-bulb temperature upwind is above the damage temperature. Apple blossom figures: Michigan State University Extension's table of critical spring temperatures (full bloom: 10% killed at 28 F, 90% at 25 F), from Washington State University bulletins: https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/critical-spring-temperatures-for-tree-fruit-bud-development-stages The 7.5 is 2501 divided by 334.5, our arithmetic; FAO's own break-even figure is 6.0 times, counting the water cooling from 20 C.
