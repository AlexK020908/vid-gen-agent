## How it goes

**People think:** That a plastic bag is the last thing you'd put a baby in, and that a wet newborn should be rubbed dry and wrapped in a warm blanket.

**Truth:** A baby born more than eight weeks early arrives wet, and the water drying off its skin carries its heat away faster than its small body can make more. Drying it with a towel takes time and leaves the skin bare. So the team dries only the head, puts on a hat, and slides the wet body into a polythene bag up to the neck. The water can't leave the bag, so the heat stays in the baby.

**Inside:** Close on the baby's wet skin: water leaving it as small balls rising into the air, each one carrying off a small warm glow, a thermometer badge falling. Then the bag closes round the body: the balls rise, meet the plastic and stay inside, the glow stays on the baby, and warmth from the lamp above comes down through the bag. The badge holds.

1. A baby born before 32 weeks arrives very small and wet, into a room far cooler than the womb.
2. As the water on its skin dries off into the air, it takes heat from the baby with it, and the baby's temperature starts to fall.
3. So the team doesn't dry the body. They dry only the head and put a hat on it, and slide the wet body into a clear polythene bag up to the neck.
4. Inside the bag the water has nowhere to go, so it stops carrying heat away, and a warming lamp above keeps heating the baby through the bag.
5. The baby's temperature holds: in the trial that started it, bagged babies reached the ward at 36.5 C on average, and towel-dried babies at 35.6 C.

**The turn:** It works well enough to cause the opposite problem. Across the trials, about 12 in every 1,000 babies cared for the usual way got too hot, and about 46 in every 1,000 of the bagged ones did. So a thermometer stays on the baby the whole time, and the aim is a narrow band: 36.5 to 37.5 C.

**The ending:** The baby lies in an incubator, still in its bag and hat, with a thermometer badge reading 37.0 beside it, and beside that the same scene without the bag, the badge falling. Said: the cold room wasn't the danger. Drying off was. So they don't let the baby dry.

## Where it's known from

Resuscitation Council UK, Newborn resuscitation and support of transition of infants at birth, guidelines published 27 Oct 2025 (which also shows it is today's practice), under 'Newborn infants < 32 weeks': 'Dry the infant's head and cover with a hat'; 'Put the infant's body in a plastic (polyethene) bag or wrap without drying'; 'Use a preheated heat source (e.g. radiant warmer)'; all newborns to be kept between 36.5 C and 37.5 C. The American Heart Association's 2025 neonatal guidelines also name plastic wrap or a bag, by a search summary; their page refused to open. Temperatures: Vohra and others, Journal of Pediatrics, 2004, a trial in babies under 28 weeks wrapped from the neck down with only the head dried: 36.5 C against 35.6 C on admission (read in a search summary of the abstract; an hour later the two groups were alike). Hypothermia and overheating: Cochrane review of 2018 as summarised by the UK's NIHR: 495 against 738 per 1,000 babies cold on admission; overheating 46 against 12 per 1,000; no clear difference in deaths (153 against 168 per 1,000): https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/plastic-wraps-or-bags-keep-pre-term-infants-warm-immediately-after-birth That evaporation from wet skin is the largest route of heat loss in these babies, and that the bag cuts it, is from Oatley, Blencowe and Lawn, Journal of Perinatology, 2016, and the Cochrane review, both read in search summaries. That the lamp's warmth passes through the clear bag is our reading of why the guideline pairs the bag with a radiant warmer, not a quoted sentence. This is done by a hospital team under a heater with the head outside the bag: the short must show that plainly, since a plastic bag is otherwise a danger to a baby.
