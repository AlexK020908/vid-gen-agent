# Why live chicks can be posted in a box with no food or water

- **Kind:** explainers, built as `why_it_happens`

## The first second

**Heard:** Live chicks come by post in a box with no food or water.

**Seen:** A cardboard box with round air holes on a post office counter, small yellow chick heads and beaks poking out of the holes, a parcel label on its side.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A parcel box on a post office counter with live chicks' heads poking out of its air holes.
- **What grabs them:** Baby animals a day old are being posted like a parcel, with nothing to eat or drink for up to three days.
- **What's at stake:** The chicks in the box: they starve or dry out if the yolk runs out before the box arrives.
- **The answer you watch:** In x-ray, the yolk inside a chick's belly shrinking as a clock counts to 72 hours; a chick in the egg drawing the yolk in; chicks under a hen waiting for the last eggs.

## How it goes

**People think:** That it must be cruel or a mistake: a baby animal a day old, shut in a box for up to three days with nothing to eat or drink, should arrive dead or starving.

**Truth:** A chick hatches with its food already inside it. Just before hatching it takes what is left of the egg's yolk into its own belly, and that yolk feeds and waters it for the first days. So hatcheries post chicks on the day they hatch, and the US Postal Service's rules give the box 72 hours from hatching to arrive.

**Inside:** An x-ray of one chick in the box: the yellow yolk inside its belly, big at the start, with a clock badge counting from 0 to 72 hours as the yolk shrinks. Then a cut through an egg just before hatching, the chick drawing the yolk in. Then a nest under a hen: two chicks already out and waiting beside eggs that haven't hatched yet.

1. A hatchery packs chicks into a ventilated box on the day they hatch: by the postal rules they must be no more than 24 hours old.
2. The box holds no food and no water, and the rules give it until 72 hours after hatching to be delivered, with the hour of hatching written on its side.
3. Just before a chick breaks out of its egg, it draws the last of the egg's yolk into its own belly.
4. That yolk is rich in energy and water, so the chick lives on it for its first days and doesn't need to eat or drink yet.
5. As the hours pass the yolk is used up, so the box has to arrive before it runs out, and the first thing the chicks are given is water and feed.

**The turn:** The yolk was never meant for the post. Eggs under a hen don't all hatch at once, and she can't leave the nest until the last one is out, so the first chicks out have to wait under her with nothing to eat. That wait is up to about two days. The post office stretches it to three. And it's a hard limit: a Purdue University article warns that when a box takes longer than 72 hours, the chicks are in danger.

**The ending:** The box is opened at a farm and the chicks tumble out and go straight to a dish of water, one seen in x-ray with its yolk much smaller. Said: every chick hatches with enough yolk inside it to last about three days. The post office has three days to get it there.

## Where it's known from

US Postal Service, Publication 52, section 526.3, Live Day-Old Poultry: chickens, ducks, geese, turkeys and others mailable; 'Poultry must be no more than 24 hours old'; in the hatchery's original unopened box with proper ventilation; 'may be delivered to the addressee within 72 hours of the time of hatching'; the section says nothing of food or water. Still done today: USPS Postal Bulletin 22706, 9 Jul 2026, revised this very rule (boxes to carry the hatching date and hour and the words 'Delivery is expected within 72 hours of hatching'): https://about.usps.com/postal-bulletin/2026/pb22706/html/updt_001.htm ; and The Alpena News, 24 Mar 2025, on a Michigan post office handling about 150 boxes of chicks a year. No food or water in the box, and the yolk: Purdue University, 4 Aug 2020, quoting poultry scientists at Purdue, Virginia Tech and Maryland ('a specially ventilated box ... without any source of food or water'; 'Chicks can survive a few days after hatching without food and water through their yolk reserves'; delays past 72 hours endanger them): https://ag.purdue.edu/news/2020/08/you-can-order-live-poultry-through-the-mail-but-should-you.html That the yolk is taken into the belly just before hatching, lasts up to three days, and lets early chicks wait while the hen finishes hatching the rest: the hatchery My Pet Chicken's own explanation (a seller, not a study): https://www.mypetchicken.com/blogs/faqs/how-come-you-can-only-ship-chicks-when-theyre-one-day-old That the yolk is 'rich in energy and moisture' and sustains a chick up to 72 hours is from a University of California extension leaflet seen only in a search summary, not opened. Studies find chicks kept 48 hours without feed and water lose weight, so 72 hours is a limit, not a comfortable allowance. Added by the fact-check, Nov 11, 2026: junglefowl hens stay on the nest until the last chick is out, early chicks living on yolk up to two days (The Poultry Site, 2016: https://www.thepoultrysite.com/news/2016/07/using-nature-as-a-reference-to-optimise-welfare-and-performance); unfed chicks held 72 hours used just over 80% of their yolk (Aviagen brief, seen in summary). The University of California leaflet was never opened and isn't relied on.
