## The first second

**Heard:** These firefighters are setting fire to the ground under the world's biggest trees.

**Seen:** The huge red trunk of a giant sequoia, a firefighter small at its foot, and a line of low flame spreading across the forest floor toward the trunk from where the firefighter stands.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A firefighter at the foot of an enormous tree, with flames spreading from their hands across the ground toward its trunk.
- **What grabs them:** The people whose job is putting fires out are lighting one under the biggest trees on Earth.
- **What's at stake:** The largest trees alive, thousands of which were killed by wildfire in 2020 and 2021.
- **The answer you watch:** Low flames eating the dead wood on the ground and stopping at bark too thick to burn through; cones drying open in the rising heat and seeds falling; a wildfire reaching the burned patch and dying down.

## How it goes

**People think:** That fire is the one thing these trees need protecting from, so the job is to put every fire out. Lighting one on purpose looks like vandalism.

**Truth:** A low fire is what keeps a sequoia grove alive. It burns up the dead wood on the ground before that wood can feed a big fire, the tree's bark is thick enough to shrug it off, and the heat opens the tree's cones so its seeds fall onto the bare soil they need.

**Inside:** A cut through the trunk at ground level: flame against the bark, a heat glow that fades out inside the thick bark and never reaches the pale living wood. Then up in the branches: warm air as rising balls reaching a closed green cone, the cone drying brown and opening, seeds spinning down. Then from above: a wall of wildfire running through thick fuel, reaching the black burned patch and dying down.

1. Dead branches, needles and logs pile up on the ground under the trees, year after year: fuel waiting for a fire.
2. A crew lights a low fire on a calm, damp day, and it creeps along the ground eating that fuel.
3. The flames reach the trunk and do little: a sequoia's bark is fibrous and up to 18 inches (46 cm) thick, so it resists burning and keeps the heat out of the living wood.
4. Hot air rises into the branches and dries out the old cones, which have held their seeds shut for as long as 20 years. They open, and within a week or two the seeds begin to fall.
5. The seeds land on soil the fire has swept bare, the only ground where they can sprout, with sunlight reaching it.
6. When a wildfire comes later, it reaches the burned ground and finds little left to burn: in 2021 one was stopped below the General Sherman Tree by a patch burned on purpose in 2019.

**The turn:** For about a hundred years people put the fires out, and the fuel kept piling up. Then one wildfire in 2020 killed an estimated 7,500 to 10,600 large sequoias. A study in 2026 of about 26,400 sequoias in those fires found that trees standing where crews had burned on purpose were nearly four times more likely to survive.

**The ending:** Blackened ground under the giant, its trunk scorched at the foot and green at the top, and a scatter of tiny green seedlings coming up through the ash. Said: putting every fire out is what nearly killed them. Lighting small ones is how they're kept alive.

## Where it's known from

US National Park Service, Sequoia and Kings Canyon National Parks, Giant Sequoias: bark 'up to 18 inches (46 cm) thick and extremely fibrous' resists burning and insulates; seeds held 'in closed cones for perhaps 20 years'; 'When fire burns through the forest the hot air dries out older cones', which open, and 'within a week or two' seeds fall onto 'fire-swept, bare soil'; 'To germinate and survive, sequoia seeds must fall on the mineral soil left bare by fire'; fire clears competitors for the sunlight young sequoias need. Still done today: NPS news release of 16 Oct 2026, a 482-acre prescribed fire in the Grant Grove area of Kings Canyon to start 17 Oct 2026 ('Prescribed burns promote the regeneration of giant sequoias'): https://www.nps.gov/seki/learn/news/update-prescribed-burning-planned-at-grant-grove-starting-october-17-to-reduce-wildfire-risk.htm ; and NPS, 24 Jun 2025, about 126 acres burned at Big Stump East to help defend Grant Grove. The 2021 fire stopped 'below the General Sherman Tree by a 2019 prescribed burn unit': NPS, Two Sequoia Groves and a Wildfire: https://www.nps.gov/seki/two-sequoia-groves-and-a-wildfire.htm Castle Fire, 2020, 'killed an estimated 7,500 to 10,600 large sequoias' (preliminary), after a 'lack of frequent fire for the past century in most groves', where fire once burned every 6 to 35 years: NPS, Wildfires Kill Unprecedented Numbers of Large Sequoia Trees. The 2026 study: Dixon and others, Nature Communications, as reported by UC Davis on 22 Jul 2026 (about 26,400 sequoias in 19 groves; treated trees 'nearly four times more likely to survive'; about 1,900 deaths prevented): https://www.ucdavis.edu/climate/news/prescribed-burns-saved-thousands-giant-sequoias The October 2026 release doesn't say whether the burn went ahead or that sequoias stand inside that unit, and no release read says how the fire is lit: the hand torch and the 'calm, damp day' are our picture of a prescribed burn, not the Park Service's words.
