# Why seatbelts are banned on Estonia's road across the frozen sea

- **Kind:** explainers, built as `why_it_happens`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** You're driving across a frozen sea. Your seatbelt must be off, and you may not drive at 30 kilometres an hour.

**Seen:** A small car driving across flat white sea ice between two rows of marker poles, nothing but ice to the horizon, the driver seen through the window with the seatbelt hanging unfastened beside them.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A car driving across a frozen sea with nothing but ice around it, its driver's seatbelt hanging undone.
- **What grabs them:** Driving a car onto sea ice with no seatbelt on, where the rules forbid a careful 30 and allow 60.
- **What's at stake:** The driver and passengers, over freezing sea water with 24 centimetres of ice under the wheels.
- **The answer you watch:** The ice bending into a bowl under the car; at the banned speed a wave in the ice keeping pace with the car, the bowl deepening and a crack opening; at other speeds the bowl staying shallow.

## How it goes

**People think:** That a seatbelt is always safer, and that on ice the slower you go the safer you are, so 30 should be safer than 60.

**Truth:** The ice is floating, and a car's weight presses it down into a shallow bowl that travels along with the car, pushing the water under the ice aside as a boat's hull does. That makes a wave, and the wave has its own speed, set by how thick the ice is and how deep the water. Drive at that speed and the car stays on top of its own wave, and the dip and the bending in the ice are amplified until the ice can crack. On this road that speed falls between 25 and 40 km/h, so drivers are told to go slower than 25, or between 40 and 70. And the belt is off and the doors easy to open so that, if the ice does give, everyone can get out at once.

**Inside:** The ice cut through from the side, with the sea under it: the car pressing the ice into a shallow bowl, water as moving balls pushed out from under it. At a slow speed the bowl simply follows the car. At the banned speed a wave in the ice travels beside the car, the bowl deepens, a bending glow grows in the ice and a crack opens, with a cross over the speed badge reading 30. At 60 the car runs ahead and the bowl stays shallow. Then the shore: the wave reaching it and coming back.

1. When the sea freezes thick enough, more than 24 centimetres, a road is marked out across it between two Estonian islands, for cars under 2.5 tonnes.
2. The ice floats on the sea, so a car's weight presses it down into a shallow bowl, and the bowl moves along with the car.
3. The moving bowl pushes the water under the ice aside and makes a wave, which travels at its own speed, set by the ice's thickness and the water's depth.
4. At between 25 and 40 km/h the car keeps pace with that wave, so the dip under it deepens, the ice bends more and more, and it can crack.
5. So the rule is under 25, or 40 to 70, never in between; no stopping; and at least 250 metres between cars.
6. And seatbelts stay unfastened with the doors easy to open: if the ice breaks, the people inside have moments to get out.

**The turn:** The most dangerous part is the end. The wave a vehicle makes can bounce off the shoreline and come back to meet it, which adds to the bending in the ice. Canada's guide for building ice roads says to avoid bringing the road straight at the shore.

**The ending:** The car climbs off the ice up a ramp of snow onto the island's road, and the driver pulls the seatbelt across and clicks it in. Said: on ice the danger isn't going fast or going slow. It's going at the speed of your own wave.

## Where it's known from

Estonian public broadcaster ERR, on the ice road between Saaremaa and Hiiumaa that the Transport Administration opened at 9 a.m. on 8 Feb 2026 (which also shows it's still done): vehicles under 2.5 tonnes; 'The recommended speed is up to 25 km/h or 40-70 km/h', speeds of 25 to 40 km/h to be avoided because they 'may create a resonance wave that could damage the ice'; at least 250 metres between vehicles; no stopping; seat belts unfastened and doors easily openable; daylight only. Associated Press, 10 Feb 2026 (Kostya Manenkov): ice kept at 'more than 24 centimeters', cars 'not allowed to stop', 'Passengers cannot wear seat belts', doors must open easily so people can get out quickly, and anything between the two speed bands 'can create a vibration that damages the ice' (AP gives the lower band as under 20 km/h; ERR's figure is used): https://www.click2houston.com/news/weird-news/2026/02/10/take-a-ride-across-frozen-sea-on-estonias-ice-road/ The physics was read in Wikipedia's Ice road article, which cites the US Army's Cold Regions laboratory (CRREL, 2006) and the Government of the Northwest Territories' Guidelines for Safe Ice Construction (2015), neither opened: below the critical speed the ice 'will assume the shape of a bowl moving with the vehicle, pushing away the water around it, as the keel of a boat does'; the critical speed depends on 'ice thickness and water depth'; at it 'the deflection and the stresses in the ice sheet are amplified'; waves can reflect off the shoreline, so approaches at 90 degrees are avoided; dynamic loading may set a minimum distance between vehicles. Not sourced: the Transport Administration's own rule page was not found, and ERR gives no reason for the seatbelt rule (AP gives the reason for the doors); that the 250 metres is to keep two bowls apart, and that at 60 the car 'runs ahead' of its wave, are our reading. The road exists only in winters cold enough: it opened in February 2026 after several years without one, by news reports seen in summary.
