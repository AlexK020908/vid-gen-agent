# The owner's idea: why a fork sparks in the microwave, when its metal walls don't

- **Kind:** explainers, built as `why_it_happens`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** A fork sparks in the microwave. The metal walls around it don't.

**Seen:** Inside a dark, running microwave lit by its lamp: a steel fork on a plate on the turning glass tray, small blue-white sparks snapping at the tips of its tines, their flicker reflected in the metal walls all round it.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** Sparks snapping at the tips of a fork's tines inside a running microwave: it looks like a fire about to start, in an oven everyone owns.
- **What grabs them:** Someone has left a fork in a running microwave, the one thing everyone's told never to do, and it's sparking. A stranger would think it's about to catch fire or wreck the oven. And the box around it is metal too, and isn't sparking.
- **What's at stake:** A fire in your own kitchen, and the oven itself: thin metal can light a paper towel, and a long arc can damage the oven. No life is at risk, so the stake is softer than a bird's brain or chicks in a box.
- **The answer you watch:** Sparks at the fork's tines while a spoon beside it stays quiet; crumpled foil crackling and a small smooth piece over the food staying quiet; a spark jumping the gap to the wall; the oven's own rack heating two plates without a spark. Inside: electrons as balls crowding into the fork's tips until the air at each tip lights.

## How it goes

**People think:** Metal and microwaves don't mix: any metal at all, a fork or a spoon, bounces the waves about, sparks, and can wreck the microwave.

**Truth:** It isn't metal as such: the oven itself is a metal box, and a clean, undamaged oven's walls bounce the waves all day without a spark. What matters is shape and place. The waves shove the loose electrons in any metal back and forth. On a big smooth surface they spread out thin; at sharp points, a fork's tines or the creases in crumpled foil, they crowd together until the air at the tip gives way in a spark. Metal close to a wall can spark across to it, and thin metal can overheat and burn. So the metal that's made to go in, the rack some microwaves come with, has no loose ends, is held off the walls by plastic supports, and is built for that one oven.

**Inside:** Close and dark inside the cavity: the waves as faint glowing bands bouncing between the metal walls, the metal's electrons as small bright balls sliding back and forth with each wave. On the wall and on the spoon they spread thin and stay dim. In the fork they bunch into the four tips, the tips glow brighter, and the air at each tip lights into a small blue-white spark. Crumpled foil with a bunch at every crease; a small smooth piece shaped over one end of the food, dim. A gap badge between foil and wall shrinking until a spark jumps it. A gold rim sparking along its line, and a paper towel beside a twist tie browning. Last, the rack: balls flowing evenly round its closed loops with no bunching, its plastic feet holding it off the wall, at a badge reading 'not touching'.

1. The oven makes waves that push back and forth 2.45 billion times a second, and its metal walls bounce them round and round through the food.
2. Every push shoves the loose electrons in any metal one way, then back the other.
3. On the big, smooth walls those electrons spread out thin, so the walls just bounce the waves, without getting very hot and without a spark.
4. In a fork they crowd into the four sharp tips, until the push at each tip is strong enough to tear electrons out of the air itself (air gives way at about 30,000 volts across a centimetre), and the air lights up: a spark.
5. A smooth spoon has no tips, so it's far less likely to spark, though makers like Frigidaire and LG still say no metal at all. Crumple foil and every crease becomes a tip, so it crackles; the makers that allow foil say a small, new, smooth piece shaped to the food, over no more than a quarter of it.
6. Close to a wall, a spark can jump across to it, so the makers that allow some metal say keep it off the walls: Panasonic says at least an inch (2.5 cm).

**The turn:** Thin metal does more than spark. A twist tie is too thin for that sloshing current, so it can overheat and burn, and a plate's gold line sparks: Sharp says gilded ware can be 'completely ruined in moments'. One maker's manual warns that a thin metal strip in a food tray 'can burn the floor of the oven or ignite a paper towel', and the US National Institutes of Health's fire marshal says metal 'can spark, damage the unit and lead to a fire'. So the moment you see a spark, switch it off: caught at once, Whirlpool says, it shouldn't damage the oven, though a long one can.

**The ending:** The wire rack that came with the oven, two plates of food on it in the glow: rounded bars joined into closed loops with no loose ends, resting on its plastic supports, not touching the walls, and not one spark. Said: it wasn't the metal alone. It was the points, the thin bits, and the walls too close. So the one piece of metal that's made to go in, the rack that came with the oven, has no points, stands clear of the walls, and is built for that one oven. Your fork has points, and wasn't built for it.

## Where it's known from

How metal behaves, from a physicist's own page: Louis Bloomfield (University of Virginia), How Everything Works, answer of 17 Jan 1997 (the source_url): 'a microwave pushes electrons back and forth through any metals it encounters'; the oven's metal walls 'simply reflect the microwaves without becoming very hot and without emitting any sparks'; 'Electrons are more likely to leave the end of a metal surface at a sharp point'; 'Most spoons are smooth enough that no sparks will occur'; 'a thin twist-tie or small bit of aluminum foil may well overheat and begin to burn'. Scientific American, Mark Fischetti with Bloomfield, 'How the Microwave Works', 1 Nov 2008 (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-the-microwave-works/): 'metal does not necessarily cause sparking inside a microwave', 'the cooking chamber walls are metal', 'Shape matters', 'A flat, round, metal platter will spread charge around it, preventing buildup', and 'sharper points, such as fork tines or the many tiny edges in aluminum foil, concentrate charge' to make 'corona discharge—a spark'. The walls: US FDA, Microwave Ovens (content current as of 12 Oct 2023): 'The microwaves reflect off the metal interior of the oven'; 'Generally, metal pans or aluminum foil should also not be used'; 'Some ovens should not be operated when empty'. The frequency: HyperPhysics (Georgia State University), 'Modern microwave ovens operate at the frequency 2,450 MHz', a wavelength of about 12 cm. The air: Troy Messina, Electromagnetics I (Berea College, on LibreTexts), section 5.21: 'about 3 MV/m for air'. Said per centimetre, as NPTEL's high-voltage lecture gives it: 'The value 31 kV/cm is applicable only for d = 1, that is for one cm gap at 760 Torr and 20°C', and 'For gaps of a few mm, the electric strength is much higher' (so not 'per millimetre'). That it roughly holds at the oven's 2.45 GHz and normal pressure is from Khodataev's 2009 abstract (a library record, not the paper), so the badge says 'about'. That the field at a tip grows strong enough to pass it is the physicists' account above, not a number anyone measured in a fork. Foil: Sub-Zero Wolf's support page, in words that match the USDA's (whose own page refused to open, so the USDA is seen only in search summaries): 'Use new, smooth foil only. Wrinkled foil can cause arcing (sparks)'; 'Shape the foil smoothly to the food so no edges stick out'; 'Do not place the foil closer than one inch from the oven walls'. The inch for any metal: Panasonic's support page, 'When using foil, skewers, racks or utensils made of metal in the oven during Microwave or Combination cooking', 'allow at least 2.5 cm (1") of space between the metal material and interior oven walls'; LG's LMV1831 manual, 'Never allow metal to touch walls or door'. GE's 'Metal Cavity' support page gives the jump to the wall: metal utensils can cause 'arcing from the metal object to the nearest grounded object which is usually a wall'. Other makers allow no metal but the rack: Frigidaire; LG's help page, 'DO NOT place aluminum or metal like items in the microwave', and that metal 'will cause sparking and damage the microwave'; Sharp (16 Jul 2019), 'The short answer is absolutely not', and even smooth foil can arc 'if microwaves encountered points or edges'. Sub-Zero Wolf, for foil: 'use small amounts', 'Cover no more than 1/4 of the food with foil'. Gold rims: Panasonic, 'Do not use metal containers or dishes with metallic trim during Microwave or Combination cooking'; Emerson's 1100W manual, 'METAL CONTAINERS or dishes with metallic rims should not be used. Arcing may occur.' LG: 'Bowls or plates with gold or silver lined patterns will begin to spark'; Sharp: 'Any gilded flatware or utensil can cause arcing and be completely ruined in moments'. Fire: Emerson's manual, of a thin strip of metal in some styrofoam trays, 'When microwaved, the metal can burn the floor of the oven or ignite a paper towel'; the NIH Fire Marshal, giving NFPA's 2023 Fire Prevention Week message: 'The metal can spark, damage the unit and lead to a fire', and 'If you have a fire in your microwave oven, unplug the unit and leave the door shut.' Damage, which is half true: Whirlpool's support page, 'Prolonged arcing can damage the oven and/or the utensil', 'If caught at once, arcing should not damage the oven', 'Aluminum foil and some metal can be used in the microwave oven'. The fear for the magnetron belongs mostly to an empty oven: Panasonic, 'Operating the oven empty for a short time will not damage the oven', but it can 'cause damage to the microwave's magnetron'; Whirlpool's patent US 8,530,807 (2013): 'strong reflection from the cavity typically occurs when the cavity is empty. Such reflection may deteriorate or even destroy the magnetron.' That a fork beside food seldom harms the magnetron is our reading of these, with a 2006 Fraunhofer IVV study done for the European Aluminium Foil Association, seen only on that association's page and in summaries (no damage to four ovens heating foil trays at full power; its PDFs no longer open). The rack: GE Appliances' support page on the Double Duty Shelf, its metal shelves 'present no danger of arcing if they are properly inserted' and 'should not touch the back wall of the oven'; Frigidaire's FAQ: 'The plastic spacers which hold the rack in place are engineered to keep it from arching against the microwave's sides'; its tines and outer edges 'are engineered to form a fully enclosed object', avoiding 'the open ends that lead to arching'; 'No other metal objects, including pans, utensils, etc. are approved for use inside a microwave'; Sharp's Simply Better Living (16 Jul 2019): 'the rack that came with the oven is specifically machined not to have edges', and it can't be moved to another oven; LG: 'Only use the rack that is supplied'; Whirlpool: 'Always remove the rack after 2-level cooking', and 'Using the provided accessory racks with microwave function can cause arcing or sparking. To avoid damage... do not allow the rack or pan to touch the inside cavity walls, ceiling, or floor'; GE: 'All microwave cavities are "tuned" to a specific size and configuration, as are the metal racks that come in some models'. Dirty or damaged cavity walls can arc too (Whirlpool names soil build-up; GE has a cavity-repair page), hence 'a clean, undamaged oven' in the truth. Still sold: P.C. Richard's listing for Frigidaire's GMOS1968AF over-the-range microwave includes a 'Metal Rack'. Metal containers sold for microwaves: LG's manual allows 'shallow foil trays (if tray is 3/4 inch deep and filled with food to absorb microwave energy)'. The spark's colour: Whirlpool, 'Arcing (a blue flash of light)'.
