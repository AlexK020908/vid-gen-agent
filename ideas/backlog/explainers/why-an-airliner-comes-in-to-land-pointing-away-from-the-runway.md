# Why an airliner comes in to land pointing away from the runway

- **Kind:** explainers, built as `why_it_happens`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** This airliner is about to land, and it's pointing away from the runway.

**Seen:** Seen from the far end of the runway: an airliner low on its approach with its wheels down, its nose pointing well off to one side of the runway, coming toward us sideways.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** An airliner a few metres above a runway, wheels down, flying sideways with its nose pointing off into the grass.
- **What grabs them:** The plane is aimed at the wrong place, seconds before it lands.
- **What's at stake:** Everyone on board, a few metres above the ground in a wind strong enough to push an airliner sideways.
- **The answer you watch:** Wind streaming across a runway and carrying a straight-aimed plane off to the side; a plane with its nose into the wind tracking straight down the centre line; the nose swinging round just before the wheels touch.

## How it goes

**People think:** That the pilot has lost control, or that it's about to skid off the side: a plane, like a car, should point where it's going.

**Truth:** A wind is blowing across the runway, and a plane in the air is carried along by the air it flies in. Aimed straight down the runway it would drift off to one side. So the pilot turns the nose partly into the wind, and the plane's path over the ground runs straight down the middle of the runway while its nose points off it. Pilots call it a crab. Then, just before the wheels touch, the pilot presses the rudder and swings the nose round to line up with the runway, so the wheels are pointing the way the plane is moving when they meet the ground.

**Inside:** The runway from straight above: wind as moving balls streaming across it. One plane aimed straight down the runway slides off to the side, its path drawn as a slanting line with a cross. A second plane with its nose turned into the wind draws a path straight down the centre line, with a tick. Then close on the wheels at touchdown: skewed wheels scrubbing sideways with a strain glow on the legs, then the nose swinging straight and the wheels rolling. Then the B-52's wheel sets turning under its body.

1. A wind blows across the runway. A plane in the air moves with the air around it, so one aimed straight down the runway is carried off to the side.
2. So the pilot turns the nose partly into the wind, and part of the plane's own push now works against the wind's.
3. The two cancel, and the plane's path over the ground runs straight down the middle of the runway, though its nose points off to one side.
4. It flies like that all the way down. Just before the wheels touch, the pilot presses the rudder on the downwind side, and the nose swings round to line up with the runway.
5. Now the wheels point the way the plane is moving, so they roll, instead of being dragged sideways across the runway and wrenching the legs that hold them.

**The turn:** One plane never straightens up. A B-52 bomber's wings are so long that it can't safely swing round at the last moment, so its wheels turn instead: both sets can be set as much as 20 degrees to either side. It touches down still pointing off the runway and rolls along it sideways, like a crab.

**The ending:** A B-52 rolls down the runway with its nose pointing off to one side and all its wheels pointing straight along the runway. Said: the nose was never aimed at the runway. It was aimed at the wind. Only the wheels have to point down the runway.

## Where it's known from

Boeing's 737 NG Flight Crew Training Manual, as quoted with page numbers by the pilot-run reference Code 7700 (page updated 24 Jun 2022; the manual itself is not public): 'The initial phase of the approach to landing is flown using the crab method to correct for drift'; 'Just prior to touchdown while flaring the airplane, downwind rudder is applied to eliminate the crab'; and 'touchdown in a crab only condition is not recommended when landing on a dry runway in strong crosswinds'. The same page quotes Cessna, Gulfstream, Bombardier and Dassault manuals to the same effect, and summarises the US safety board's report on FedEx flight 647 (2003), where a very hard landing made in a 6-degree crab collapsed the right landing gear. The B-52: The Aviationist, 5 May 2024, quoting NASA, which flew one: 'Both the front and rear bogies can be set at angles of as much as 20 degrees to either side of the straight-ahead position', so it can touch down with its gear turned, which reduces side loads; the article says that because of its wingspan the B-52 can't straighten up as other planes do: https://theaviationist.com/2024/05/05/the-b-52-landing-gear-explained/ Still done today: Malay Mail, 13 May 2026, on an AirAsia jet filmed on 4 May 2026 flying a crab landing in strong crosswinds in Taiwan and straightening just before its wheels touched (read only as a search summary: the page refused to open). Not opened: the US aviation authority's Airplane Flying Handbook, chapter 9, a PDF, which by search summaries says the crab must be removed the instant before touchdown. Our own wording: 'part of the plane's own push works against the wind's' and 'wrenching the legs' (the manuals speak of drift and of side loads); Boeing's manual in fact lets a 737 touch down crabbed within limits, so 'dragged sideways' is the reason for straightening in strong wind on a dry runway, not a rule for every landing. Pilots also lower the wing on the windward side as they straighten; it's left out to keep one thing to follow.
