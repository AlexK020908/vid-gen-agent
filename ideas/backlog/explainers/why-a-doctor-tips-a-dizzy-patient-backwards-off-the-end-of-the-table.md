# Why a doctor tips a dizzy patient backwards off the end of the table

**Heard:** She's too dizzy to stand, so the doctor tips her over backwards, fast.

**Seen:** A woman sitting on an exam table, a doctor holding her head in both hands, turned to one side, and tipping her quickly backwards so that her head hangs just off the end of the table.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A doctor grips a seated woman's head and drops her backwards so her head hangs off the end of the table.
- **What grabs them:** She's dizzy, and he is throwing her head about, which is exactly what she's been avoiding.
- **What's at stake:** The viewer's own body: about 1 person in 40 gets this in their life, and while it lasts, turning over in bed makes the room spin.
- **The answer you watch:** Bright grains rolling round a curved tube inside the ear as the head is turned and laid back, then dropping out of its end.

## How it goes

**People think:** That someone whose world is spinning should be kept still, and that flinging her head about is the worst thing for it. And that dizziness like this is in the brain, or needs pills.

**Truth:** The commonest vertigo of this kind (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo) is tiny calcium crystals in the wrong place. They have come loose inside the ear and fallen into one of its curved, fluid-filled tubes, where they set off the motion sensors every time the head tips, so the brain is told the head is spinning when it isn't. The doctor turns the head through a set of positions so that gravity rolls the crystals round the tube and out of its end, back into the chamber they came from.

**Inside:** An x-ray of her head with the inner ear made big beside it: one chamber and three curved tubes, the crystals as bright grains lying in the rear tube. As the head model is turned and laid back, the tube turns with it and the grains roll along it like a ball in a tilting maze, a spin badge flaring each time they move; at the last position they fall out of the tube's end and the badge goes dark.

1. Deep in each ear are tiny calcium crystals that belong in one chamber, and beside it three curved tubes full of fluid that sense the head turning.
2. Some crystals break loose and fall into one of the tubes, most often the rear one.
3. Now whenever she rolls over in bed or bends down, the crystals roll along the tube and set off its sensors, and the room seems to spin.
4. The doctor turns her head 45 degrees toward the bad ear and lays her back quickly with her head just off the table: the crystals roll down to the low part of the tube, and the spinning comes on hard.
5. She is held there for about 20 to 30 seconds while they settle, then her head is turned slowly to the other side, her body rolled onto its side, and she is sat up: each position lets gravity roll the crystals further round.
6. The crystals drop out of the open end of the tube into the chamber they came from, and tipping her head no longer sets anything off.

**The turn:** It takes five to ten minutes, with no drug and no instrument, and it helps about 8 in 10 people. And the cure sets off the very thing she came in with: the fast drop backwards brings on the spinning, because the crystals have to roll to be moved. The crystals can also come loose again later, and then it's done again.

**The ending:** In x-ray, the last crystals slide out of the end of the curved tube and settle in the chamber. The woman sits up on the table and the room behind her holds still. Said: nothing was wrong with her brain. A few crystals were in the wrong tube, and gravity put them back.

## Where it's known from

Merck Manual Professional, Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (Mickie Hamiter MD, full review October 2025, which also shows it's today's practice): 'BPPV is thought to be caused by the displacement of otoconial crystals', normally embedded in the utricle and saccule, into the semicircular canals, where they 'stimulate hair cells most commonly in the posterior semicircular canal'; vertigo is triggered when the head moves (rolling over in bed, bending over); the Epley manoeuvre returns the crystals to the utricle and can be repeated; lifetime prevalence 2.4% in a German survey. The steps, the 20 to 30 seconds, 5 to 10 minutes, 'about 8 out of 10 people' and 'your vertigo symptoms may worsen during this part of the procedure': Cleveland Clinic, Canalith Repositioning Procedure, last updated 21 Sep 2025: https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/17930-canalith-repositioning-procedure-crp Still the first choice: Xin, Fang and Wu, Frontiers in Neurology, 2 Feb 2026, a network meta-analysis ('guide displaced otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) from the affected semicircular canal back into the utricle' by 'gravitational forces'; Epley ranked first). Not opened: the American Academy of Otolaryngology's 2017 guideline (Bhattacharyya and others), which other pages cite as recommending it. Our own wording, not a source's: that the three tubes 'sense the head turning' and that the crystals 'roll like a ball in a maze' (the sources say they are moved by gravity); that the spinning comes on 'because the crystals have to roll to be moved' is our reasoning from the two facts. Recurrence and the manoeuvre's inventor (John Epley, early 1980s) were seen only in search summaries. It treats one kind of vertigo, diagnosed first by a clinician: the short should not suggest it cures all dizziness.
