# Why intensive care teams turn a patient on a ventilator face down

## How it goes

**People think:** That face down is how you smother someone, and that a person who can't breathe should lie on his back with his chest free.

**Truth:** When you lie on your back, your heart lies on your lungs. In scans of seven patients, up to 42% of the left lung and 16% of the right sat underneath the heart when they were on their backs. Turned face down, the heart rests on the breastbone instead, and almost no lung is under it: 1% of the left or less, 4% of the right or less. In lungs already failing, getting that weight off matters. In a French trial, patients with severe lung failure who were turned face down for long sessions died about half as often in the next four weeks.

**Inside:** A cut across his chest, seen from the feet, in x-ray style: on his back, the heart sits on top of the left lung and the part under it is dark and flattened, air as moving balls reaching only the upper part, a badge reading 42% under the heart. The whole picture rolls over with him: the heart drops onto the breastbone, the flattened part springs open and the balls spread through it, and the badge falls to 1%. An oxygen badge rises.

1. His lungs are failing (doctors call it ARDS): a ventilator pushes air in through a tube, but too little oxygen reaches his blood.
2. He's on his back, so his heart rests on his lungs: up to 42% of the left lung lies under it, and the heart's weight presses on that part.
3. A team rolls him onto his front together, one of them holding the breathing tube so it can't come out.
4. Now the heart rests on the breastbone, and almost no lung is underneath it.
5. The back of the lungs, freed from the weight, gets more of the air again.
6. He stays that way for many hours, about sixteen at a time, is turned back, and the next day it is done again.

**The turn:** It needs no new machine and no drug, and in the trial that settled it, in 2013, 16% of the face-down patients had died after 28 days against 32.8% of those left on their backs. Yet it's hard work: a team has to turn a sedated patient with a tube in his throat, and hours on the face cause pressure sores. Surveys before the pandemic found that only 16% to 33% of the patients with the worst lung failure were being turned at all.

**The ending:** In x-ray, the man lies face down with his heart resting on his breastbone and both lungs lit from top to bottom, and an oxygen badge beside him climbs. Said: nothing was added. They just took the weight of his own heart off his lungs.

## Where it's known from

Albert and Hubmayr, The prone position eliminates compression of the lungs by the heart, American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine, 2000 (abstract read through Europe PMC): scans of seven patients; supine, the share of the left lung under the heart rose to about 42% and of the right to about 16%; prone, 1% or less and 4% or less. Guerin and others (PROSEVA), New England Journal of Medicine, 2013 (abstract read through Europe PMC): 28-day mortality 16.0% prone against 32.8% supine, 90-day 23.6% against 41.0%; 'early application of prolonged prone-positioning sessions significantly decreased 28-day and 90-day mortality'. Still done today, from abstracts opened through Europe PMC: Critical Care, 4 Dec 2025, a study measuring patients '16 h after' the start of prone ventilation; Heart and Lung, November 2025, a study of ventilator settings during prone positioning in severe ARDS, which says prone positioning with protective ventilation 'improved survival'; Nitric Oxide, February 2026: prone positioning increased 'dorsal ventilation and perfusion': https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=ext_id:10806172%20OR%20ext_id:23688302&resultType=core&format=json How hard it is and how seldom it was done: Cleveland Clinic, Consult QD, 8 Jun 2023: in two observational studies only 16% to 33% of patients with severe ARDS received it; almost 60% of its own proned patients in the 2009 flu surge had pressure injuries; a checklist protects the breathing tube during the turn. THIS IS THE CARD'S WEAK POINT FOR 'COMMONLY DONE': it is what guidelines recommend for severe ARDS (the 2023 European and 2024 American guidelines, seen only in search summaries), and it became widely known in the pandemic, but the surveys above say most eligible patients were not turned. No figure for 2025 or 2026 was found. From memory or search summaries, not from a page opened: that the trial's sessions were at least 16 hours and that it had 466 patients; that the lung's own weight and the belly also press on the back of the lungs when supine (a 2017 review in Chest); that proning's main benefit may be spreading the ventilator's stress more evenly rather than raising oxygen. Our own reasoning: that the part of lung under the heart 'gets little air' and 'springs open' when freed (the paper measures how much lung lies under the heart, and its title says compression; it did not measure air in that part); 'died about half as often' is 16.0 against 32.8; the oxygen badge rising (the trial's result was survival; about one patient in five doesn't improve in oxygen, by the Critical Care abstract). The seven-patient study is small. The picture of the heart's weight is one cause among several, chosen because it can be seen.
