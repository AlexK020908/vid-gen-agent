# Why Phoenix fire crews pack a heatstroke patient into a bag of ice before the hospital

- **What grabs them:** The ambulance is right there with its doors open, and instead of driving him to hospital they are burying him in ice.
- **What's at stake:** The man in the bag: at 108 F inside, every minute he stays that hot does more harm.
- **The answer you watch:** The ice shrinking to water round him as a red glow drains out of his whole body at once and a thermometer falls past 104.5; beside it, ice packs alone fading the glow slowly in three small patches.

## How it goes

**People think:** That the ambulance is right there, so you'd get him to hospital as fast as possible. And that burying an overheated person in ice sounds like a shock that would make things worse.

**Truth:** In heatstroke the harm depends on how long the body stays too hot. The experts' target is to get it below 104.5 F (about 40 C) within 30 minutes of the collapse, and their rule is 'cool first, transport second'. The fastest way to cool a person is to put them in cold water, and an ambulance can't carry a bathtub. So since 2024 every Phoenix fire vehicle has carried a person-sized waterproof bag: the crew fill it with ice and water round the patient, and the cooling carries on inside the ambulance all the way to the hospital.

**Inside:** The man in the bag seen in x-ray style with a red glow through his body and a thermometer badge at 108. Two versions: with ice packs only at the neck and armpits, the glow fades slowly in three small patches; in the bag, water covers him, the glow drains out everywhere at once into the water, the ice cubes shrinking to nothing as it does, and the badge falls past a line marked 104.5. A clock badge counts toward 30 minutes.

1. On a day above 110 F (43 C), a man collapses with heatstroke: the temperature inside his body is 108 F (42 C) or more.
2. Every minute it stays that high does more harm, so the aim is to bring it below 104.5 F within 30 minutes of his collapse.
3. A drive to hospital uses up those minutes, and ice packs and fans cool a body slowly.
4. So the crew slide him into a waterproof bag and fill it with ice and water. Cold water now lies against his skin all over, and the ice melts as it takes the heat out of him.
5. The bag goes into the ambulance with him and keeps cooling him on the road: Phoenix crews report patients going from 108 to 110 F down to 100 or 101 F by the time they arrive.

**The turn:** The equipment is almost nothing. One Phoenix firefighter calls the bag 'essentially a large trash bag', and each station keeps eight seven-pound bags of ice ready for it. The crews say it cools three to five times faster than what they did before, and in its first summer they used it more than 300 times.

**The ending:** Inside the moving ambulance the man lies in the bag of half-melted ice with a thermometer badge reading 101 F, and his eyes open. Said: the hospital was never the cure. Getting cold was. So they made the cold fit in the ambulance.
