# Why firefighters aim a giant fan into a house that's on fire

- **Kind:** explainers, built as `why_it_happens`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** This house is on fire, and firefighters aim a giant fan inside.

**Seen:** The open front door of a house with dark smoke rolling out of the top of the doorway, and a firefighter standing a big round fan on the path a few steps from the door, blowing straight in.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A firefighter stands a huge fan at the front door of a smoking house and blows air straight in.
- **What grabs them:** Blowing air at a fire is how you make it bigger.
- **What's at stake:** Anyone still inside, breathing the smoke, and the crew who have to go in blind to reach them.
- **The answer you watch:** Dark smoke pushed down a hallway and out of a far window by clear air coming in from the door; and, with no window open, the smoke staying and the fire's glow spreading.

## How it goes

**People think:** That wind feeds a fire: you blow on embers to make them glow, so a fan at the door should turn a house fire into a furnace.

**Truth:** The fan isn't aimed at the fire. It's aimed at the smoke. Hot smoke fills the house, blinds the crew and is what anyone inside is breathing. With the fan at the door they come in by, and a big enough opening made in the burning room on the far side, the air it pushes in drives the smoke and heat out through that opening, away from the crew. Fire-service experiments in whole houses found it can improve what crews can see and how survivable the rooms are while the fire is being put out. Water still puts the fire out.

**Inside:** The house with its roof lifted off, seen from above: smoke as dark moving balls filling every room, the fire room glowing. A window in the fire room opens and a few balls leave. The fan starts at the front door: clear air as pale balls marches down the hallway, the dark balls are pushed ahead of it and out of the window. Then the same house with no window opened: the pale balls go in, nothing leaves, and the glow of the fire room spreads.

1. A fire in one room fills the whole house with hot smoke, so the crew can't see and anyone inside is breathing it.
2. First an opening is made in the burning room, on the far side from the door the crew will use: the way out for the smoke.
3. Then the fan at the front door pushes air in, so the pressure inside is a little higher than outside, and the smoke and heat are pushed toward that opening.
4. Smoke pours out of the far window, and behind it clearer, cooler air fills the hallway from the door inward.
5. The crew go in along the cleared hallway with the hose and put water on the fire, which is what puts it out.

**The turn:** Get the opening wrong, and the first guess is right. Without a big enough way out, two fire chiefs who teach it say the house becomes 'a convection oven' and the fire can grow fast. The researchers who tested it say it depends on the exhaust, won't work in an open-plan house or under high ceilings, and is less safe when nobody knows where the fire is. So many crews start the fan only once the fire has been knocked down.

**The ending:** Smoke roars out of the back window while the hallway from the front door stands clear to the floor, and a firefighter kneels in it with the hose aimed at the glow of the fire room. Said: the fan was never fighting the fire. It was moving the smoke, so the crew could reach the fire.

## To settle before it's written

- FOR YOU TO JUDGE: a fan blowing in while the house is still burning is the argued-over, minority use. Most departments start the fan after the fire is knocked down, and the research says water goes on first. No dated case from the last two years of a fan used during the attack was found.
- Honest hook: 'This house is full of smoke from a fire, and firefighters aim a giant fan inside.'
- Steps, water first: 'The crew hit the fire with water as fast as they can, from outside or inside. An opening is made in the fire room on the far side. Then the fan at the door pushes air in, and the smoke and heat are driven out through that opening. With the hallway clear, the crew can see to finish the fire and find anyone inside.'
- Ending: 'The fan was never fighting the fire. It was moving the smoke.'
- Turn: the 'convection oven' quote is Garcia and Kauffmann, Salt Lake City battalion chiefs, 2007; the opening out must be 'two to three times the size of the entrance'.

## Where it's known from

UL's Fire Safety Research Institute, Study of the Effectiveness of Fire Service Positive Pressure Ventilation During Fire Attack in Single Family Homes (reports of 4 May 2016, from fires set in full-size houses): 'positive-pressure attack can provide increased visibility and tenability for firefighters and potential trapped occupants while fire suppression efforts are underway'; and its tactical considerations: 'Positive Pressure Attack is Exhaust Dependant'; 'An outlet of sufficient size, must be provided, in the fire room'; it 'will not be effective on a fire located in an open concept floor plan or any floor plan with high ceilings'; its safety 'is decreased when the location and extent of the fire is not known'; water should be applied as quickly as possible; it 'does not negatively affect the survivability of occupants behind a closed door': https://technicalpanels.fsri.org/research-projects/effectiveness-of-fire-service-positive-pressure-ventilation.html How it's set up, and 'a convection oven': Battalion Chiefs Kriss Garcia and Reinhard Kauffmann, Salt Lake City Fire Department, in Fire Engineering, 26 Nov 2007 (the fan at the attack entrance; an exhaust 'at least two to three times the size of the ventilation point', ahead of the crew on the far side of the fire; the pressure difference pushes heat and smoke toward it): https://www.fireengineering.com/firefighting/positive-pressure-attack-questions-and-answers/ Still done today: Shropshire Fire and Rescue Service's own incident record 144295, a kitchen fire in a bungalow in Telford on 11 Jan 2026, equipment used: 'Breathing Apparatus (BA), Hosereel jet, Positive Pressure Ventilation, Thermal imaging camera': https://www.shropshirefire.gov.uk/incidents/144295 That record doesn't say whether the fan ran during the attack or after the fire was out; using it after the fire is controlled is the commoner use, and using it during the attack is argued over in the fire service. 'Many crews start the fan only once the fire has been knocked down' rests on the research institute's own split between the two uses and on a Fire Engineering line seen only in a search summary. The picture of smoke hanging in rooms and clear air advancing from the door is our staging of 'pushes heat and smoke toward the exhaust'. The full technical report is a PDF that could not be read.
