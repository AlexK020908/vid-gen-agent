## How it goes

**People think:** That it's backwards: the whale is already dragging rope it can't shake off, and they're adding more weight and more drag. Surely you'd dive in and cut it free.

**Truth:** A tangled whale is free-swimming and far too strong to hold, and it can dive and swim away from a boat. So the team hooks the rope it's trailing and clips large floats to it. The floats add lift and drag, so the whale finds it hard to dive and tires, and stays near the surface moving slowly. Only then can a small boat get close enough to cut the rope with a hooked knife on a long pole.

**Inside:** A view from the side, half above and half under the water: the whale diving freely with the rope, then the floats added and a lift arrow at each float and a drag arrow behind it, the whale's dive getting shallower each time until it stays at the top; a speed badge with no numbers falling. Then the old picture beside it: a wooden whaleboat and kegs on a line, the same shape.

1. A whale swims with fishing rope wrapped round its tail, and it can dive and swim away from any boat that comes close.
2. From a small inflatable boat the team throws a grapple, a hook on a line, that catches the rope trailing behind the whale.
3. They clip large floats onto that line, and the floats pull up and drag through the water behind the whale.
4. Now the whale finds it hard to dive and has to pull the floats along, so it tires, slows down and stays at the surface.
5. The boat comes up close behind it, and a hook-shaped knife on a thirty-foot pole reaches out and cuts the rope from the tail.
6. The rope and the floats fall away together, and the whale swims off.

**The turn:** The trick comes from whaling. Whalers called it kegging: they harpooned a whale and tied wooden kegs to the line, to wear it out and hold it at the surface. The rescuers kept the floats and swapped the weapon for a knife that cuts rope. It doesn't always work: in January 2026 a young right whale off the US coast, slowed with buoys and a sea anchor, still swam away with rope round its tail.

**The ending:** The last wrap of rope is cut, the orange floats bob loose on the surface, and the whale lifts its tail and dives. Said: the floats were never there to save it. They were there to slow it down long enough for one cut.

## Where it's known from

Center for Coastal Studies (Provincetown, Massachusetts), Marine Animal Entanglement Response, which works under a NOAA Fisheries permit and says it has freed more than 200 large whales and other animals since 1984: the technique is 'a modification of an old whaling practice called kegging', attaching 'large floats, or kegs, to the gear entangling the animal'; 'The floats add buoyancy and drag to the animal, making it difficult for it to dive, eventually tiring it out'; the aim is 'a relatively immobile animal that is more safe to cut free'. The steps (grappling hooks attaching working ropes, buoys added 'to slow the whale and keep it at the surface', a hooked knife on a long pole) are from the Center's report of an 11-hour rescue on 2 Aug 2015. Still done today: the Center's report of 20 Jan 2026 (large buoys and a sea anchor added to a yearling right whale's entanglement to slow it; part of the gear broke away and the whale left with rope round its tail): https://coastalstudies.org/?p=19047 ; its report of 15 Jun 2026 (a humpback freed with 'a hook-shaped knife at the end of a thirty-foot pole'); and the San Juan Journal, 6 Sep 2025, on Cascadia Research Collective and The Whale Museum kegging a young humpback on 21 Aug 2025 ('adding large buoys to slow the whale and keep it at the surface'). That whalers fastened kegs to whales to tire them and keep them up is NOAA's account as given in a search summary, not opened; the Center's own page says only 'an old whaling practice'. That the boat comes up close behind once the whale is slow is how the reports read; the exact way the boat closes the distance is not spelled out in what was read. Added by the fact-check, Nov 11, 2026: the whaling history is NOAA Fisheries' Alaska page (https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/alaska/marine-life-distress/large-whale-entanglements-alaska); the San Juan Journal's own caption reads 'Trained responders slow the whale down by adding large kegging buoys to the entangling gear'.
