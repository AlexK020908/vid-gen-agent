# Why ships are ordered out to sea when a tsunami is on its way

- **Kind:** explainers, built as `why_it_happens`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** A tsunami is coming, and the Coast Guard orders every ship out to sea.

**Seen:** A harbour mouth with a container ship and two fishing boats steaming out past the breakwater in a line, a warning siren flashing on the quay behind them, and ahead of them a long low line of wave on the horizon.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** Ships steaming out of a harbour in a line, straight toward a long wave on the horizon, while a warning siren flashes on the quay.
- **What grabs them:** Everyone else runs from a tsunami, and these crews are sailing at it.
- **What's at stake:** The crews on board: caught in the harbour, their ships are thrown about by the surge; sent out wrongly, they meet the wave at sea.
- **The answer you watch:** A low hump of water passing under a ship in deep water and barely lifting it; the same hump slowing, bunching up and rising as the sea floor comes up under it.

## How it goes

**People think:** That the open sea is the last place to be when a tsunami comes: you'd tie the ship up tight and get everyone to land.

**Truth:** Out in the deep ocean a tsunami is rarely more than a metre high, though it is hundreds of miles long and moves as fast as a jet, and sailors don't normally notice it pass under the hull. It grows only where the water gets shallow: there it slows, bunches up and rises, and its currents get strong. A harbour is the shallow end. So when there are hours of warning, ships are sent out to deep water to wait.

**Inside:** The sea cut through from the side, from deep ocean to the harbour: a long low hump travelling fast over deep water with a ship riding over it, a height badge reading 1 metre; then the sea floor rising, the hump slowing, its back catching up with its front, and the water piling higher until it pours into the harbour. A speed badge with no numbers falls as the height badge climbs.

1. An earthquake far across the ocean heaves the sea up, and a tsunami sets off: it will take hours to arrive.
2. In deep water the wave is rarely more than a metre high but hundreds of miles long, moving at over 800 km/h, so a ship rises and settles without anyone on board noticing.
3. Near land the sea gets shallow and the wave slows to about 30 to 50 km/h. Its length shrinks, so the same water is squeezed into less room and rises.
4. Inside a harbour that arrives as surges and strong currents that go on for hours, and the first surge may not be the largest.
5. So the Captain of the Port orders the ships out, and they wait in deep water, beyond about 30 fathoms (55 metres), until the harbour is opened again.

**The turn:** It only works with hours to spare. If the earthquake is close, the wave comes in 10 to 30 minutes, and then the official advice is the opposite: leave the boat and get to high ground. Oregon's guidance says it plainly: you don't have time to save your boat and could die if you try.

**The ending:** The ships sit far out on deep water as a low hump of sea passes under them and barely lifts them, while far behind, water surges through the empty harbour. Said: out here a tsunami is a bump a metre high. It only becomes a wall where the sea runs out of depth.

## Where it's known from

NOAA, JetStream, Tsunami Propagation: 'In the deep ocean, tsunamis can move as fast as a jet plane, over 500 mph (800 km/h)', are 'rarely more than three feet (one meter) high', with wavelengths that 'can be hundreds of miles'; 'Mariners at sea will not normally notice a tsunami as it passes beneath their hulls'; 'As a tsunami enters shallow water near land, it slows down, wavelengths decrease, waves grow in height, and currents intensify', to 'approximately 20 to 30 mph (30 to 50 km/h)'. What boats do: Oregon Department of Geology and Mineral Industries, Tsunami Awareness for Boaters, Fishermen and Mariners (page updated 28 May 2021; the national guidance behind it, from the National Tsunami Hazard Mitigation Program and the US Coast Guard, is a PDF that could not be read): for a distant tsunami, vessels on the water head to a staging area 'greater than 30 fathoms (180 ft)'; for a local one, beyond 100 fathoms only if shore can't be reached in 10 minutes, and at the dock you 'don't have time to save your boat and could die if you try to do so'; 'The first surge may not be the last or the largest'; dangerous harbour currents can last 60 hours or more: https://oregon.gov/dogami/tsuclearinghouse/Pages/maritime.aspx Still done today: The Maritime Executive, 29 Jul 2025, after the magnitude 8.8 earthquake off Kamchatka: the Coast Guard Captain of the Port Honolulu ordered 'all commercial vessels to evacuate all commercial harbors in the state', with harbours closed to incoming traffic: https://maritime-executive.com/article/major-earthquake-off-kamchatka-prompts-tsunami-warnings-around-the-pacific ; and US Coast Guard bulletin MSIB 25-005, 30 Jul 2025, reopening them. The hook's 'every ship' is every commercial vessel, in that order. For small boats the same Oregon page says the opposite of the hook in most distant tsunamis: stay docked and leave the evacuation zone, because sea and weather 'may be more dangerous than the tsunami'. That 'the same water is squeezed into less room' is our way of saying 'wavelengths decrease, waves grow in height'. The wave line on the horizon in the first picture is drawn for the viewer: in deep water there is nothing to see.
