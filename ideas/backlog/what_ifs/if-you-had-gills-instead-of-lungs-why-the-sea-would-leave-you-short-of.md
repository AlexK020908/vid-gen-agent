# If you had gills instead of lungs: why the sea would leave you short of air, then cold

## The first second

**Heard:** If you had gills instead of lungs, you might think you could live under the sea and never come up for air.

**Seen:** A person sitting cross-legged on a sunlit sandy sea floor with no mask, no tank and no bubbles; slits along each side of their neck open and close, and a fish drifts past at eye level.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** Someone sitting calmly on the sea floor with no mask and no bubbles, slits in their neck opening and closing as a fish goes by.
- **What grabs them:** The wish is never needing air again. It turns on you twice: the water has so little oxygen in it that you can't ever stop pumping it, and the gills that let the oxygen in let your body's heat out.
- **What's at stake:** Your own body: short of oxygen the moment you stop pumping water, and cooling toward the temperature of the sea.
- **The answer you watch:** At least a bathtub's worth of water streaming through your neck every four minutes with only a few oxygen dots in it; gill sheets slumping shut in air; your warm glow pouring out through the gills while the thermometer badge falls; the whale staying warm beside you.

## How it goes

**Wish:** Sitting on the sea floor for as long as you like, among the fish, with nothing on your back, the slits working calmly. Held about four seconds.

**Number:** A litre of water holds about 30 times less oxygen than a litre of air: about 7 mL against 209 mL. It drives how much has to be moved: a resting adult needs about 245 mL of oxygen a minute, so at least 35 litres of water a minute across the gills, against about 6 litres of air.

**Law:** How little oxygen dissolves in water, and heat crossing the same thin surface the oxygen crosses.

**Real thing:** One reason nearly every fish is close to the temperature of its water: blood that goes through gills gives up its heat there (the body wall loses heat too: in the 1976 study most of the fish's own heat left that way). The sea's fully warm animals, the whale, the seal, the penguin, are all air breathers, so their blood never passes through the water. The few fish that keep any warmth had to plumb their way round the gills: the opah, found in 2015 to be warm all through, has heat exchangers inside its gills, where warm blood leaving the body warms the cold blood coming back from them, and its muscles run about 5 C above the water (NOAA Fisheries; Wegner 2015).

**Sums:** Ours, to be tested apart from the sources: (1) 209 mL / 30 = 7 mL of oxygen per litre of water. (2) 3.5 mL/kg/min x 70 kg = 245 mL/min; 245 / 7 = 35 L/min of water; a 150 L bathtub in 4.3 minutes. (3) Air: 12 breaths x 0.5 L = 6 L/min, x 1.2 g/L = about 7 g. (4) Heat: 5.25 L/min x 1.05 kg/L = 5.5 kg/min of blood; x 3.5 kJ/kg/C x 22 C x 0.85 = about 360 kJ/min = 6.0 kW. (5) Resting heat: 1,680 kcal/day = 81 W. (6) 70 kg x 3.5 kJ/kg/C = 245 kJ/C; 360 / 245 = 1.5 C/min at the start, slowing as you cool.

**Unsure:** The 30 is for cool fresh water full of air; warm or salt water holds less, so the real figure is worse. 35 is the floor: real gills take out up to about 80% of the oxygen, and sea water at 15 C holds roughly a fifth less than fresh, so a real figure is nearer 45 to 65 litres a minute (the sea-water figure is an estimate from solubility figures). The cooling sum is a ceiling for the first minute: it takes a fish's plumbing (all the blood through the gills on every lap) and one fish's 80 to 90%. The same paper found that in that fish, already as cold as its water, 70 to 90% of the heat it made left through the body wall and fins, not the gills, so 'fish are cold because of their gills' is said less firmly here than textbooks say it. The ending keeps to two facts side by side and does not say whales have lungs because of heat: they have them by descent from land animals. Opened: Tyler (EBSCO), Hays (MSU Denver), NOAA Fisheries. Abstract only: Stevens and Sutterlin 1976, Wegner 2015. The 3.5 mL/kg/min is the convention for a resting adult, read on secondary pages.
