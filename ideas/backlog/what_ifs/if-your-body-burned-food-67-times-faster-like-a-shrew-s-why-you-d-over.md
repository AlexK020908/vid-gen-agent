- **What grabs them:** The wish is to eat anything and never gain a gram. It turns on you because all that food becomes heat, made all through a body as big as yours, with only your skin to get rid of it.
- **What's at stake:** Your own body: starving between meals six minutes apart, short of oxygen sitting still, and heating by about a degree a minute.

## How it goes

**Wish:** A table stacked with burgers, pizza and cake is eaten down plate by plate, while the bathroom scales under your chair never move. Held about four seconds.

**Number:** 67 times: gram for gram, a resting Etruscan shrew uses 67 times the oxygen a resting human does (Jurgens and others 1996, measured at 22 C). Applied to you, it drives three quantities: food from about 1,700 to about 113,000 kcal a day, heat from about 80 W to about 5.5 kW, and oxygen from about a quarter of a litre a minute to 16 to 19 litres. At 35 C, where it need not warm itself, the same paper gives about 100 mL of oxygen per kg a minute, some 25 to 29 times a human's (from a search summary of the paper's text): about two-thirds of the 67 is the shrew keeping warm.

**Law:** Surface against volume: a body makes heat all through its bulk and loses it only through its skin, which is one big reason each gram of a big animal burns slower.

**Real thing:** The Etruscan shrew, the smallest mammal: about 2 grams, a heart at 835 beats a minute, eating round the clock and unable to last more than a few hours without food unless it goes torpid. It has to burn like that, and can, because it is tiny: shaped like you but 35,000 times lighter, it has about 30 times more skin for every gram of body to let the heat out. The viewer leaves knowing why small animals live fast and big ones slow.

**Sums:** Ours, to be tested apart from the sources: (1) 1 kcal/kg/h x 70 kg x 24 h = 1,680 kcal/day; x 67 = 112,560 kcal; / 500 = 225 burgers; 1,440 minutes / 225 = 6.4 minutes each. (2) 1,680 kcal/day = 7.03 MJ / 86,400 s = 81 W; x 67 = 5.45 kW. (3) Oxygen: 67 x 3.5 mL/kg/min x 70 kg = 16.4 L/min; or the paper's 267 mL/kg/min x 70 kg = 18.7 L/min; against 7.4 L/min, 2.2 to 2.5 times. (4) 5.45 - 2.4 = 3.05 kW; / (70 kg x 3,500 J/kg/C) = 0.0125 C/s = 0.75 C/min; with 1 kW of sweat, 4.45 kW gives 1.1 C/min. (5) Skin for each gram: same shape, 70,000 g / 2 g = 35,000 times lighter; cube root of 35,000 = 32.7.

**Unsure:** The 67 was measured at 22 C, which is cool for a shrew, so part of it is the shrew keeping itself warm: it burns that fast because it loses heat that fast, which is the same law seen from the other side (that step is textbook reasoning, not from a page opened today). Jurgens 1996 was read as its abstract; the abstract gives no absolute resting figure; about 270 mL per kg a minute at 22 C and about 100 at 35 C are from search summaries of the paper's text and a later paper by the same authors, and 67 x the usual human 3.5 gives a little less, so the oxygen figure is a range. The shrew's food is a range, 1.5 to 6 times its weight a day, and 'a few hours' without food is from reports, not a paper I opened (Brecht 2011 was seen as a summary). The athlete's 7.4 L/min (96.7 mL per kg) is from the paper's abstract as summarised; higher values have been claimed without a paper. Sweat maximums are a textbook's (Guyton, via HyperPhysics); other sources give 2 to 3 litres an hour. The heat sum ignores the little heat lost by breathing and to the air. Near title of his that couldn't be read inside: 'What If Hummingbirds Were Jet-Sized' (0.8x), which runs the scale the other way and may make the same heat point about a bird.

**The ending:** The shrew close up on the table, calm, its heart flickering in x-ray at 835 beats a minute, beside your white-hot outline. Said: 'A shrew has to burn that fast: it is so small that its heat pours out through its skin. You're big, and your heat can't get out that fast. So you have to burn slow.'
