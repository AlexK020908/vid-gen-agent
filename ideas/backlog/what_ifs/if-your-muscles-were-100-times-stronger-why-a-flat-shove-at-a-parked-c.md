# If your muscles were 100 times stronger: why a flat shove at a parked car moves you, not the car

- **Kind:** what_ifs, built as `absurd_test`

## The first second

**Heard:** If your muscles were a hundred times stronger, you might think you could shove a parked car out of your way with one hand.

**Seen:** A person with one palm flat on the side of a parked car in an ordinary street; in x-ray their arm and leg muscles flare bright, and '100x' stamps on beside them.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** Someone with glowing muscles shoves a parked car, and it is their own shoes that skid backwards down the road while the car sits still.
- **What grabs them:** The wish is strength: shove the car, squash it, knock it flying. It turns on you because a push needs something to push from, and a flat shove has only 70 kg of you on two shoes behind it: the stronger the shove, the further it throws you instead.
- **What's at stake:** Your own body, thrown backwards by its own strength, and the job you wanted done: the car is still in your way.
- **The answer you watch:** Shoes skidding back and leaving rubber streaks while the car stays put; the grip badges, 70 kg against 1,500 kg; a handstand on the bonnet with the scales stuck at 70 kg; you flying backwards from your own shove; sand going under a locomotive's wheels as they bite.

## How it goes

**Wish:** One shove and the car skids sideways out of the parking space and across the road, tyres smoking, while you stand there with one arm out. Held about four seconds, as what you'd expect to happen.

**Number:** Muscles 100 times stronger. What it does not change: with a level push, the hardest you can shove anything is the grip of your shoes, about 1.0 x the weight on them on dry concrete, so about 690 N for a 70 kg person, whether your muscles are 1 times or 100 times as strong. A 1,500 kg car shoved sideways grips the same road about 21 times as hard. (Push upward as well, or lift, and the car's weight comes onto your shoes and the limit rises: that is the way out.)

**Law:** Friction can hold no more than a fixed share of the weight pressing down, and every push pushes back just as hard (Newton's third law).

**Real thing:** Why the machines that pull the heaviest loads are made heavy on purpose. A modern American freight locomotive, GE's ET44AC, weighs 426,000 lb, about 193 tonnes, and its listed starting pull is 183,000 lbf, under half its own weight (43% by our division; the page itself prints 35%, which does not match its weight figure): grip, not engine power, sets the limit. So it carries sand, 50 cubic feet of it on that model, and blows it under its driving wheels, and US rules require a locomotive to have working sanders 'that deposit sand on each rail in front of the first power operated wheel set' before it leaves its terminal (49 CFR 229.131).

**Sums:** Ours, to be tested apart from the sources: (1) 1.0 x 70 kg x 9.81 = 687 N of shoe grip. (2) 1.0 x 1,500 kg x 9.81 = 14,715 N for the car shoved sideways, when all four tyres must slide whatever the brakes; 14,715 / 687 = 21.4, which is just the two masses' ratio. From behind it would depend on the brakes: with only a handbrake on, the back wheels alone are locked: at a guessed 40% of the weight on them, about 5,900 N, still nine times your grip. (3) 426,000 lb x 0.4536 = 193,200 kg; 183,000 / 426,000 = 43%.
