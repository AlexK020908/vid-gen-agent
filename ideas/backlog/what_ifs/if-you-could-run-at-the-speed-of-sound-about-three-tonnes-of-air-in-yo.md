# If you could run at the speed of sound: about three tonnes of air in your way

- **Kind:** what_ifs, built as `absurd_test`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** If you could run at the speed of sound, 1,225 kilometres an hour, you might think you'd cross a whole city in one minute.

**Seen:** A runner on a long empty desert road, already a blur: a white cone of mist wraps round them, dust tears up behind, and a speed counter beside them spins past 300, 600 and 900 toward 1,225 km/h.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A runner tearing down an empty road inside a white cone of mist, with a speed counter spinning past anything a person could do.
- **What grabs them:** The wish is the speed. It turns on you because the air can't get out of your way that fast: the quicker you go, the harder and hotter it piles up on your front, by the square of the speed.
- **What's at stake:** Your own body, with the weight of two cars of air leaning on its front and that air about 58 degrees hotter than the day.
- **The answer you watch:** The dish of squeezed air growing on the chest with its weight badge going about 20 kg, about 300 kg, roughly 3 tonnes, and cars resting on it for size; the thermometer in it climbing to 73 C; the power counter climbing past eight million watts; then the suited figure falling faster still with only a small dish, a fortieth as strong.

## How it goes

**Wish:** The camera pulls back and up: the runner's trail crosses a whole city from one edge to the other while a clock in the corner counts sixty seconds. Held about four seconds.

**Number:** The speed of sound at sea level: about 1,225 km/h (761 mph, 340 m/s on a 15 C day). What it drives: the push of the air on anything moving through it grows with the square of the speed, so on a runner's front it is about 20 kg at 100 km/h and roughly 3 tonnes at 1,225.

**Law:** The push of moving air grows with the square of the speed (dynamic pressure), and air brought to a stop is squeezed and heated.

**Real thing:** Twice, people have fallen faster than sound with nothing but a suit. In 2012 a skydiver stepped off a balloon capsule 38,969 m up and reached 1,357.6 km/h, Mach 1.25, fifty seconds into the fall (Red Bull Stratos final data; the FAI holds the record), at about 27,800 m by reports of that data; in 2014 another fell from 41,422 m at about 1,322 km/h (FAI). By NASA's model the air at 27,800 m is about 50 times thinner than at the ground, so by our sum the wall of air there was about a fortieth of a runner's at sea level: about the faller's own weight, which is why he stopped speeding up.

**Sums:** Ours, to be tested apart from the sources: (0) NASA gives 761 mph; x 1.609 = 1,225 km/h; / 60 = 20 km a minute, the 'city in a minute'. (1) q at Mach 1, sea level: 0.7 x 101.3 kPa = 70.9 kPa = 7.2 tonnes-force per m2. (2) Drag = q x drag area: 70.9 kPa x 0.33 to 0.43 m2 = 23.4 to 30.5 kN = 2.4 to 3.1 t. (3) At 100 km/h (27.8 m/s): 0.5 x 1.225 x 27.8 x 27.8 = 473 Pa; x 0.43 = 203 N = 20.7 kg-force; at 400 km/h, 16 times that, 331 kg-force. (4) 288.15 K x 1.2 = 345.8 K = 72.6 C; 1.2 to the power 3.5 = 1.89. (5) 23.4 to 30.5 kN x 340.3 m/s = 8.0 to 10.4 MW; / 2,619.5 W = about 3,000 to 4,000. (6) Air at 27,833 m by NASA's model: T = -131.21 + 0.00299 x 27,833 = -48.0 C; p = 2.488 x (225.1 / 216.6) to the power -11.388 = 1.60 kPa; density = 1.60 / (0.2869 x 225.1) = 0.0248 kg/m3, against 1.225 at the ground: 49 times thinner. (7) His q: 0.5 x 0.0248 x 377.1 x 377.1 = 1,760 Pa, a fortieth of 70.9 kPa.

**Unsure:** The drag figure is a range and rests on slow-speed drag numbers: 3 tonnes is the top of what slow-speed drag figures give, and near the speed of sound drag is usually higher, so it is a round figure, not a floor. Pugh 1971's own text could not be opened; the coefficients are from a later paper's table. A vapour cone needs humid air; the first picture's setting should allow for that. The sprinter's peak power (2,619.5 W, 0.89 seconds into the race) was read on the paper's preprint by the fact-checker; that paper's drag coefficient is not used. **The ending:** A figure in a white pressure suit, visor down, steps off a small capsule with the curve of the Earth below and a black sky above, and falls; a counter passes 1,225 and stops at 1,357 km/h, and only a small dish of air builds on the chest, its badge reading about his own weight.
