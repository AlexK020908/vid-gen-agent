# If you turned completely invisible: why you'd be blind, and how see-through animals get found

- **Kind:** what_ifs, built as `absurd_test`

## The first second

**Heard:** If you turned completely invisible, you might think you could walk past anyone, anywhere, and never be seen.

**Seen:** A person facing a hallway mirror, turning to clear glass from the fingertips inward: within the first second the hands are gone, then the arms and the face, and a hoodie and jeans are left standing with nobody in them.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** Someone at a mirror turning to clear glass from the fingertips in, until only their clothes are standing there.
- **What grabs them:** The wish is that light goes straight through you, so nobody can see you. It turns on you because seeing is stopping light: the same light goes straight through your eyes, and the only way to get your sight back is to put two dark eyes back in the air for everyone to see.
- **What's at stake:** Your own sight. Fully invisible you'd be blind, and able to see you'd be found.
- **The answer you watch:** A chewed biscuit hanging in the air and sliding down inside nobody; beams of light crossing a glass eye without bending and leaving through the back of the head; two dark eyeballs hanging at head height, which shutting does nothing to hide; a glass squid with the same two dark eyes.

## How it goes

**Wish:** The empty hoodie stands right beside a seated guard, who looks straight through it; a biscuit lifts off his plate by itself. Held about four seconds.

**Number:** 100% of the light that reaches you passes straight through. What it drives: an eye sees only the light it stops, so the share of light your eyes catch falls to 0%. The count on the picture is 'light caught', falling from 100% to 0. (This is a weaker number than a scale idea's: nothing multiplies, as 4 becomes 64.)

**Law:** Optics: an eye has to bend light and then absorb it, and a thing that bends and absorbs light can be seen.

**Real thing:** The see-through animals of the open sea, the glass squid among them. Their bodies really are clear, but their eyes and their guts are not, so those are what a hunter looks for; many wrap them 'in iridescent mirrored bags', which in open water only reflect more water (Haddock and Johnsen 2025). It is probably also a large part of why see-through animals live in water and almost never on land: 'it's easier for tissues to match the properties of water than it is to match air alone' (Haddock and Johnsen 2025), and transparency is extremely rare on land (Johnsen 2001, abstract).

**Sums:** No arithmetic in this one. The steps that are ours, not the sources': (1) a cornea and lens that bend light no more than air can't focus; (2) a retina that passes light catches none; (3) clear eyelids can't shut light out. All three follow from the two quoted laws applied to a made-up case.

**Unsure:** Johnsen 2001: text read at https://archive.org/stream/biostor-11081/biostor-11081_djvu.txt (opened by the fact-checker); Johnsen's own 2025 piece with Haddock, which I did open, says the same thing in other words. The glass squid as the example is from a news report naming Johnsen, not from the paper. H. G. Wells made the eyelid point about his Invisible Man in 1897, as I remember it; not reopened, so it's not on the card as a source. The number is the weakest of the six. Whether this surprises: the 'invisible man would be blind' point is old among physics readers and may be new to most viewers; the owner will know better than I do. Near titles of his: none on invisibility of a person ('The Invisible Barrier', invisible ink, 'What Would You See With No Eyes?' are other subjects by title).

**The ending:** Dark open sea: a glass squid drifts past, its whole body as clear as a bag of water, except two dark eyes and a thin dark gut hanging in the middle. Said: 'The sea is full of animals that really are see-through. The ones that can see still cannot make their eyes clear: an eye that lets all the light through can't see.'
