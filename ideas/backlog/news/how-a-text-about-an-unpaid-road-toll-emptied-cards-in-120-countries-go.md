# How a text about an unpaid road toll emptied cards in 120 countries (Google's suit, Nov 2025)

- **Kind:** news, built as `why_it_happens`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** A text says you owe a small road toll. Google says texts like it caught over a million people.

**Seen:** A phone lying on a table lights up with a text: a little road-toll badge, a small amount due, a link. A thumb hovers over the link.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A text nearly every viewer has had themselves, and a thumb about to press the link. They stop because they know that text, and some of them pressed it.
- **What's at stake:** The viewer's own bank card. Google puts the cards taken in the United States alone at between 12.7 million and 115 million.

## How it goes

**Area:** a scam many people fell for

**Happened:** On 12 November 2025 Google sued the unnamed operators of a scam kit called Lighthouse, saying it had harmed over a million people in more than 120 countries with texts posing as road-toll and parcel services. The kit is rented out ready-made: the text, the fake payment page and the tools to collect what a victim types.

**When:** Google's lawsuit and its public post: 12 November 2025. Google told the trade press days later that the kit's operation had been shut down. I found no court ruling since, so the claims stand as Google's allegations in a case that, as far as I could find, is still open.

**Newcomer:** Phishing is a fake shop front: a page built to look like the toll agency's, with a till that belongs to a thief. A phishing kit is that fake shop sold flat-packed, sign and till included, rented by the month to people who couldn't build one. A one-time code is the second lock on your card: after the card number, your bank texts you a short code to prove it's you. Smishing is the same trick sent as a text instead of an email.

**Lesson:** The code your bank texts you is for you alone: a page that asks for it is the thief. No toll is paid through a link in a text. Look the agency up yourself, and forward the text to 7726.

**Said by:** Google alleges, in a civil lawsuit and a public post of 12 November 2025, that Lighthouse harmed 'over 1 million victims across more than 120 countries' and that its legal action 'is designed to dismantle the core infrastructure of this operation'; it calls those behind it 'foreign cybercriminals' and names no one (the defendants are listed as unknown). https://blog.google/outreach-initiatives/public-policy/legal-action-and-legislation-fight-scammers/ . These are one company's claims in a lawsuit, not a court's findings and not an agency's attribution, and the short says 'Google says'. Security firms tie the kit to a group in China; the short does not repeat that.

1. A text arrives saying a small toll is unpaid and a late fee is coming, with a link. The amount is small on purpose: nobody argues over a few dollars.
2. The link opens a page that looks like the toll agency's. Google says it found at least 107 ready-made page designs carrying its own branding alone.
3. The viewer types a name, an address and a card number. Each key pressed goes to the thief, not to any toll agency.
4. The bank spots a new payment and texts a one-time code. The fake page has a box waiting for it, and the victim types the code in. The bank's second lock has just been opened by its owner.
5. With card and code together the thieves can spend, and the same flat-packed shop is being run by many renters at once, in over 120 countries.

**The ending:** The phone face up with the text still on it, a thumb sliding it away to a bin marked 7726. Said: 'The code is the last lock. Never hand it to a link.'
