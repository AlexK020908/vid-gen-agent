# How $6.3 billion left Binance in twenty minutes without a single key being stolen (Oct 2026)

## The first second

**Heard:** Six billion dollars left the world's biggest crypto exchange in twenty minutes.

**Seen:** A bank safe the size of a room, door shut and locked, with glowing coins streaming out through its closed door into the dark, and a counter above it racing up past 6,000,000,000.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A locked safe with its door still shut, and coins pouring out of it anyway while a number climbs into the billions. Nothing needs reading: money is leaving a thing that is plainly closed, and a viewer stays to see how.
- **What's at stake:** The savings of the exchange's customers, sitting in its shared safes, and whether they would ever see them again. Binance says it will carry the loss itself, not its users.

## How it goes

**Area:** crypto theft (the owner's own example)

**Happened:** On 29 October 2026 about $6.34 billion in digital coins was moved out of Binance's own wallets across ten blockchains, most of it in about twenty minutes. Binance's initial report traces it to a fake job interview that put hidden software on an engineer's work computer weeks earlier, and the FBI says North Korea was responsible.

**When:** The theft: 29 October 2026, from 20:05 UTC. The FBI's alert: 31 October 2026. Binance's 'Initial Findings and Response Update': 3 November 2026. It is thirteen days old today, Binance calls its report initial, and an independent review is still to come.

**Newcomer:** A crypto exchange is a bank that holds its customers' coins in a few big shared safes. A hot wallet is the safe at the front counter, kept plugged in so withdrawals are quick. There is no single key to that safe: a machine in the back room (the signing service) turns the lock for any withdrawal slip that reaches it through the staff hatch. Malware is a copy of your door pass hidden inside a file you were asked to open. A deployment token is the staff pass that lets its holder wheel a new machine into the back room. A delegation (the real term is EIP-7702) is a standing order stuck on the safe itself saying 'this stranger may take from me too', so closing the counter does not cancel it: it has to be peeled off. A blockchain is a ledger anyone can read, which is why stolen coins can be followed. A freeze is the maker of a dollar-coin marking the stolen ones void.

**Lesson:** Nobody cracked the safe. Someone was offered a job. Binance's own report says the way in was a test file from a stranger run on a work computer, and that it has since rebuilt the wallet system and the internal AI tool and scanned every employee device. The loss fell on Binance, not on its customers.

**Said by:** The FBI, in an alert dated 31 October 2026 titled 'North Korea Responsible for $6 Billion+ Binance Attack': North Korea 'was responsible for the theft of more than $6 billion USD in virtual assets from cryptocurrency exchange, Binance, on or about October 29, 2026', and 'The FBI refers to this specific North Korean malicious cyber activity as "TraderTraitor."' https://www.fbi.gov/investigate/cyber/alerts/2026/north-korea-responsible-for-6-billion-binance-attack . Binance's report cites that FBI release and does not make the attribution itself. North Korea denied involvement in a statement carried by its state news agency on 3 November 2026. So the short says 'the FBI says a North Korean group did it, and North Korea denies it', never more. No engineer is named anywhere in the record, and none is in the short.

1. June: an engineer who looks after the wallets installs a tampered copy of a common developer tool. Binance catches it and swaps his laptop, but the intruders have already learned who works on the safes.
2. September: a recruiter who doesn't exist finds another engineer on a real job site, gives him two interviews, and sends a take-home test. He runs it on his work computer, and hidden inside is software that security tools miss.
3. 20 October: from that one computer, through an internal AI tool, the intruders reach two more engineers' computers, and on one they find the staff pass that lets new software be put on the wallet system.
4. 29 October: with that pass they place their own program next to the signing service and hand it withdrawal slips. The machine signs them. No key was stolen, because the machine that turns the lock was doing as it was asked.
5. From 20:05 UTC coins pour out of the front-counter safes on ten blockchains. An alarm for large transfers fires and Binance stops withdrawals, but a standing order planted on one safe keeps working until engineers remove it at 00:10.
6. Within a day the makers of two dollar-coins freeze close to $3 billion of the haul, and Binance refills the safes from its own reserves. On 31 October the FBI says North Korea was responsible. North Korea denies it.

**The ending:** The safe refilled and shut, the peeled-off standing order lying on the floor beside a laptop showing a job offer. Said: 'Nobody broke the safe. They applied for a job.'
