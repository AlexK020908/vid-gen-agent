## How it goes

**When where:** Beside the wreck of the destroyer Fred T. Berry, sunk on purpose the year before as a reef, lying on its side in 350 feet of water about 15 miles south-east of Key West, Florida, USA, 17 and 18 June 1973. The cut shows the surface ships, the wreck with its masts and cables, and the submersible Johnson Sea Link in two parts: a clear acrylic sphere in front and an aluminium divers' chamber behind.

**People:** Four, named with their ages in TIME's report of 2 July 1973. In the front bubble: Jock (Archibald Menzies, 30, the pilot) and Robert (Robert Meek, 27, a marine biologist). In the back chamber: Clayton (Clayton Link, 31) and Albert (Albert Stover, 51, a veteran diver). A colour each. On the ship above, Clayton's father Edwin Link, 68, who designed the submarine. The Coast Guard's own account names nobody.

**Worst moment:** Four men were stuck in one small submarine at the bottom of the sea. Two sat in a clear bubble at the front and two in a metal room behind it, and the metal room went as cold as the water, which weakened the chemical that cleaned their air.

**The turn:** Which room a man was in. The Coast Guard's board found that lives might have been saved if there had been some way of warming the back chamber, or if a television camera had reached the place on the first day. And in the front the pilot kept his own air clean with the chemical held in his shirt in front of a fan.

**Outcome:** Jock and Robert came out of the front unharmed after 31 hours. Clayton and Albert died of carbon dioxide poisoning; their chamber had to be brought down from pressure slowly before the hatch could be opened. The one official report holds two findings. The Coast Guard's board concluded the snagging came first from the pilot not making sure the water he was moving in was clear, and said the submarine's shape made it easy to snare. The safety board gave the probable cause as the snagging itself, with the back chamber's air-cleaning system and the lack of suitable rescue equipment contributing, and named nobody. The Coast Guard's board recommended a law on small submarines (saying where they dive, a set number of hours of air, a way to find them and to hook them) and a television unit that can be flown to a rescue. The Coast Guard's commandant agreed, and the safety board asked for the same camera. Edwin Link said, 'We're not going to stop.' What a viewer takes away: the air ran out because of the cold, and a rescue can turn on one tool being a day away. What changed: the boards' requests were recommendations, and no source opened shows any of them adopted. By Wikipedia's article on the accident, a weaker source than the rest, Edwin Link spent the following two years designing an unmanned Cabled Observation and Rescue Device that could free a trapped submersible; Florida Tech's Link collection holds an undated photograph titled 'CORD being taken out of the water'.
