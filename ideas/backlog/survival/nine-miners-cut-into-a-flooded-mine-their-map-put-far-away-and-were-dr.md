## How it goes

**When where:** Quecreek No. 1 coal mine, Pennsylvania, USA, from about 8:45 p.m. on Wednesday 24 July to 2:45 a.m. on Sunday 28 July 2002. The cut shows the surface, about 240 feet of rock, a coal seam about four feet high (48 to 50 inches by one miner's estimate at the hearing) sloping up to the section the crew was working, the old flooded Harrison No. 2 mine beside it, a 6.5-inch air hole and a rescue hole begun with a 30-inch bit and finished with a 26-inch one coming down from the top.

**People:** The nine men of one crew, all named in the federal report. Three carry the story and get a colour each: Randy (Randall Fogle, the foreman), Mark (Mark Popernack, running the mining machine when it broke through, then cut off alone, last man out) and Dennis (Dennis Hall, who phoned the warning). The other six (Thomas Foy, John Unger, John Phillippi, Ronald Hileman, Robert Pugh, Blaine Mayhugh) are a group in one colour. Nine more men in another part of the mine, who got out. A drill crew on the surface. No ages: the passages of the report and the hearing that I read give none.

**Worst moment:** Nine miners cut into an old flooded mine that their map said was still far away. The water shut every way out, so some of them tied themselves together in the dark, and they wrote notes to their families and sealed them in a bucket.

**The turn:** Two things done right in the first hours. Dennis kept phoning until the warning got through, and nine other men got out. And the rescuers' first hole was a small one, aimed at the part of the mine the crew had been working: it came through the roof beside them about eight hours after the flood, gave them air to breathe, and told each side the other was there. The federal report credits the warning to the other crew, the nine men's teamwork and the speed of the response.

**Outcome:** All nine came out alive after 76 to 78 hours, and the nine who were warned got out the first night. The federal investigators found the cause was an undated, uncertified map of the old mine that didn't show its last workings: no certified final map was in the state's map store, and a 1964 map that showed more of them turned up in a local coal museum. Three companies were cited for the inaccurate map (12 August 2003); in 2010 the review commission upheld the citation against PBS Coals and threw out the one against Musser Engineering. Pennsylvania's governor ordered mines to establish where old workings are within 500 feet, up from 200; the federal agency set up a task force on old mine maps, and the state proposed putting its old maps on computer. What a viewer takes away: an old map is a guess until someone has drilled ahead to check it; and if you're cut off, go high, stay together, and make a noise at regular times.

**Shown as:** Nobody dies in this one. At the worst moment the picture is nine simple figures in a line, a rope drawn between some of them, not all nine, in a tunnel too low to stand in, their lamps dimmed, a bucket beside them, and the water's edge with a measure on it: 70 ft. Fear is in how they sit, never in a face.

**The ending:** 2:45 on Sunday morning: the ninth man steps out of a steel capsule 21.5 inches wide, 78 hours after the water came in. Then the two maps side by side, the old mine where the paper put it and where it really was. Last line: Pennsylvania widened the zone in which a mine has to prove where old workings are, from 200 feet to 500.
