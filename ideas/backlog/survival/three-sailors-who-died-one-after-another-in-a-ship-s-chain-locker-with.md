## How it goes

**When where:** The chain locker of the rescue ship Viking Islay, standing by a gas rig in the North Sea about 25 miles off the East Yorkshire coast of England, at about 11 in the morning on Sunday 23 September 2007. The cut shows the bow: the store room on top, a square hatch in its floor held down by eighteen nuts, and under it the locker where the anchor chain lies, with the chain's pipe plugged with foam.

**People:** Three seamen, named with their ages in the official report: Robert Ebertowski, 40, who went in first; Robert O'Brien, 59, the leading hand, who stood at the hatch; and Finlay MacFadyen, 46, who was on watch on the bridge. Two share a first name, so on screen they are told apart by colour. Also the ship's master, and the seaman who went in afterwards wearing breathing apparatus.

**Worst moment:** Three sailors went down into the same small room, one after another, each to help the man before him, and none came out alive. Nothing in the room looked wrong: rust had used up the oxygen.

**The turn:** One thing would have saved all three: testing the air before anyone went in. The investigators found the crew never saw the locker as a dangerous space and that the ship had not been given the equipment to test it. The second man went in with no breathing gear. The third could not get down the hatch in a breathing set, and went in wearing an escape hood. Britain's safety regulator judged the oxygen was probably under 10 percent, and an expert's analysis found it could have been as low as 4.4; ordinary air is about 21.

**Outcome:** All three died from lack of oxygen. The steel and the chain had been rusting in a sealed space, and rusting uses oxygen up. The company that ran the ship put air testers and personal gas alarms on its ships and had itself audited from outside; the accident branch sent a safety flyer round the industry and asked for better training on what an escape hood is and isn't for; it counted 15 deaths in similar British-linked accidents since 1997. The master was tried at Sheffield Crown Court in 2009 and found not guilty. The company that ran the ship was fined £280,000, most of it for not providing an oxygen meter. Since 1 January 2015 the international ship safety rules have required crews to practise entering and rescuing from enclosed spaces at least every two months (not said to be because of this ship). What a viewer takes away: bad air gives no warning, and the instinct to go in after a friend is what kills the second and the third.
