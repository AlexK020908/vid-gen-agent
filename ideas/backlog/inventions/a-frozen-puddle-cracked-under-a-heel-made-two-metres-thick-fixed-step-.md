# A frozen puddle cracked under a heel, made two metres thick, fixed step by step into an icebreaker

- **Kind:** inventions, built as `you_invent`

## The first second

**Heard:** Stamp on a frozen puddle and it cracks. Now the ice is two metres thick, and a ship is stuck in it.

**Seen:** A boot heel coming down on a frozen puddle, the ice starring and breaking under it.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A boot cracks a frozen puddle, then the picture pulls back and the puddle is a white frozen sea with a ship held fast in the middle of it, tiny, nothing but ice to the horizon. A viewer sees the same job at a size no boot can do.
- **What grabs them:** A simple problem built up to an industrial fix. The everyday thing: cracking a frozen puddle with your heel. The twist: the ice is two metres thick and a ship is stuck in it. It ends on the icebreaker, which breaks ice the way the boot did, from above, with its own weight.
- **What's at stake:** The job is to cut a road of open water to a ship held in the ice and to the people waiting beyond it. On 17 January 2026 the US icebreaker Polar Star freed a cruise ship stuck in pack ice near McMurdo Station, and the tanker it led in brought the Antarctic base more than six million gallons of fuel for the year.
- **The answer you watch:** An ordinary bow stopping dead against the ice edge and the ice closing round the hull; the sloped bow sliding up onto the ice and the ice snapping downward under the weight; slabs piling up in front, then being pushed down and under the ice at the sides; water sloshing between side tanks as the ship rocks itself loose; the channel left behind with ships following in it.

## How it goes

**Everyday:** Stamping on a frozen puddle to crack the ice.

**Goal:** Break a path through thick sea ice to a ship that's stuck.

**Invention:** the icebreaker

**Seen it:** Working every winter in the Baltic, the Great Lakes, the Arctic and the Antarctic. The US Coast Guard's Polar Star, 13,500 tons and up to 75,000 horsepower, cut a seven-mile channel through fast ice to McMurdo Station in early 2026 (Marine Log, 15 March 2026).

**Cycle:** back, ram, ride up, crack

**Inside:** The ship and the ice cut in half lengthways, seen from the side: the bow climbing onto the ice sheet, a glow for the ship's weight pressing down, the slabs tipping and sliding under the hull; then a cut across the ship showing water moving between the two side tanks.

1. An ordinary ship's upright bow meets the ice edge-on, stops, and the ice closes round and holds it like a vise, so the bow is cut back into a long slope that pushes the ice down, where ice is weak: the sloping bow.
2. Thick ice won't bend, so the ship drives its bow right up onto the ice and its own weight breaks it from above, and it backs off several ship-lengths and rams again: riding up.
3. Ice that hard would tear the hull open, so the bow is plated in steel up to 50 millimetres thick: the ice-strengthened hull.
4. The broken slabs pile up in front and slow the ship more than the breaking does, so the hull is shaped to shove them down and under the ice at the sides, and is wider at the bow than the stern: the icebreaking hull form.
5. The ice still grips the ship along its sides, so water is pumped from tanks on one side to the other to keep it rolling, and air is bubbled out along the hull: the rolling tanks and the bubbler.

**The ending:** The icebreaker backing off, charging, riding up and dropping through the ice, the channel opening behind it and the stuck ship sailing out along it: "Congratulations, you just built an icebreaker."

## To settle before it's written

- Step 1: 'An ordinary ship's upright bow has to crush the ice edge-on and hasn't the power, so the bow is cut back into a long slope that bends the ice downward, where ice is weak: the sloping bow.' (The vise is floes pushed by wind and tide, a separate thing.)
- Step 4: only some icebreakers are wider at the bow than the stern: say 'some'.
- Step 5: rolling tanks and bubblers are on some icebreakers (Oden), and the Polar Star had its tanks removed as ineffective: say 'on some icebreakers', and don't draw them on the Polar Star.
- Stake: 'On 17 January 2026 the US Coast Guard says its icebreaker Polar Star broke the cruise ship Scenic Eclipse II free of pack ice near McMurdo Station (the cruise line says it was slowed, not in need of rescue), and the tanker it led in delivered more than six million gallons of fuel to the base.'
- The hook's 'two metres' holds as a round figure.

## Where it's known from

Wikipedia, Icebreaker: 'The bending strength of sea ice is low enough that the ice breaks usually without noticeable change in the vessel's trim'; 'In cases of very thick ice, an icebreaker can drive its bow onto the ice to break it under the weight of the ship'; it 'may even have to repeatedly back down several ship lengths and ram the ice pack at full power'; icebreaking ships have 'a sloping or rounded stem as well as sloping sides'; 'Nipping occurs when ice floes around a ship are pushed against the ship, trapping it as if in a vise'; 'A buildup of broken ice in front of a ship can slow it down much more than the breaking of the ice itself', so the hull is designed 'to direct the broken ice around or under the vessel', and some have 'a hull that is wider in the bow than in the stern'; bow plating 'can be up to 50 millimetres (2.0 in) thick in older polar icebreakers'; 'air bubbling systems are used to reduce friction'; 'Pumping water between tanks on both sides of the vessel results in continuous rolling that reduces friction'. In use today: Marine Log, 15 March 2026, on Polar Star's Operation Deep Freeze 2026: a seven-mile channel through fast ice, the cruise ship Scenic Eclipse II freed on 17 January 2026 in a six-hour job, the tanker Stena Polaris escorted in with more than 6 million gallons of fuel, the ship 13,500 tons with up to 75,000 horsepower, commissioned 1976. OUR OWN REASONING, not a source's: the order of the steps; that an upright bow 'stops' at the ice edge (the article says only that ordinary ships are 'not powerful enough' and get immobilised); and likening riding up to a boot stamping from above. Note the article says riding up is for very thick ice: in thinner ice the sloping bow just bends it down. 'Two metres' in the hook is a round figure of ours; the Coast Guard's own release, which reports the ice thickness, refused our request (403), and news reports of it gave up to eight feet.
