## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A tin opener takes the lid off a tin, then the tin becomes a crumpled car with its doors jammed shut and a person sitting inside, awake, looking out through the glass. A viewer sees a box that has to be opened and someone waiting in it.
- **What grabs them:** A simple problem built up to an industrial fix. The everyday thing: opening a tin. The twist: the tin is a crashed car with someone stuck inside. It ends on the hydraulic rescue tool every fire crew carries, the Jaws of Life.
- **What's at stake:** The job is to open a steel car around a person without hurting them, and quickly. The tool's inventor is said to have thought of it after watching it take over an hour to get an injured driver out of a race car; one fire department reported the time falling from an hour and a half to seven minutes.
- **The answer you watch:** A crowbar slipping and the car rocking; a saw throwing sparks beside a fuel leak; oil pushed down a hose into a cylinder and the piston sliding out; two arms forcing a jammed door open; two curved blades closing through a roof pillar; a ram pushing between two points; the roof lifted off and the person helped out.

## How it goes

**Everyday:** Opening a tin with a tin opener.

**Goal:** Open a crashed car around the person stuck inside, fast, without hurting them.

**Invention:** the Jaws of Life

**Seen it:** On every fire engine: firefighters carry it to the car at a serious crash, and it's named in crash reports every week. Durham, Maine's crew used it to free a car's only occupant on 3 October 2025.

**Cycle:** pump, push, spread, cut

**Inside:** The tool made glass: oil as liquid pushed into the cylinder, the piston sliding, the linkage opening the arms or closing the blades; a glow at the tips for the force.

1. The door is jammed, and when you lever at it with a crowbar the whole car rocks and shifts onto the person, so the force has to come from a machine that holds still.
2. A circular saw throws sparks that could start a fire, screams beside their head and takes over an hour, so nothing spins: a pump pushes oil down a hose into a cylinder, and the piston slides out with tonnes of force: the hydraulic cylinder.
3. The piston drives two steel arms apart, their tips pushed into the door gap, and the door is forced open: the spreader.
4. The roof pillars are in the way, so the same piston closes two curved blades through them, and the roof comes off in about two minutes: the cutter.
5. The crushed front is pressed in on the person's legs, so a piston on its own is braced across the gap and pushes it away: the ram.
6. The first one weighed over 200 pounds with its petrol engine and hoses, so the pump and a battery now sit inside the tool itself: the battery rescue tool.

**The ending:** The roof lifted clear, the door on the ground, the person helped out of the seat, and the firefighter setting the tool down, its arms closing: "Congratulations, you just built the Jaws of Life."

## Where it's known from

Wikipedia, Hydraulic rescue tools: George Hurst conceived it 'circa 1961' after a stock car crash where 'it took workers over an hour to remove an injured driver from his car'; before it, 'Saws can create sparks that could start a fire, create loud sounds, stress the victim(s), and often cut slowly', and with crowbars and Halligan bars 'this could compromise the stability of the vehicle, or injure the victims further'; the spreader 'uses hydraulic pressure to separate or spread the arms'; the cutter is 'a pair of hydraulically powered shears'; the ram 'consists of a piston or telescoping cylinder that extends under hydraulic pressure'; blades 'spread or cut with a force of several tons', tips 'spreading up to a meter'; fluid pressure 'up to 720 bars'; 'It takes about two minutes to take the roof off a car'; battery tools have 'a small electrical pump in the tool'. The maker's own history (jawsoflife.com, 'The Evolution of the Extrication Tool'): first shown at the 1971 SEMA show as the HURST Power Tool; the JL-32, a 32-inch spreader with a two-cycle petrol engine, weighed over 200 pounds and was first used in rescues in 1972; first cutter 1977, first ram 1978, battery eDRAULIC line 2010; one department reported extrication falling 'from an hour and a half to just seven minutes'. In use today: 1st Responder News, 3 October 2025, Durham, Maine. DATES DISAGREE: Wikipedia says Hurst had the idea about 1961 and that it was first used in 1963; the maker says he watched the crash in 1971. The card gives no year for that reason. OUR OWN REASONING, not a source's: the order of the steps; that a machine 'holds still' where a crowbar rocks the car; and that the ram is used to push a crushed front off someone's legs (standard practice as far as we know, but the sources read say only that rams 'apply directed force much farther than the arms of a spreader'). 'Jaws of Life' is Hurst's registered brand, used loosely for all such tools. His 'How A Hydraulic Press Works' covers the same push of oil on a piston, not this tool.
