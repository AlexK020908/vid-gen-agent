# Dough under a rolling pin, made twenty tonnes of glowing steel: the hot strip mill

- **Kind:** inventions, built as `you_invent`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** Roll out dough with a rolling pin. Now the dough is twenty tonnes of glowing steel.

**Seen:** Two hands pushing a rolling pin over a lump of dough, flattening it.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A rolling pin flattens dough, then the dough is a slab of steel the size of a bus, glowing orange, with the same rolling pin lying on top of it starting to smoke. A viewer sees the job and sees that the tool in their hands can't do it.
- **What grabs them:** A simple problem built up to an industrial fix. The everyday thing: rolling out dough. The twist: the dough is twenty tonnes of steel at over a thousand degrees. It ends on the hot strip mill, the machine that rolls the sheet steel for car bodies.
- **What's at stake:** The job is to roll the slab as thin as a car door before it cools: steel that cools too far has to go back in the furnace. And everything nearby depends on the stands staying in step, because a strip with nowhere to go loops into the air at over a thousand degrees.
- **The answer you watch:** The slab dragged into the gap between two turning rolls and coming out thinner and longer; going back and forth, thinner each pass; water jets blasting black crust off in clouds of steam; a thin roll bowing under the load until a huge roll is set behind it; one strip running through seven stands at once, each turning faster; the strip looping up when one stand falls out of step; the coiler winding it up.

## How it goes

**Everyday:** Rolling out dough with a rolling pin.

**Goal:** Roll a twenty-tonne slab of glowing steel as thin as a car door before it cools.

**Invention:** the hot strip mill

**Seen it:** Its work is everywhere: car bodies, fridges, tins, and the big steel coils on lorries and goods trains. The mill itself is inside a steelworks, so most people have only seen clips of it. A new one at Hoa Phat's Dung Quat works in Vietnam, with a seven-stand finishing mill, passed final acceptance in 2026.

**Cycle:** grip, squeeze, stretch, speed up, coil

**Inside:** The line seen from the side with each stand cut in half: the two small work rolls and the two big backup rolls, the glowing strip thinning stand by stand, a glow for the squeeze, speed ticks rising along the line.

1. You can't push a pin across twenty tonnes of steel, so the pin stays where it is and turns, with a second one under it, and the slab is dragged through the gap between them: the roll stand.
2. Ask for too big a squeeze and the rolls just slip on the steel, so it's taken down a little at a time, back and forth five or seven times: the reversing roughing mill.
3. A black crust forms on the hot steel, so water jets blast it off in a burst of steam before the rolls can press it in: the descaler.
4. Under that load the rolls bow, leaving the sheet thick in the middle, so the thin rolls that touch the steel get huge rolls pressed behind them: the backup rolls.
5. Now it's long, thin and cooling fast, so there's no more back and forth: seven stands in a row squeeze the same strip at once, each turning faster than the last because the strip keeps getting longer: the finishing mill.
6. If one stand falls out of step the strip has nowhere to go and loops into the air, so an arm between each pair of stands keeps the strip tight: the loopers, and a drum at the end winds it up: the coiler.

**The ending:** The strip running through seven stands at once, leaving the last at seventy kilometres an hour, thin as a car door, and winding into a coil: "Congratulations, you just built a hot strip mill."

## To settle before it's written

- FOR YOU TO JUDGE: the weakest stake of the five. Nobody and nothing alive is at risk, only the strip cooling or looping, and most people have never seen the machine.
- Ending: '...leaving the last at up to seventy kilometres an hour, a couple of millimetres thick, and winding into a coil' (not 'thin as a car door': car body sheet is rolled thinner again, cold).
- It's 'the mill that rolls the steel coil car bodies start from', and the slab is 'as long as a bus' (it's only knee-high).
- Step 6: 'If one stand falls out of step the strip gathers into a loop, so an arm between each pair of stands takes up the slack and holds it tight: the loopers, and a drum at the end winds it up: the coiler.'
- Seven finishing stands is one mill's count: say 'up to seven'.

## Where it's known from

Wikipedia, Rolling (metalworking): 'The friction at the interface between the material and the rolls causes the material to be pushed through'; 'if the change in thickness is too great the rolls just slip over the material and do not draw it in'; stock that cools too far 'must be re-heated prior to additional hot rolling'; roll 'deflection causes the workpiece to be thinner on the edges and thicker in the middle'; backup rolls 'provide rigid support required by the working rolls to prevent bending under the rolling load'. IspatGuru, 'Rolling of steel in Hot Strip Mill' (Satyendra, 3 December 2015): slabs heated to 1100 to 1250 deg C; primary descaling water at up to 400 kg per sq cm; the slab rolled reversibly '5 or 7 times' to a bar of about 30 mm; five to seven finishing stands in tandem; hydraulic loopers between stands to control tension; top speeds 'as high as 20 m/sec' (72 km/h); a coiler at the end. The same site: as the steel gets thinner 'it also becomes longer and moves through the rolls faster'. Wikipedia, Strip mill: the first was built at Ashland, Kentucky, in 1923, replacing pack mills where plates were passed over the rolls by hand. Cobbles: mechanical-engineering.com, 6 October 2017 (a trade blog, the weakest source here): the steel 'will flick into the air creating enormous loops', at material temperatures 'well over 1000°C'. In use today: Machine Maker, 26 June 2026: Primetals issued final acceptance for Hoa Phat's Dung Quat hot strip mill, with a seven-stand finishing mill, three coilers, coils up to 36 tonnes and strip from 1.2 to 25.4 mm. OUR OWN REASONING, not a source's: the order of the steps; 'twenty tonnes' as a round figure (the only weight read is coils up to 36 tonnes); that scale left on would be pressed into the surface (the source says only that descaling matters for surface quality); and that loopers are what prevent a cobble (the sources say loopers hold tension, and that cobbles come from a roll fault or the strip leaving its path). No source read here says anyone was hurt.
