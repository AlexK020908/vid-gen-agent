## How it goes

**Kind:** invented

**Strange:** Nobody's been home for a week, every tap in the house is off, and water is pouring through the kitchen ceiling.

**Answer:** Inside the pipe, with the water as moving balls: ice forms at the coldest spot and seals the pipe like a cork. As more water freezes and grows, it pushes on the water trapped between the ice and the closed tap, which has nowhere to go. The pressure climbs to thousands of pounds per square inch, and the pipe bulges and splits at its weakest point, where there's no ice. When the house warms, the cork melts and the water from the mains pours out of the split.

**Solved:** invented

**People:** The homeowner, back from a week away, who reacts. Nobody's named.

**Shown as:** nothing hurtful

1. Every tap is shut and every sink is dry: it isn't a tap left running.
2. The oven clock is blinking 00:00, and the kitchen's max-min thermometer has its low marker at -5°C: the power went off while they were away, the heating with it, and the house froze.
3. In the loft a bottle of water has split open, with a puddle under it: water grows when it freezes.
4. Where the pipe passes the outside wall there's a gap in the loft insulation and a cold draught; the pipe there is whole, but the split is a metre further on, in the stretch that ends at a closed tap: the pipe didn't burst where it was coldest.
5. The puddle under the ceiling is an hour or two's worth, not a week's: the water only began once the power came back and the loft thawed, and until then the ice plug held it in (that the water waits for the thaw is our reasoning, not the study's).

**The ending:** That's why a dripping tap saves a pipe in a freeze: not because moving water can't freeze, but because an open tap gives the squeezed water somewhere to go.

## Where it's known from

Jeffrey R. Gordon, 'An Investigation into Freezing and Bursting Water Pipes in Residential Construction', Building Research Council, University of Illinois, 1996: a pipe bursts in the sealed stretch between an ice blockage and a closed tap, usually where little or no ice has formed, at pressures of thousands of psi; a pipe open at one end can't be pressurized, so a slightly open tap prevents the burst. University of Illinois News Bureau, 4 Sept 1996 (Gordon: a pipe usually will burst in a place that has almost no ice in it; more than 4,000 psi in copper), https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1012932; the dripping tap: IBHS, Freezing and Bursting Pipes (water can freeze even with a slow flow; an open faucet relieves the pressure between it and the ice blockage)
