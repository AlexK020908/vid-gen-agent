## How it goes

**Kind:** real

**Strange:** A horse in Berlin answers sums by tapping his hoof, right almost every time, and a panel of thirteen men, a circus manager among them, can find no trick.

**Answer:** In slow motion, with a glow on the man: he bends forward to watch the hoof, and the horse starts tapping; the count reaches the answer, the man relaxes, his head lifts a hair, and the horse stops. Nobody was cheating, and the man had no idea he was doing it.

**Solved:** Solved by the psychologist Oskar Pfungst, in tests he published in 1907.

**People:** The horse's owner (named in the record: Wilhelm von Osten), a man asking the questions, and the psychologist who tested them (Oskar Pfungst). The horse is Clever Hans.

**Shown as:** Nothing hurtful. The owner is never shown as a cheat: the record says nobody gave the signs on purpose.

1. With his owner away from the yard, other people ask him and he still gets answers right: his owner isn't signalling him on purpose.
2. A number card is shown to the horse but not to the man asking: now he's right about 8 times in 100 instead of 98. He only knows what the person asking knows.
3. Big blinkers hide the man asking: right about 6 times in 100, against 89 when he can see him. He's watching the person, not the board.
4. Watch the man, not the horse: once he's set the sum he bends forward a little, and the horse starts tapping; at the right number his head gives a tiny jerk upward, and the horse stops a moment later.

**The ending:** Pfungst then played the horse himself with 25 people, and all but two of them gave him the same tiny head movements, almost none of them aware of it.

## Where it's known from

Oskar Pfungst, 'Clever Hans (The Horse of Mr. von Osten)', 1907, in Carl L. Rahn's English translation of 1911: the commission of thirteen, the tests without knowledge (about 8% right against 98%) and with blinkers (6% against 89%), the forward bend and upward jerk of the head, and the 25 people he tested himself
