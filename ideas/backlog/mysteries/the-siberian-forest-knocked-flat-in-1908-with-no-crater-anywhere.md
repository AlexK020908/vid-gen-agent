## How it goes

**Kind:** real

**Strange:** A whole forest is knocked flat around one spot, every tree pointing away from it like spokes, and at the centre there's no crater and no rock.

**Answer:** A space rock streaks in, glowing, and bursts in the air kilometres above the forest (estimates run from about 5 to 15 km; NASA's 2019 study says 10 to 14). The flash of heat comes first, then the blast: straight down at the centre, snapping off the branches before it can push the trunks over, and outward all round, laying every tree flat, pointing away. The rock is all but used up in the burst, so there's no crater.

**Solved:** Best theory: a space rock that burst in the air above the forest (NASA, 2008, with JPL's Don Yeomans). Still argued: whether it was a stony asteroid or a piece of a comet, and how big; NASA says some still debate the cause.

**People:** A man sitting outside the trading post at Vanavara, 65 km away (named in the record as S. B. Semenov, who described it in a letter), and the scientist who first reached the site (Leonid Kulik, 1927).

**Shown as:** Nothing hurtful shown: the man at the trading post is knocked off his seat and gets up. The herders and reindeer who were nearer are left out, and the short never claims nobody was hurt.

1. From above, every fallen trunk points away from one spot: a storm lays a forest down one way, so this blow came from a single point (the storm comparison is ours).
2. At that spot the trees are still standing, stripped of branches and bark like telephone poles: the blast came down on them from above, so fast it snapped the branches off before it could push the trunks over.
3. No crater and no big rock anywhere: whatever did it never reached the ground.
4. 65 km to the south, a man sitting outside a trading post is knocked off his seat, tugging at his shirt from the heat: a flash of heat came first, then a blast, strong enough to reach that far.

**The ending:** NASA's latest estimate is 10 to 30 megatons, roughly 700 to 2,000 Hiroshima bombs, and a blast that size comes about once in a thousand years or more, on average, not every few centuries as was thought.

## Where it's known from

NASA Science, 'The Tunguska Impact, 100 Years Later' (30 June 2008, with JPL's Don Yeomans; the release's text at https://www.spaceflightnow.com/news/n0806/29tunguska/): 80 million trees in a radial pattern, upright stripped trees at ground zero, no crater, the man knocked from his chair at Vanavara; Semenov's own account, in a letter of 1930; NASA Ames, Tunguska Revisited (26 June 2019): 10 to 30 megatons, 6 to 9 miles up, intervals on the order of millennia; the fast-shock explanation of the standing trees is NASA's (2008), the from-above part is the usual account (Wikipedia, uncited)
