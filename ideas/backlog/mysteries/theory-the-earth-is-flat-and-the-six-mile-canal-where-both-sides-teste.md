# Theory: the Earth is flat, and the six-mile canal where both sides tested it

- **Kind:** mysteries, built as `mystery`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** Three equal poles along a straight canal, and the middle one stands higher.

**Seen:** A dead-straight canal running to the horizon, three poles of the same height standing in it in a row, seen through a telescope: the middle pole's top sits clearly above the other two.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** Three matching poles in still water with the middle one sticking up above the line of the other two: it reads as wrong at a glance, like a shelf that isn't level.
- **What grabs them:** It looks like a mistake: someone has measured three poles the same and one is plainly taller. A stranger would blame the poles, and it's the water that's bent.
- **What's at stake:** A bet of 500 pounds a side in 1870, about a labourer's wages for ten years, and for the man who won it, fifteen years of threats.
- **The answer you watch:** A boat's flag that should sink out of sight and doesn't, three poles and a telescope, a hump of water drawn between them, and a ray of light bending over cold water: each claim is watched.

## How it goes

**Kind:** real

**Strange:** Three markers stand at exactly the same height above the water of a canal that runs straight for six miles. Sight from the first to the last, and the middle one is about five feet too high.

**Answer:** The theory is told as a theory: some believe the Earth is a flat disc, and the modern belief starts on this canal in 1838. Both sides' tests are staged on the same six miles. Low to the water, the far flag stays in sight, which is the evidence for. Thirteen feet up, three equal marks show a hump of about five feet, which is the evidence against, and it's been repeated since. The low-down result is what bent light over cold water gives, and it changes with the weather, where the hump doesn't.

**Solved:** Solved: the record supports a curved surface. Best primary evidence against the theory on this canal: Alfred Russel Wallace's own account of the 1870 test in The Field (April 1870), and the referee's award. What remains: the low-level sightings of 1838 and 1904 were real observations, explained by the bending of light near the water, which wasn't measured on those days.

**People:** A man with a telescope standing in the canal (named in the record: Samuel Rowbotham, who wrote as 'Parallax'); a surveyor with a telescope on a bridge (Alfred Russel Wallace); the man who lost the bet (John Hampden, whom a court convicted of libelling Wallace).

**Shown as:** Nothing hurtful. Believers aren't mocked: the first test is shown as an honest observation with a real cause. The threats are told in a line, not shown.

1. Where the belief started: in 1838 a man stands in this canal with a telescope eight inches above the water and watches a boat row away for six miles. On a globe its flag should drop about eleven feet below his view. He says he sees it all the way, and writes a book: the water is flat.
2. 1870: a believer bets 500 pounds that nobody can show water curving. A surveyor takes the bet and sets three marks at the same height above the water, far higher up than eight inches, at the two ends and the middle of the six miles.
3. Through the telescope the middle mark stands about five and a half feet above the line between the end ones: the water between them humps up in the middle, as a globe's would. The referee awards him the bet.
4. Why the first man saw the flag: just above cold water the air is cooler than the air over it, and light skimming it bends downward, round the hump. Raise the telescope clear of that layer and the hump appears. In 1904 a believer photographed a sheet six miles off from two feet above the water, which the same bending explains.

**The ending:** The loser never paid up in peace: he hounded the winner for years and went to prison for it. The canal is still there, the Old Bedford River in Norfolk, six straight miles, and the test costs three poles and a telescope.

## To settle before it's written

- The hook's picture is the wrong test. Three equal poles is the 1901 repeat (middle pole about 6 feet high). The 1870 bet used 'a telescope on one bridge, a disc on a pole three miles off, a black band on a bridge six miles off, all the same height above the water, and the disc sits about five feet above the line.' Date the hook to 1901 or redraw it.
- 'The umpire, the editor of The Field, awards him the bet' (each side had its own referee, and they disagreed).
- Ending: 'The winner never kept the money: a court made him hand it back, and the loser hounded him for years and went to prison for it.'
- Stake: 'A bet of 500 pounds a side in 1870, and for the man who won it, in his own words, fifteen years of persecution.' Cut 'a labourer's wages for ten years': no source.
- Use 'about five feet' throughout, and 'the hump is there every time'. He was 'a naturalist trained as a surveyor'.
- The researcher's own caution: the stake is thin (a bet).
