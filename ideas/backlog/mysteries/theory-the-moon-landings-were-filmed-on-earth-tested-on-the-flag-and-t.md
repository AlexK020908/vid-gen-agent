# Theory: the Moon landings were filmed on Earth, tested on the flag and the shadows

- **Kind:** mysteries, built as `mystery`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** A flag swings on the Moon, where there's no air to move it.

**Seen:** A flag on a pole in grey dust under a black sky, swinging from side to side, a spacesuit glove just letting go of the pole.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A flag swinging under a black sky on grey dust: everyone knows there's no wind on the Moon, so the picture asks its own question.
- **What grabs them:** It looks like a mistake in the film itself: a flag that waves where nothing can wave. Some believe that's the slip that gives a film set away, and the viewer stays to see whether it does.
- **What's at stake:** Whether twelve people really walked on another world, or nobody ever has: the biggest thing people say they've done.
- **The answer you watch:** A rod in the flag's top hem, a flag swung in air beside one swung with no air, a camera opened wide and shut down, two poles on bumpy ground, and photos taken from orbit: every test is a thing watched.

## How it goes

**Kind:** real

**Strange:** In the film from the Moon, a flag swings as if in a breeze, in a place with no air. The sky behind it is black with no stars, and the shadows on the ground don't run the same way.

**Answer:** The theory is told as a theory: some believe the six landings of 1969 to 1972 were filmed in a studio, a belief that spread from a pamphlet of 1976, and its three best-known proofs are a waving flag, a starless sky and crooked shadows. Each is staged both ways, as a studio would give it and as an airless, sunlit Moon would. A breeze would stop the flag quickly; lamps would give each pole several shadows; a studio sky could have had stars painted on. What the film shows is what one sun and no air give. Then the orbit photos: the hardware and the tracks are still where the film says they were left.

**Solved:** Solved, as firmly as anything is: the record supports the landings. Best primary evidence against the theory: the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera's photos of all six sites (2009 on; flags, Mark Robinson, 27 July 2012). Nothing in the three claims remains unexplained; whether the flags still have any colour after decades of sunlight is not known.

**People:** An astronaut in a suit with a mirrored visor, so no face is shown, twisting a flagpole; nobody is named on screen. The theory is put as what 'some believe', never as a charge against anyone.

**Shown as:** Nothing hurtful. The theory accuses no person: the short tests three claims about a film and says what the photos show.

1. The theory's first proof, the flag. Look at its top edge: a rod runs through the hem to hold it out, because with no air it would hang limp, and the creases are from being folded in a tube for the trip. It only moves when the pole is handled.
2. Twist the same pole in air and the flag stops in a second or two, since the air brakes it. Twist it with no air and it keeps swinging far longer: the long swing is what no air looks like, not what wind looks like. (The side-by-side test is from a TV experiment in a vacuum chamber, not a NASA paper.)
3. The second proof, no stars. Set a camera for bright sunlit ground and the faint stars don't register; open it up for the stars and the ground burns white. It's daytime there, and the same happens in any photo of a floodlit pitch at night.
4. The third proof, shadows at different angles, said to mean several studio lamps. Stand two poles on bumpy ground under one sun and photograph them from low down: the shadows splay apart on the picture. Several lamps would give each pole several shadows, and each has one.
5. From orbit: since 2009 a NASA camera circling the Moon has photographed the landing sites. The bottom halves of the landers, the tracks of boots and wheels, and at five of the six sites the flag's own shadow, swinging round the pole through the lunar day.

**The ending:** At the first landing site the flag casts no shadow. One of the two men who planted it said at the time that the rocket blast knocked it flat as they took off, and the photos from orbit, forty years on, agree with him.
