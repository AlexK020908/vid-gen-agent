# Unsolved: where Amelia Earhart's plane came down in 1937

- **Kind:** mysteries, built as `mystery`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** A silver plane, twenty hours of fuel gone, and the island isn't there.

**Seen:** A silver twin-engined plane low over an empty ocean, its fuel needle on empty in the corner, and nothing on the water in any direction.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A plane over bare sea with a fuel needle on empty: anyone sees two people about to run out of sky, before a word is said.
- **What grabs them:** It looks like a mistake: a famous pilot and a ship that can hear each other and can't find each other. A stranger would expect wreckage, and in nearly ninety years of looking there's been none.
- **What's at stake:** Two people, Amelia Earhart and her navigator Fred Noonan, and the one thing that would say how they died: the plane.
- **The answer you watch:** A line ruled on a chart, a fuel gauge, a sea floor swept by sonar, a tape measure on arm and leg bones, a shiny shape in a lagoon: each explanation is tested on those.

## How it goes

**Kind:** real

**Strange:** A plane with fuel for about twenty hours is twenty hours out, looking for an island two kilometres long and three metres high. A ship is waiting there, hears her on the radio close by, and never sees her.

**Answer:** Two explanations, each staged from the same last minute. One, the US government's since 1937: the tanks run dry near Howland, she puts the plane down on the sea, and it sinks five kilometres. That fits the fuel and the radio calls heard close by; against it, the swept sea floor is empty. Two: she turns south-east down the line, lands on Nikumaroro's flat reef at low tide, and the two live for a while as castaways while the surf takes the plane. That fits the line and, perhaps, the bones; against it, Navy planes flew over the island a week later and saw no aircraft, and many expeditions since have not found one piece that is certainly hers.

**Solved:** Unsolved. The official finding (1937) is that the plane ran out of fuel and came down in the sea near Howland Island; she was declared dead in 1939. Still argued: crashed and sank, or landed on Nikumaroro. The 2018 bone study (Jantz) is disputed, since it rests on seven measurements from 1941 and on her size judged from photos.

**People:** A pilot and a navigator, seen only as a plane from outside (named in the record: Amelia Earhart, Fred Noonan); a radio operator on the waiting ship; a doctor with a measuring tape (D. W. Hoodless, 1941).

**Shown as:** Nothing hurtful shown. The plane is seen flying, never crashing: each explanation ends on the sea closing over an empty picture, or on an empty reef. The bones are shown as a card of seven measurements and a tape, never as remains.

1. The fuel: about 1,100 gallons, enough for roughly twenty hours, and her last call comes about twenty hours after take-off: she can't have flown much further.
2. The chart: in that last call she says they're flying along the line 157-337. Rule it on the chart and it runs through the island she wanted, Howland, and, about 650 km to the south-east, through a ring of coral called Nikumaroro.
3. The sea floor round Howland, five kilometres down: sonar has swept about 1,200 square miles of it and found no plane. In 2024 a plane-shaped smudge on a sonar screen turned out to be a rock.
4. Nikumaroro, 1940: bones, part of a shoe, a bottle and a sextant box are found under a tree. A doctor measures the bones in 1941 and says a man; then the bones are lost. In 2018 his numbers are run again and fit a woman of her build; and the same year the box is traced to a Navy survey ship.
5. Nikumaroro, October 2026: a shiny shape in the lagoon, seen from space and thought to be the plane, is reached at last. It's a clump of dead trees with a white crust.

**The ending:** Both stories end with a ten-metre aluminium plane that has to be somewhere, and nobody has it. The sea floor that's been searched is a small part of where it could be, and the people who went to the lagoon last month say they'll go back.

## To settle before it's written

- The fuel clue contradicted the second explanation. Use: 'The fuel: about 1,100 gallons. Twenty hours by the short estimates, twenty-four by the long, and her last call comes about twenty hours after take-off. That gap is the whole argument.'
- Hook: 'A silver plane, twenty hours out, fuel running low, and the island isn't there.'
- Sonar has swept 'thousands of square miles' of sea floor, not about 1,200.
- The lagoon object is 'most likely a clump of dead trees with a white crust', and the team 'say they'll keep searching', not that they'll go back.
- Add: 'Navy planes flew over the island a week later and saw signs that someone had been living there, but no aircraft and nobody waving.'
- The radio log's times (20 h 13 min, the 157-337 line) were not re-checked at the archive: check before it's written.
