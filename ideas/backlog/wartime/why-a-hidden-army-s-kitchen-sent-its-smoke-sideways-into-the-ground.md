# Why a hidden army's kitchen sent its smoke sideways into the ground

**Seen:** The forest floor cut open like a slice of cake. Below ground, a cook crouches at a big blackened pot over a bright fire in a dug-out pit. ## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A cook at a pot over a bright fire in a pit under the forest floor, a plane passing overhead, and the smoke crawling sideways into the earth instead of going up.
- **What grabs them:** Smoke is exactly what a spotter plane hunts for, and this cook has a fire going in broad daylight with one overhead.
- **What's at stake:** Everyone in the hidden camp. VietnamNet's account says soldiers were killed or wounded after smoke from cooking fires 'revealed their positions to enemy aircraft'.
- **The answer you watch:** Smoke leaving the fire sideways, running along roofed channels that fan out underground, thinning as it goes, and seeping out as a faint ground mist while the plane flies straight on.

## How it goes

**War:** Invented in the First Indochina War (the Viet Minh against France) by an army cook, Hoang Cam, and named after him by the army in 1952. Then used through the Vietnam War, on the Ho Chi Minh Trail and in the Cu Chi tunnels.

**Problem:** Feed a hidden army hot food every day without the fire giving it away: smoke by day and firelight by night are what a spotter plane looks for.

**How known:** Documented, mostly from the Vietnamese side. Vietnam News, the state English-language daily (6 Nov 2022), and VietnamNet (21 Sep 2026) both describe the stove and its channels, and the army named it after him in October 1952: his division, the 308th, chose the name (Tien Phong, read by the fact-check: https://tienphong.vn/vinh-danh-nguoi-anh-nuoi-sang-tao-ra-bep-hoang-cam-post1877505.tpo). Western books cited by Wikipedia also describe the stove (Elliott 2003 p. 249; Lanning and Cragg 2008 p. 113; Van Dyke 1972), not opened. That it worked is the word of the army and of veterans quoted there ('No smoke by day, no light by night', a veteran of the Ho Chi Minh Trail): I found no test of it and no American record saying aircraft couldn't see it. What Hoang Cam saw that made him build it (soldiers killed after cooking smoke gave them away) is told by the same sources, not by a record of the time. The papers differ on when he built it: one says during the 1952 campaigns, a provincial paper (Hoa Binh's, seen in a search summary) says the Hoa Binh campaign of 1951 to 1952. So the card says only that the army named it in 1952, which Vietnam News dates to that October.

**Left today:** A Hoang Cam stove is on display at the Dien Bien Phu Victory Museum (photo caption, VietnamNet, 21 Sep 2026). The preserved Cu Chi tunnels at Ben Dinh and Ben Duoc are open to visitors, and Nhan Dan (8 Apr 2025) lists 'Hoang Cam kitchens (smokeless stoves)' among what the tunnels held. Vietnam News says the stove 'remains in the syllabus of military academies'. Hoang Cam (1916 to 1996) was posthumously named a Hero of the People's Armed Forces: the title was presented at a ceremony on 17 Sep 2026, on behalf of the State President.

**Shown as:** No harm is shown. The danger is a plane crossing the sky, and once, a flash and smoke on an empty clearing where an open fire had been. Nobody is hit. It's a kitchen, not a weapon, so nothing in it is a thing a viewer could build to hurt anyone.

1. A cooking fire sends up smoke by day and a glow by night, and a plane that sees either one knows where the camp is. So the cooks cooked at night and doused the fire when they heard engines, and the rice was left uncooked.
2. So the fire goes down into a pit dug in the ground, with a covered hole in front of it that hides the light and lets air in to the fire.
3. From the fire, long channels are dug away through the ground, spreading out like the arms of a squid.
4. The channels are roofed with branches and a thin layer of soil that's kept moist, so the smoke can't rise: it has to travel along them.
5. As it travels it cools and thins. What reaches the open air, low to the ground, is a thin mist that looks like morning mist and quickly disperses.
6. So, the army says, its cooks could cook in daylight with a plane overhead.

**The ending:** The same cutaway at dawn: the pot steaming underground, a thin mist lying low among the trees, the plane flying straight on. Then the stove as it's shown today, in the Dien Bien Phu Victory Museum. Said: 'Soldiers who used it said: no smoke by day, no light by night. An army cook named Hoang Cam worked it out, and in 1952 the army gave it his name. It still teaches it, and in 2026 Vietnam named him a hero for it.'

## Where it's known from

Vietnam News (Viet Nam News Agency's English daily), 'A Kitchen God: how a stove helped save lives', Nguyen My Ha, 6 Nov 2022: the stove is dug into a hill or the ground with smoke channels spreading out like 'a squid's tentacles'; 'He would place tree branches on the tunnels and then cover them with dirt so no visible smoke could come out'; a covered hole in front hid the light and gave the fire more air; smoke 'just like the morning mist'; cooking at night before it, the rice 'was left uncooked'; 'the army gave this new kitchenette a name: the Hoang Cam stove, after its inventor' (October 1952); 'No smoke by day, no light by night' (veteran Trinh Hoang, of the Ho Chi Minh Trail); a photo caption says one was used daily in the Cu Chi guerrilla tunnels; still in the syllabus of military academies. VietnamNet, 'Vietnam honors soldier behind ingenious smoke-hiding wartime stove', Tran Thuong, 21 Sep 2026: 'smoke rising from cooking fires in the forest revealed their positions to enemy aircraft'; the stove 'connected to a network of smoke channels', covered with branches and 'a thin layer of soil that was kept moist'; the smoke 'emerged near ground level as a thin mist that quickly dispersed'; born 1916, died 1996; named Hero of the People's Armed Forces, ceremony 17 Sep 2026; a stove 'on display at the Dien Bien Phu Victory Museum': https://vietnamnet.vn/en/vietnam-honors-soldier-behind-ingenious-smoke-hiding-wartime-stove-2557233.html Nhan Dan, 'In Pictures: Cu Chi Tunnels', 8 Apr 2025, lists 'Hoang Cam kitchens (smokeless stoves)' among the tunnels' facilities: https://en.nhandan.vn/in-pictures-cu-chi-tunnels-heroic-underground-feat-post146312.html Not found: the Cu Chi site's own page on its kitchens, or a museum's object page.
