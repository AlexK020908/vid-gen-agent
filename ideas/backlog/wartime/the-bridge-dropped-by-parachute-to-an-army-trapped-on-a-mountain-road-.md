# The bridge dropped by parachute to an army trapped on a mountain road in Korea

**Heard:** This steel bridge is falling out of the sky on parachutes.

**Seen:** A long steel bridge beam hanging level under huge parachutes, coming down over snow-covered mountains toward a small ring of tents and trucks, with a twin-boomed cargo plane pulling away above it.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A steel bridge beam hanging under huge parachutes, coming down over snowy mountains toward a small camp.
- **What grabs them:** A ton of steel dropped from a plane ought to land bent, and on the test drop it did.
- **What's at stake:** More than 14,000 men with their wounded, trucks and tanks, on the only road off the mountain, with a gap in it.
- **The answer you watch:** The gap in the road with the drop below it; a test section landing hard and bent; eight sections coming down under bigger parachutes, one drifting away; two steel tracks laid across the gap; a tractor's wheel going through the plywood; the tracks pushed apart; a jeep creeping over with its tyres scraping both edges.

## How it goes

**War:** The Korean War: the US 1st Marine Division's breakout from the Chosin Reservoir, at Funchilin Pass south of Koto-ri, North Korea, 6 to 10 December 1950.

**Problem:** Get a whole division, with its trucks, guns and tanks, down the only road off a mountain when the bridge on it has been blown and there's a cliff on one side and a drop on the other.

**How known:** Documented in the Marine Corps's official history: Lynn Montross and Captain Nicholas A. Canzona, U.S. Marine Operations in Korea, 1950-1953, volume III, The Chosin Reservoir Campaign (Historical Branch, G-3, Headquarters US Marine Corps, 1957), chapter XIV, whose footnotes rest on an interview with Partridge of 25 June 1951 and on an account of the airdrop in Combat Forces Journal (May 1951). Other accounts give the sections other weights (1,900, 2,900, 4,000 pounds) and name the parachutes' sizes: the card keeps to the official history, which gives 2,500 pounds and says only 'larger parachutes'. The history doesn't count the usable sections: six is eight less the two it names. An Air Force article calling this the only bridge ever airdropped to troops was seen in a search summary only, so the card doesn't say it.

**Left today:** One of the eight planes: a C-119, number 352, stands in the Air Mobility Command Museum beside Dover Air Force Base, Delaware, dedicated on 1 Oct 2022 ('Eight C-119 aircraft, including #352, delivered M2 Treadway bridge sections'). The sources differ on whether it is a C-119B or a C-119C. The pass itself is in North Korea. I didn't find what is at the site now.

**Shown as:** No fighting and nobody hurt on screen. The danger is the gap, the drop below it and the cold. One bridge section drifts down beyond a ridge and is lost, and a tractor's wheel goes through a plywood panel. The rest is steel, trucks and a jeep's tyres on two narrow tracks.

1. The only road down the mountain crosses a bridge over a sheer drop, and it's been blown for the third time. The gap is 16 feet, 24 counting the broken ends, and there is 'no possibility of a bypass'. More than 14,000 men are above it.
2. The division's engineer, Lieutenant Colonel John Partridge, asks for steel treadway bridge sections to be dropped by parachute. A test drop the day before badly damages one.
3. So bigger parachutes are flown in from Japan, and on 7 December eight C-119 cargo planes drop eight sections of 2,500 pounds each, twice what's needed. One comes down among the Chinese and one is damaged.
4. On 9 December trucks carry the sections to the gap. The broken ends are built up, the two steel treadways are laid across with plywood panels between them, and in about three hours there is a bridge.
5. Then a tractor breaks through the plywood, and nothing on wheels can cross. Partridge has the two treadways pushed as far apart as they'll go: 136 inches. That gives a tank two inches to spare on the steel, and a jeep half an inch on the inside edges.
6. The first jeep crosses in the dark, 'its tires scraping both edges', and all night engineers with flashlights guide the column over.

**The ending:** Night, snow on the ground: a line of trucks and men on foot, engineers' flashlights showing the way, coming down the mountain across two steel tracks laid over the drop. Then one of the planes that dropped it, a C-119 standing today in a museum in Delaware. Said: 'More than fourteen thousand men were above that gap. They came down the mountain over a bridge that arrived by parachute.'

## Where it's known from

Lynn Montross and Captain Nicholas A. Canzona, USMC, U.S. Marine Operations in Korea, 1950-1953, volume III, The Chosin Reservoir Campaign (Historical Branch, G-3, Headquarters US Marine Corps, Washington, 1957), chapter XIV, 'Onward from Koto-ri', read as Project Gutenberg eBook 65009: the bridge three and a half miles south of Koto-ri blown for the third time; 'Between the cliff and the sheer drop down the mountainside there was no possibility of a bypass'; 'Thus the gap of 16 feet (24 feet, counting the abutments) must be spanned if the Division was to bring out its vehicles, tanks and guns'; 'a bridge section was badly damaged on the 6th after being test-dropped at Yonpo by an Air Force C-119'; 'larger parachutes flown to Yonpo from Japan'; eight sections asked for where four were needed; 'three of the 2500-pound bridge sections were dropped inside the Koto-ri perimeter' at 0930 on 7 December; 'The remaining five sections were delivered by noon, one of them falling into the hands of the Chinese and one being damaged'; 'After the abutments were constructed, a Brockway truck laid the treadways and plywood panels in position', the bridge in place about 1530 on 9 December, three hours after work began; 'A tractor towing an earth-moving pan broke through the plywood center panel, rendering it useless'; a total width of '136 inches', 'two inches to spare' for the M-26 tanks and 'barely half an inch for the jeeps'; the first jeep, 'its tires scraping both edges'; 'All night long on 9-10 December an endless stream of troops and vehicles poured across the span'; 'More than 14,000 men would then be organized for the next stage of the breakout' (at Koto-ri, before the move down the pass). The plane: 436th Airlift Wing, 'Historic aircraft from Korean War battle dedicated at AMC Museum', Mauricio Campino, 1 Oct 2022: https://www.dvidshub.net/news/printable/430695 The history says communist prisoners were put to work on the abutments, which the card leaves out. 'A ton of steel' in the mistake line is 2,500 pounds, a little over a ton.
