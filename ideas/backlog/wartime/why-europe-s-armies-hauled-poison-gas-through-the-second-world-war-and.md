# Why Europe's armies hauled poison gas through the Second World War and held it back

- **Kind:** wartime, built as `why_it_happens`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** Both these armies brought poison gas to the front, and almost none of it is ever fired.

**Seen:** A war-room map table of Europe, 1944. Two armies of wooden blocks press against each other along a front line. Behind each side sits a stack of shells with a yellow band round each one, and a gloved hand rests on each stack without moving it. Beside the table stands one soldier with his gas mask on.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A war going on in blocks across a map table, one stack of yellow-banded shells on each side that stays put, a hand resting on each, and a soldier standing by with his gas mask on. It is a table and a masked man, not a bizarre sight: of the four tests this is the one it passes most weakly.
- **What grabs them:** Armies in a total war, burning each other's cities, carry a ready weapon across a continent for six years and leave almost all of it in the crate.
- **What's at stake:** The soldiers at both fronts and the people in the cities behind them. Roosevelt's threat names 'munition centers, seaports, and other military objectives throughout the whole extent of the territory' of any country that used gas.
- **The answer you watch:** Introduced in the voice as 'one reading:', a hand lifts one side's yellow-banded stack toward the front, and across the table a row of model bombers with the same yellow band lights up, pointed at that side's cities. The hand puts the stack back. Then the far corner of the map, China: Japan's banded block moves forward where no banded stack faces it, and stays put where one does. What's watched is a pattern on a table. The fear itself is in commanders' heads and can only be shown this way.

## How it goes

**War:** Second World War, Europe, 1939 to 1945: Germany on one side, Britain, the United States and the Soviet Union on the other, all with gas in store. And the war in China in the same years, where Japan's army did use it.

**Problem:** Fight a total war without being the first to use a weapon the other side can send straight back onto your own soldiers and cities.

**How known:** Two things are documented and one is a reading. Documented: the Allied policy. Roosevelt's 'Statement Warning the Axis Against Using Poison Gas', 8 June 1943 (American Presidency Project, read in full): 'we shall under no circumstances resort to the use of such weapons unless they are first used by our enemies', and 'full and swift retaliation in kind'. His 'Statement on Japanese Use of Poison Gas', 5 June 1942 (same archive, read in full), promised 'retaliation in kind and in full measure'. Documented: that it was used where the other side couldn't answer. Walter Grunden (Bowling Green State University), who studied the Tokyo trial interrogations of Tojo and two other generals, on why Japan gassed Chinese troops and not Americans: 'Because they knew the Chinese could not retaliate in kind in the field', 'But they feared massive retaliation from the others' (the university's news page, June 2015, read; his chapter 'No Retaliation in Kind', Springer 2017, wouldn't load and was not opened). The reading: why Germany held back. Richard Overy (University of Exeter), reviewing M. Girard Dorsey's Holding Their Breath (Cornell, 2023) in EuropeNow, 15 Feb 2024 (read): the Axis states 'were presumably deterred by the risk of retaliation', and for the Allies the trigger 'was always going to be their use by the enemy'; he adds that the book leaves the Axis side thin. FactCheck.org (April 2017, read) lists the other reasons historians give: that gas didn't suit Germany's way of fighting and risked hitting its own troops, that Hitler had been gassed himself in 1918, and, from Frank Dinan, professor emeritus of chemistry at Canisius College, that a German scientist led Hitler to think the Allies had the new nerve agents too (the chain is FactCheck citing the Daily Mail citing Dinan: a chemistry professor quoted in the press, not a historian's study), when in fact, says John Hart of SIPRI there, 'There was a general lack of awareness of these agents in the UK and the US during the war'. So fear of the answer is one reason historians give, not a proven one (FactCheck ranks none of them and leads with 'tactical decisions'; Overy says 'presumably deterred'), and the hook gives no reason at all. It was not a moral line on either side: Overy's review says Churchill considered gas against an invasion in 1940, that RAF plans of 1943 to 1944 to gas German cities had a target list, and that the Allies discussed gas against Japan in summer 1945. Bari is from the same review. Britain's civilian masks (about 38 million by September 1939) were seen only in search summaries of the UK National Archives' education page, which no longer loads, so the card gives no number. 'Almost none' used on Europe's battlefields is the brief's starting point and fits these sources. One exception is on record there, and it is disputed: FactCheck.org says 'some believe the Germans did use poison gas against enemy soldiers in World War II', citing the historian Catherine Merridale on some 3,000 Soviet troops and civilians in caves after Kerch in 1942 (https://www.factcheck.org/2017/04/hitler-chemical-weapons). That is why the hook and the last step say 'almost'. Not opened: Dorsey's book itself, Frederic Brown's Chemical Warfare: A Study in Restraints (1968), SIPRI's history, or the US Army's official history of its Chemical Warfare Service. A First World War gas strategy was weighed instead and dropped: it can't be told without saying how gas was used, which is outside the lines, and the reference has two First World War gas shorts already.

**Left today:** A later treaty: the Chemical Weapons Convention, in force since 1997. Its watchdog, the OPCW, confirmed on 7 July 2023 that the last stockpile any member country had declared was destroyed that day at Blue Grass in Kentucky, 72,304 tonnes in all since 1997. Those were later stocks, not the war's own. The wartime masks are in museums (the London Museum lists a child's one: seen in a search summary only).

**Shown as:** Nothing is released on anyone. Gas is a yellow band on a stack of shells and, once, a yellow tint spreading over a patch of the map of China, at the distance of a model. No cloud on people, no victims, no symptoms. Nothing about what any gas is made of, how it's made or how it's delivered. One line the script must keep exact: this is about the battlefield. Nazi Germany murdered millions of people with gas in its camps, and the short never says or implies that Germany 'didn't use gas': the ending's first line says so outright.

1. Both sides go into the war with gas in store and keep adding to it. Soldiers carry masks, and in Britain masks go out to civilians too.
2. On 8 June 1943 President Roosevelt says in public that America will never use it first, and promises 'full and swift retaliation in kind' on any enemy that does.
3. The stock travels with the armies. In December 1943 a German air raid on the Italian port of Bari hits an Allied ship with gas aboard, and it gets out. The Allies keep that secret.
4. Germany by now has a newer and deadlier kind that the Allies don't know exists, and still doesn't fire it. One account, from a chemistry professor quoted in the press, is that Hitler was led to believe the Allies had it too.
5. A historian who read the Japanese generals' interrogations says: in China it goes the other way. Japan's army uses gas on Chinese troops, who have none to send back, and holds it back from the Americans, who do.
6. So in Europe the stacks sit behind both fronts until 1945, ready and almost entirely unused on the battlefield.

**The ending:** The front line has crossed the whole table and the war in Europe is over. Both yellow-banded stacks are where they started, hardly touched. Then a date on the picture: 7 July 2023. Said: 'Germany murdered millions with gas in its camps. At the front, its stock was hardly touched. Whether America's promise to answer gas with gas is what kept it there, historians still argue. Stockpiles like these were kept for decades more: the last one any country had declared was destroyed in 2023.'
