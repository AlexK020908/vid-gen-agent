# Why Japan flew planes into ships in 1944, and the ring of destroyers that took the blows

- **Kind:** wartime, built as `why_it_happens`

## The first second

**Heard:** This plane is diving at a carrier, and it won't pull up.

**Seen:** A blue map table of the sea off Okinawa. In the middle, a model aircraft carrier among its escorts. A hand holds a small model fighter, a bomb under its belly, tipped nose-down at the carrier's deck.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A hand tipping a model fighter, bomb under its belly, nose-down at the deck of a model aircraft carrier, and not pulling it out of the dive.
- **What grabs them:** An air force destroying its own planes and pilots on purpose, one for every attack, when it is already short of both.
- **What's at stake:** The crews of the ships and the men flying the planes. By the Survey's count about 45 ships were sunk, and every one of the 2,550 missions was meant to end with its pilot dead.
- **The answer you watch:** A model plane lets go of its bomb over a model ship and the bomb falls wide. The same plane steered all the way in reaches the deck. Then a ring of small destroyers is set out far from the carriers, a sweep of light turning from each; planes coming in from the north meet the ring first; fighters are lifted off the carriers and set down over the ring.

## How it goes

**War:** The Pacific War, October 1944 to August 1945: Japan's navy and army air units against the American and Allied fleets, from the Philippines to Okinawa.

**Problem:** Stop a fleet you can no longer match, with pilots who have had a fraction of the flying hours their opponents have.

**How known:** Documented, mostly in what the United States collected at the time and just after. The United States Strategic Bombing Survey, Summary Report (Pacific War), 1 July 1946: the flying hours, the 2,550 missions and 475 effective ('18.6 percent'), the ships damaged and sunk, 2,000 B-29 sorties turned onto the airfields in Kyushu, and its judgement that 'Had the Japanese been able to sustain an attack of greater power and concentration they might have been able to cause us to withdraw or to revise our strategic plans'. The Survey's interrogation of Captain Inoguchi Rikibei of the First Air Fleet's staff, Tokyo, 15 October 1945 (Nav No. 12): 'Admiral ONISHI ordered the organization of the Kamikaze on 19 October 1944', the first attack on 25 October, and his own estimate that about one-sixth of the planes hit in the Philippines and about one-ninth at Okinawa. The US Fleet's 'Antiaircraft Action Summary: Suicide Attacks' (COMINCH P-009, 30 April 1945), for October 1944 to January 1945: 312 attempts and 121 hits, and for ordinary attacks '1092 non-suicide planes were taken under fire' and '23 or 1.2 percent, scored hits on ships'. The printed '1.2 percent' is likely a transposition of 2.1 (23 of 1,092), and the running text also gives 1,132 for the same group, so the card gives the count, 23, and says 'about two in a hundred'. What is told, not documented here: what Onishi meant it to do. A secondary account (James P. Duffy in American Heritage, from the Japanese officers' memoir The Divine Wind, 1958, not opened) says the first units were to put the carriers' decks out of action for at least a week so that Japan's battle fleet could reach Leyte Gulf. The Survey's own wider reading is that 'The one and only asset which they still possessed was the willingness of their pilots to meet certain death'. Whether the pilots were volunteers: Inoguchi told his interrogators that anyone who wished could join; how free that choice was has been questioned since, I opened nothing on it, and the short doesn't speak of it. The ring: 16 radar picket stations by the US National Park Service's page, 15 by Captain David Boslaugh's history of the fighter directors, which also gives 'fifty to sixty miles from the main body' as the practice and a team of fighter directors for every picket 'As far as possible', which is why the card says 'most'. That pilots went for the first ships they saw is the Park Service's: 'The first potential target that the Japanese airmen often saw were dangerously exposed American destroyers.' Not opened: the US Navy's own history pages (they wouldn't load) or Samuel Eliot Morison's volumes.

**Left today:** USS Cassin Young (DD-793), a destroyer that stood radar picket duty off Okinawa and was hit on 12 April 1945 and again on 30 July ('22 men dead and 45 wounded'), is kept by the US National Park Service at Boston National Historical Park, and visitors go aboard. The word itself, in most languages. In Japan the Chiran Peace Museum keeps the pilots' letters (general knowledge, not opened here).

**Shown as:** Models on a table, moved by a hand, at a distance. A model plane meets a model deck and there's a puff of smoke: no cockpit, no face, no body, no burning ship with people on it. The dead are numbers on a card, on both sides. The pilots are neither mocked nor praised: the short says what the plan was, why it was chosen and what it cost. 'Suicide attack' is the US Navy's term of the time; the script can say 'flown into the ship'.

1. In 1941 Japan's first-line pilots have 500 to 800 flying hours each. By the end of the war the average is 'just over 100', against 600 for an American. By the summer of 1944 its air commanders see 'no way in which they could equal the United States air arms at any point'.
2. The US Navy's own count, October 1944 to January 1945: of 1,092 ordinary planes its ships' guns engaged, 23 scored a hit. About two in a hundred.
3. On 19 October 1944, with the American fleet off the Philippines, Vice Admiral Onishi orders special units formed: a fighter with a bomb, flown into the ship. The pilot no longer has to aim and let go, only steer. In the same count, of 312 such attempts, 121 hit.
4. Off Okinawa in 1945 the fleet answers by pushing its warning outward: a ring of destroyers with radar, tens of miles out, most with a team steering fighters onto whatever its radar sees. And bombers are sent against the airfields the planes rise from.
5. The attackers tend to dive on the first ships they see, and those are the ring. The destroyers take the first blows.
6. The count at the end: 2,550 missions, 475 of them effective. About 45 ships sunk, most of them destroyers. Over the whole campaign twelve carriers are among the ships damaged, and nothing bigger than an escort carrier is sunk.

**The ending:** The table at the end: the ring of small destroyers with gaps in it, and the big carriers still afloat inside. Then a real ship: the destroyer Cassin Young, one of that ring, hit twice, tied up today in Boston where visitors walk her deck. Said: 'It hit far more often than a bomb did, and it still didn't stop the fleet. The ring of small ships took the first blows. This is one of them.'
