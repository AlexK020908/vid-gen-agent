# What the Viet Cong's traps were for: what one US division learned by counting 758 of them

## The first second

**Heard:** Nobody is watching these traps. The army that counted them asked its prisoners why.

**Seen:** A model of delta country on a table, seen from above: rice paddies, dikes, trails, and a patch of jungle with a few low bunkers hidden in it. Under the leaves, along the trails and on the dikes, small red glows. A line of soldier-blocks has stopped at the first of them. There is nobody near any of them.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A patrol of blocks stopped dead at a scatter of red glows near a hidden camp, with nobody there to stop them. It works only once the glows read as traps, which the reference's own trap shorts suggest a viewer gets quickly.
- **What grabs them:** A trap with nobody watching it looks wasted: no ambush, nobody to follow it up. And the side that has stopped is the one with the helicopters.
- **What's at stake:** The men on the patrol. In April 1969 traps caused 41 percent of the division's dead and 63 percent of its wounded, and 40 percent of all its casualties were soldiers less than two months in the country.
- **The answer you watch:** Marks going onto a tally board one by one, 758 of them, the tallest columns under jungle growth (36.1%), trails (20.3%) and rice paddy dikes (13.3%); a column headed 'covered by fire' that stays almost empty; then, told as the prisoners' account, the glows on the model drawn in a ring round the bunkers, with nobody standing at them; a clock face with one cluster of marks at 11 and another at 4; a red circle on the table that covers three blocks when they're bunched and one when they're spaced; small flags going up on the glows before the blocks reach them.

## How it goes

**War:** The Vietnam War: Viet Cong units in the Mekong Delta, and the US 9th Infantry Division that fought them there, 1968 to 1969. The figures are the division's own, most of them for April 1969.

**Problem:** Guard a hidden camp against a far stronger army without leaving anyone there to guard it.

**How known:** Documented, by one side, in its commanders' own study: Lieutenant General Julian J. Ewell and Major General Ira A. Hunt Jr., Sharpening the Combat Edge: The Use of Analysis to Reinforce Military Judgment (Department of the Army, Vietnam Studies, 1974), chapter VI, section 'Mines and Booby Traps', with its tables 22 to 26. Every figure in the card was read there. The study has no map: it has those tables and a chart, so the short shows a tally board, and the tables put the traps mostly in jungle growth (36.1 percent), on trails (20.3 percent) and on rice paddy dikes (13.3 percent). The ring round the bunkers is the prisoners' account, not a plotted finding. What the traps were for rests on prisoner interrogations as those commanders report them: I read no Vietnamese account, and the brief's line holds, that this is what the other side recorded. The tables give 758 traps without saying what months they cover; the 41 and 63 percent, and the 230 that went off, are for 1 to 30 April 1969. The table has 66.4 percent found by eye and 29.2 percent gone off, which the book rounds to 70 and 30. The US Army's 1967 circular on Viet Cong traps (TC 5-31, the source of the trap-markers card) says in general that they were meant to 'inflict personnel casualties and hinder the progress of the troops'. 'A fence' is the card's word for the prisoners' 'defensive measure around perimeters'. That the ring bought the camp time to empty is NOT in the pages read, so the card doesn't say it. Two cautions. This is a study by the officers whose methods it praises, not an outside audit. And its figures are for April 1969, inside the 9th Division's Operation Speedy Express, for which secondary sources give a US Army Inspector General estimate of 5,000 to 7,000 civilian casualties (The Nation on Newsweek's 1972 report, found by the fact-check, not by me: https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/vietnam-exposeacute-wasnt/). The ending keeps 'by the division's own count' for that reason. And an Army-wide figure often quoted, about a tenth of deaths and a sixth of wounds from mines and traps over the whole war, was seen only in search summaries, so it isn't in the card. The Marine Corps's official histories, which describe the same thing around Da Nang, were found but not opened.

**Left today:** The study itself, free to read in the US Army Center of Military History's Vietnam Studies series, and the 1967 training circular on Viet Cong traps, in public archives. Nothing else was checked.

**Shown as:** No trap is seen working on a person, and no trap is drawn at all: each is a red glow under leaves or a mark on a tally board. No device, no sizes, no materials, no steps. One flash on an empty stretch of model path, then the blocks stop. Men are blocks, and casualties are percentages on a card.

1. In April 1969 traps cause 41 percent of the 9th Division's soldiers killed and 63 percent of those wounded. Its commanders call them 'the single most important casualty producer' in their area.
2. So the division counts them. Every one it meets goes into a table, 758 of them: where it was, how it was found, what time of day.
3. The tally shows a surprise: 94 percent 'were not covered by fire'. Nobody was waiting beside them. Prisoners explain why: they were set 'as a defensive measure around perimeters of bunkered positions'. A fence that needs no guard.
4. The clock shows another. Finds peak around 11 in the morning, 'when the troops were fresh and alert'. Detonations peak around 4 in the afternoon, 'when the troops were tired'. And 46 percent of those that go off catch more than one man, because the men are walking bunched together.
5. So the answer is drilled, not built: teach every man the few common kinds, change the man in front before he tires, keep the spacing, and walk with former Viet Cong as scouts. The share found before they go off rises 'from less than 50 percent to over 70 percent'.

**The ending:** The same table: the line of blocks, spread out now, goes on through the glows, and small flags stand on most of them before any block reaches them. A counter turns from 'under 50%' to 'over 70%'. Said: 'The traps were a fence that needed no guard. The answer wasn't a machine. It was counting them: where, what time of day, and how new the men were. Found before they went off, by the division's own count: from under half to over seven in ten.'
