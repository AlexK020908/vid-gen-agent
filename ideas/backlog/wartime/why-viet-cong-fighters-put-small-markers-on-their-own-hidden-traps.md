# Why Viet Cong fighters put small markers on their own hidden traps

## How it goes

**War:** The Vietnam War: Viet Cong fighters in South Vietnam, as the US Army recorded it in a training circular of May 1967.

**Problem:** Hide a trap on a path so well that a patrol won't see it, without it catching your own fighters and the villagers who walk the same path every day.

**How known:** Documented, but only by the other side. Department of the Army Training Circular TC 5-31, 'Viet Cong Boobytraps, Mines and Mine Warfare Techniques' (10 May 1967), paragraph 18, 'Marking of Mines and Boobytraps by the Viet Cong', with drawings (Figures 79 to 82). It is the US Army's record of what its troops found: I read no Vietnamese account. The circular is careful itself: its soldiers were to read the signs 'only as danger signals', a sign reading MIN 'does not pinpoint the location', and the Viet Cong used markings for other things too. On how often, the circular's own words are 'little consistency in the methods and frequency of marking', though 'Roads and trails are frequently marked', and a sign's meaning 'can be changed from day to day'. Read as the OCR text of a scan on archive.org, not from an Army archive's own copy.

**Left today:** The circular, in public archives. The idea is now treaty law: Amended Protocol II (3 May 1996) to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons requires a mined area's marking to be 'visible to a person who is about to enter', and its annex sets the sign, a triangle or square, 'red or orange with a yellow reflecting border'.

**Shown as:** The trap never works on anyone. It's seen only as a dark hollow under leaves in x-ray: no spikes, no sizes, no materials, nothing about how one is made. Every foot that reaches it stops or steps round. The short is about the signs, and a sign hurts nobody.

1. The fighters who set a trap on a path share that path with their own people. The US Army's circular says they marked their traps to keep their own 'freedom of movement', and out of 'some regard for the protection of their own people and villagers'.
2. So a trap could get a sign that looks like nothing: a small tripod of sticks over a spike trap, three stones in a row across a road, or a stick broken near one end, with the broken end pointing at the mined stretch.
3. A villager who knows the signs comes down the path, sees the tripod and steps round it. A stranger sees litter.
4. At the entrance of a boobytrapped dwelling, cave or tunnel the sign is one to three pieces of string: 'inconspicuous to the casual observer but apparent to the VC and others who know what to look for'.
5. When the fighters pulled back in good order, they took the signs away. When a position was overrun quickly, 'many markings are likely to be still in place', and patrols began to find them.
6. So in 1967 the US Army drew the sticks, stones, grass and strings in a circular for its own soldiers, and the men the traps were set for were taught to treat every one as a danger signal.

**The ending:** The patrol's boots stop at the tripod, short of the patch of leaves, and the tripod is left standing. Then a cut to the red triangle that marks a minefield today. Said: 'A hidden trap still had to be safe for the people who lived beside it. A treaty on mines now asks for more: a sign that anyone about to walk in can see.'
