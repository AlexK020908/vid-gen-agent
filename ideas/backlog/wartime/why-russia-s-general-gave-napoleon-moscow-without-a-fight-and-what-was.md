# Why Russia's general gave Napoleon Moscow without a fight, and what was left of Napoleon's army

- **Kind:** wartime, built as `true_story`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** Napoleon is at the gates of Moscow. Russia's general, Kutuzov, lets him have it.

**Seen:** A map table of western Russia, stamped September 1812. One long road runs from a river at the left edge to a city at the right, marked Moscow. A long column of blue blocks fills the road and has almost reached the city, under a paper tag on a pin: 'NAPOLEON'. Between it and Moscow stands one green block: 'KUTUZOV, Russia's main army'. The green block steps off the road.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A long column of blocks about to reach a city, one block standing in its way, and that block stepping aside so the column walks in. Then the column walks back out, shorter at every step. The thinning line reads without words; who these are needs the tags. A map table: the silent test passes only softened.
- **What grabs them:** The general defending a country hands the invader its most famous city without a fight.
- **What's at stake:** Russia's main army, which Kutuzov says is what stands between a lost city and a lost country; and, by the end, the men of Napoleon's column: 100,000 into Moscow and 10,000 out of Russia, on Minard's chart.
- **The answer you watch:** The blue column shrinking as it comes east, the counter over it falling from 422,000 to 100,000; one burst at Borodino; the green block stepping through Moscow and looping round to the south-west; the blue column sitting in the city while a calendar runs five weeks and nothing comes; the column setting off south-west, meeting the green block across its road, and bending back onto the road it came by; blocks turning pale and dropping away while the counter falls to 10,000 at the river.

## How it goes

**War:** Napoleon's invasion of Russia, June to December 1812: France and its allies (the Grande Armee) against the Russian Empire under Tsar Alexander I, whose main army Mikhail Kutuzov commanded from late August.

**Problem:** Save a country when the last battle has left your army too weak to keep the invader out of its old capital.

**How known:** The decision is documented in the Russian army's own papers. The headquarters' journal of military operations has the council at Fili on 1 September 1812 (Old Style: 13 September on today's calendar): Barclay de Tolly says a battle in that position is 'impossible' and the army should go back through Moscow; Bennigsen calls the position he chose unbeatable; Kutuzov says 'с потерянием Москвы не потеряна еще Россия' ('with the loss of Moscow, Russia is not yet lost'), that he 'holds it his first duty to save the army', and that it will retreat by the Ryazan road. His report to the Tsar of 4 September (Old Style) says the army was in 'extreme disorder' after Borodino, that no position near Moscow would do, and that 'the enemy's entry into Moscow is not yet the conquest of Russia' so long as the army is whole. Both were read in Russian on Wikisource, which reprints the 1954 to 1955 collection of Kutuzov's documents. No minutes of the council were kept (Wikipedia's article on it), so the journal is the headquarters' own summary. The numbers are Minard's, and they are softer than they look. His chart (20 November 1869) draws the 'men present' with the main column only, from five French books and one diary, and he says on it that he treated two detached corps as if they had marched with the army throughout; the armies on the flanks are not in it. I read it through a 2018 commentary that translates its legend (masswerk.at), not from the chart itself. Historians' figures, from Wikipedia's article on the invasion with its citations (Riehn, Mikaberidze, Sokolov, Clausewitz, Zamoyski: none opened): 440,000 to 449,000 in the first wave and 600,000 to 685,000 over the whole campaign; a 'roughly 100,000-strong army' into Moscow and 108,000 out of it; about 110,000 came back of some 615,000, counting the flanks; and on 14 December the rearguard recrossed the Niemen with 'barely a thousand men fit for action'. What is argued. How much was a plan: the long retreat began under Barclay de Tolly, whose retreats were unpopular, and the article says Kutuzov 'continued Barclay's successful strategy'; whether Russia's retreat was designed in advance or forced on it I did not settle. How much was Kutuzov: the Tsar's refusal to answer, the distance, sickness and Barclay's earlier retreat all did their part, and the same article gives the historian Dominic Lieven's view that Tolstoy's novel and later Russian and Soviet writing shaped the popular picture of 1812 (his book, Russia Against Napoleon, not opened). And it wasn't simply the winter: by Minard's own chart most of the men were gone before Moscow. Left out: the burning of Moscow (who set it is argued, and I read nothing on it), and a line often given as Kutuzov's at Fili, that the French would soak into Moscow 'like a sponge', which Wikipedia's article on the Council at Fili gives as Kutuzov's (its own source not opened); the card doesn't use it. Borodino's figures are Wikipedia's ('more than 250,000 troops', 'at least 70,000 casualties'). June 1812 for the crossing of the Niemen is general knowledge.

**Left today:** Minard's chart of 1869, still reprinted wherever people are taught to draw data. A rebuilt hut at Fili (the original burned in 1868; rebuilt 1887) has been a branch of the Borodino Panorama Museum since 1962. Aleksei Kivshenko painted the council twice: 1880, Russian Museum; 1882, Tretyakov Gallery (Wikipedia's article on the council). Tolstoy put the council into War and Peace (book eleven, chapter four: seen in a search result).

**Shown as:** A column of blocks on a road with a counter over it, and a line that thins: no figures, no hand, no battle seen close. Napoleon, Kutuzov and the Tsar are named on tags and never drawn. Borodino is two blocks pressed together with one burst of light, both turning pale. The retreat's dead are a number falling on a counter and blocks turning pale and being lifted away. Nobody is shown freezing or starving.

**People:** Mikhail Kutuzov, commander of Russia's main army from late August 1812, on the side that had been falling back all summer: after Borodino he wanted above all to keep the army in being. Napoleon, emperor of the French, with the bigger army: he wanted one decisive battle and then a peace from the Tsar. Tsar Alexander I, in St Petersburg, who refused to answer Napoleon's offers. Barclay de Tolly, who had commanded during the long retreat and been replaced by Kutuzov: at Fili he argued for giving up the city. General Bennigsen, who argued for fighting in front of Moscow.

**Lesson:** So when holding on would cost you everything, think of Kutuzov: give up the place, and keep what lets you fight again. The saying is his own, from the council at Fili as his headquarters' journal has it: 'с потерянием Москвы не потеряна еще Россия', with the loss of Moscow, Russia is not yet lost.

1. June 1812: Napoleon crosses the river Niemen into Russia. Charles Minard's chart of 1869 starts his main column at 422,000 men. Russia's armies fall back before it all summer, and by Moscow the chart's band is down to 100,000. Some corps were sent off to guard its flanks, but most of the loss comes before any snow.
2. 7 September: Kutuzov, in command since late August, stands and fights at Borodino. More than 250,000 men are on the field and at least 70,000 men are lost. Napoleon's army is still coming.
3. 13 September, at Fili, just outside Moscow: the generals are split between one more battle and retreat. Kutuzov ends it: 'With the loss of Moscow, Russia is not yet lost.' His first duty, he says, is to save the army. It marches through the city and out the other side.
4. 14 September: Napoleon enters Moscow and waits for the Tsar to ask for peace. He waits five weeks, and the Tsar never answers. Kutuzov, meanwhile, swings round to the south-west of the city, to a camp at Tarutino, where he holds the roads and food and fresh troops reach him from the south.
5. 19 October: Napoleon gives up and marches out, with about 100,000 men, by a road to the south-west. At Maloyaroslavets, on 24 October, Kutuzov's army bars it. Napoleon turns back onto the road he came by, which both armies have already marched over.
6. Then come the cold, the hunger and the river crossings. On Minard's chart, even with the corps that had guarded its flank joining it on the way back, only 10,000 reach the Niemen in December.

**The ending:** The table in December 1812: the road back to the river, and on it a thin line of pale, cracked blue blocks, the counter over them stopped on 10,000 and labelled as Minard's count of the main column. Behind them, whole, the green block. Said: 'So when holding on would cost you everything, think of Kutuzov: give up the place, and keep what lets you fight again. With the loss of Moscow, he said, Russia is not yet lost.'
