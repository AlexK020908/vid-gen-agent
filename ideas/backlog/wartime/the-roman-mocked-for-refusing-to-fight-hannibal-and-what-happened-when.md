# The Roman mocked for refusing to fight Hannibal, and what happened when Rome stopped listening

- **Kind:** wartime, built as `true_story`

## The first second

**Heard:** Hannibal has beaten two Roman armies in a row. Rome's new commander, Fabius, refuses to fight him.

**Seen:** A map table of Italy, stamped 217 BC. Down in the plain, a mass of dark red blocks under a paper tag on a pin: 'HANNIBAL, army of Carthage', with small flames on the painted farmland round it. Up on the line of hills beside it, keeping level with it and coming no closer, a row of white blocks: 'FABIUS, commander of Rome'.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** An army of blocks burning its way across a plain while a second army keeps pace with it along the hills and never comes down. Then a far bigger mass does come down, and walks into a line that bends and closes round it. The trap reads without words; the refusing needs them. A map table: the silent test passes only softened.
- **What grabs them:** The commander sent to stop an invader who is burning the country watches from the hills and will not fight, while his own soldiers call him a coward.
- **What's at stake:** Rome itself: Hannibal has beaten two of its armies and is loose in Italy. And at Cannae, by Polybius's count, about 70,000 Roman soldiers.
- **The answer you watch:** White blocks keeping to the hill line beside the dark red mass as it moves, never closing; small flames spreading on the plain below while the white blocks stay put; the taunts landing on Fabius's tag as paper slips; then a far bigger white mass marching down onto the plain at Cannae, the red line's centre bending back before it and its two ends swinging in behind; white blocks cracking under a counter that runs to 70,000; then white blocks back on the hills, a year counter running, and the red mass leaving by sea.

## How it goes

**War:** The Second Punic War between Rome and Carthage, 218 to 201 BC: Hannibal's invasion of Italy. Fabius held sole command for Rome in 217 BC, and the battle of Cannae was fought in 216 BC.

**Problem:** Stop an invader who has beaten every army sent against him, without handing him another battle to win.

**How known:** Two ancient accounts, both read in the Loeb translations on LacusCurtius (University of Chicago). Polybius, Histories, book 3: a Greek who wrote within the century of the war and is the nearest full account (his dates, about 200 to 118 BC, are general knowledge, not checked here). Plutarch's Life of Fabius Maximus: written about three hundred years after, and the source of the jokes and of Fabius's reply. Livy's book 22, the third main account, was not opened. The quotations in the steps are theirs. The numbers at Cannae differ: Polybius has about 80,000 Roman foot and 'rather more than six thousand horse' against Hannibal's 40,000 and 10,000, with about 70,000 Romans dead and 10,000 taken; Plutarch has 88,000 drawn up and 'fifty thousand Romans fell'. Which is nearer is argued by modern historians, none of whom I opened, so the card says 'Polybius counts'. That 'Cunctator' began as an insult and became an honour is Wikipedia's wording in its article on Fabius, without a source there; what the ancient texts show is the mockery, under other words: Livy 22.12 has Minucius 'calling his deliberation indolence and his caution cowardice', and Ennius's 'cunctando' is praise, so the card calls the name only what Rome remembers him by. Rome did meet Hannibal in the field again after Cannae and lost whole armies (Herdonia, 212 BC, about 16,000 of some 18,000 killed as Livy tells it, and again in 210), so the card says Rome never again staked everything on one battle, not that it never fought him. The dates (sole command in 217 BC, Cannae in 216 BC, Fabius's death in 203 BC 'shortly after Hannibal's army left Italy') are from that article; 'thirteen more years' is 216 to 203. That Fabius picked off Hannibal's foragers is in Wikipedia's article on the Fabian strategy, not in the excerpts I read, so the steps leave it out. Told level, three things belong beside it. Delay kept Rome alive but did not end the war: that took Scipio's invasion of Africa, which Fabius opposed (Wikipedia: 'he opposed the young and ambitious Scipio Africanus, who wanted to carry the war to Africa'). The delaying was paid for by Italy's farmers, which is why Rome was angry: he let the countryside burn sooner than fight for it. And after Cannae several of Rome's allies did go over to Hannibal (Polybius 3.118 names Tarentum), so the cost of that one battle was more than the dead. Plutarch reports a saying he credits to Poseidonius, that 'the Romans called Fabius their buckler, and Marcellus their sword'. Why 'pedagogue' stung (the word for the slave who walked a boy to school and back) is my gloss, not Plutarch's sentence.

**Left today:** His name, as a word: a 'Fabian' strategy is one that wins by refusing battle (Wikipedia's article on it, read). George Washington was called 'the American Fabius', and the Fabian Society, founded in Britain in 1884 to change things step by step, took its name from him (Wikipedia's articles on Fabius and on the strategy, read). The same article says Kutuzov, the subject of another card here, was nicknamed 'the Russian Fabius'.

**Shown as:** Blocks on a map of Italy: no figures, no hand. Fabius and Hannibal are named on tags and never drawn. Burning farms are small flames on painted fields, with no people. Cannae is a big mass of white blocks pushing into a curved red line that bends back and closes round it, then white blocks turning pale and cracked under a counter. No bodies and no close view.

**People:** Quintus Fabius Maximus, given sole command of Rome's armies in 217 BC: he wanted to keep Rome's army whole and let time work, because Rome could replace its men and supplies. Hannibal, general of Carthage, who had brought an army into Italy: he wanted big, quick victories, and Plutarch says he alone saw how clever Fabius's refusal was. Marcus Minucius Rufus, Fabius's deputy, who wanted to fight and mocked him: he was voted 'an equal share in the command', walked into a trap, was rescued by Fabius, and then 'addressed him with a loud voice as Father' (Plutarch). Gaius Terentius Varro, the consul who led Rome's army into Cannae and got away with a few riders.

**Lesson:** So when a stronger rival wants the fight today, think of Fabius: you don't have to give it to them. If time is on your side, not losing comes first: it keeps you in the fight until you can win, and you may be called a coward while you do it. The saying is the Roman poet Ennius's, written of Fabius: 'Unus homo nobis cunctando restituit rem', one man, by delaying, restored the state to us (the Latin and its translation as given in Wikipedia's article on Fabius, which says Virgil repeats the line in the Aeneid; Ennius's own text was not opened).

1. 217 BC: Hannibal has crossed into Italy and beaten Rome twice, at the Trebia and at Lake Trasimene. Rome gives one man, Quintus Fabius Maximus, full command. He reasons, as Polybius tells it, that Hannibal's men have been 'trained in actual warfare constantly from their earliest youth', while Rome has 'inexhaustible supplies of provisions and men'. So time is on Rome's side, and a battle is the one thing that can lose it.
2. So he follows Hannibal along the hills, 'out of reach of the enemy's cavalry', camps on high ground and will not come down. Hannibal burns the farms in the plain below to pull him into a fight. He still doesn't come down.
3. Rome is furious. His own soldiers call him 'Hannibal's pedagogue'. His deputy, Minucius, asks whether he is 'taking his army up into heaven'. People say he is 'playing the coward'. Fabius answers that he would be a greater coward than they think him if he dropped his plan for fear of jokes.
4. 216 BC: Fabius is no longer in command, and Rome has had enough of waiting. It raises eight legions and their allies, about 80,000 foot soldiers in all, more than it has ever sent out together, and offers battle at Cannae. Hannibal's centre gives way before them, and when the Romans have pushed deep into it his wings close round them. Polybius counts about 70,000 Romans dead in a day.
5. Rome goes back to Fabius. 'In him', Plutarch says, 'Rome at once placed her last hopes.' No more staking everything on one battle: follow him, wear him down, and take back the towns one at a time.
6. Hannibal stays in Italy thirteen more years. Rome never again risks everything on one battle with him, and he never takes Rome. In 203 BC he sails home to Africa, where Rome finishes the war. Rome remembers him as Cunctator, 'the Delayer': 'one man, by delaying, restored the state to us.'

**The ending:** The table in 203 BC: Hannibal's dark red blocks sliding off Italy by sea, Rome still standing, the white blocks still on their hills. Over Fabius's tag, the name Rome remembers him by: CUNCTATOR, 'the Delayer'. Said: 'So when a stronger rival wants the fight today, think of Fabius: you don't have to give it to them. If time is on your side, not losing comes first: it keeps you in the fight until you can win.'
