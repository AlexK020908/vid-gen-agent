**Seen:** A wide, shallow, brown river with rocks showing through the water. Upstream, a row of squat iron-plated gunboats sitting still. In front of them, soldiers on the bank felling whole trees into the river, building a wall out across it.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** Iron gunboats sitting stranded in a river with its rocks showing, and soldiers felling trees into the water right in front of them.
- **What grabs them:** The river is the ships' only way out, and their own army is walling it off.
- **What's at stake:** Ten gunboats and their crews. If they can't get below the rapids they 'would have to be destroyed', or be lost to the other side.

## How it goes

**War:** American Civil War: the Union's Red River Campaign, at Alexandria, Louisiana, April and May 1864.

**Problem:** Get ten heavy gunboats down a river that has fallen to less than half the depth they need, before the army protecting them marches away.

**How known:** Documented. Steven D. Smith and George J. Castille III, Bailey's Dam (Louisiana Department of Culture, Recreation and Tourism, Division of Archaeology, Anthropological Study No. 8, March 1986), written from the official records and from surveys of the dam's remains, which went on the National Register of Historic Places in 1976. I didn't open the wartime Official Records themselves. The study gives no day for the start of work ('Through the next two weeks, troops struggled to build the dam'), so the card gives none. On Bailey's reward it says 'Congress gave Bailey a gold medal', where other accounts say the formal thanks of Congress: the card uses neither. Porter's 'greatest engineering feat' comes to the study through a 1985 book, not from his report, so the card doesn't use it. The line it does use, 'Words are inadequate to express the admiration I feel for the abilities of Lieutenant Colonel Bailey', is from his report of 16 May 1864 (Official Records of the Union and Confederate Navies, series I, volume 26), read by the fact-check in transcription.

**Left today:** The dam's remains 'could be seen at times of low water' into the 1980s. They have been under water since 1987, when the lock and dam downstream was finished (Wikipedia's article on the dam, found by the fact-check; not checked at the Army engineers' own record). Louisiana's Forts Randolph and Buhlow State Historic Site, at 135 Riverfront Street, Pineville, says it 'includes the remains of Bailey's Dam', with signs and a boardwalk overlook: the overlook shows the place, not the dam.

**Shown as:** No fighting is shown. The danger is a deadline: ships their own crews will have to destroy if they can't get down. What moves on screen is water, trees, barges and ships.

1. The river has fallen. Over the rapids at Alexandria it's only about 3 feet deep, and even the lightest gunboat needs 7. Ten gunboats are stuck above the rapids, and if they can't get down they'll have to be destroyed by their own crews.
2. Lieutenant Colonel Joseph Bailey, who had built dams in Wisconsin, where lumbermen used them to float logs, proposes a dam just above the lower rapids, to back the water up over the rocks upstream.
3. About 3,000 soldiers build it from both banks at once. From one side come whole trees, laid along the current and weighted with stone, so the water's own push holds them down. From the other come big wooden boxes weighted with brick and stone. Four coal barges are sunk to close the middle.
4. The water behind the dam rises: 4 feet by 6 May, 5 feet 4 inches by 8 May.
5. At about 5:30 on the morning of 9 May, two of the barges break loose and the river pours through. The gunboat Lexington is sent at the gap: she gets over the rapids, steers straight for the opening, and comes out into deep water. Three more follow her.
6. Six are still above. So Bailey leaves the gap open as a chute and builds smaller dams up at the rapids to squeeze the water into one channel. By 13 May all ten are through.

**The ending:** One of the big ironclads, the Mound City, rides the rush of water through the gap between the broken ends of the dam, and the river behind her falls back onto its rocks. Then the same bend of the Red River today, calm, from the overlook at Pineville: the overlook shows the place, not the dam, whose remains have been under water since 1987. Said: 'He blocked the river to raise it, and let it break to carry them out. Their admiral wrote that words were inadequate to express his admiration.'

## Where it's known from

Steven D. Smith and George J. Castille III, Bailey's Dam, Louisiana Department of Culture, Recreation and Tourism, Division of Archaeology, Anthropological Study No. 8 (Baton Rouge, March 1986), read as Project Gutenberg eBook 62052: 'the water was only 3 feet deep'; 'Even the lightest gunboats needed at least 7 feet of water to pass'; 'Ten of Porter's gunboats were trapped above the rapids'; 'they would have to be destroyed like the Eastport, otherwise they would be lost to the rebels'; Bailey spent some twenty years in Wisconsin building dams, mills and bridges, where lumbermen used dams to float logs to the mills; about 3,000 troops; the tree dam 'presented a dam face of logs angled upward with the stream flow', held down by the water's pressure; 'he built the dam of large wooden boxes called cribs', filled with stone; four coal barges sunk in the gap between the two halves; the river about 758 feet wide at the lower rapids; 'By May 6, the water held by the dam had risen 4 feet'; 'By May 8, the water level was up 5 feet 4 inches'; on 9 May 'at around 5:30 in the morning' an officer 'heard a great crashing in the direction of the dam'; 'Two of the barges used in the dam had broken loose, and the water was gushing through'; 'The Lexington succeeded in getting over the falls and then steered directly for the opening in the dam'; 'The Lexington's run was followed by the three gunboats waiting behind the dam'; 'he added smaller, lighter dams near the upper rapids'; the Mound City, the largest, ran the gap on 12 May; 'The next day all of the trapped vessels lay safely below the rapids'; Porter, as quoted there: 'without doubt the greatest engineering feat ever performed'; 'In 1976 the archaeological remains of Bailey's Dam were placed on the National Register of Historic Places'.
