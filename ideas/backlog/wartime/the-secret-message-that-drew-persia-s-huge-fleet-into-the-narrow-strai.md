# The secret message that drew Persia's huge fleet into the narrow strait at Salamis

- **Kind:** wartime, built as `true_story`

## The first second

**Heard:** Themistocles sends his enemy a secret message: the Greeks are about to run. It's bait.

**Seen:** A night-time map table of the sea by Athens, stamped 480 BC. A narrow strait runs between the mainland and the island of Salamis. Inside it, a small cluster of ship-shaped blocks under a paper tag on a pin: 'THEMISTOCLES, the Greek fleet, about 370 ships'. Out in the open water, a far bigger mass of ships: 'XERXES, king of Persia'. One tiny boat with a folded paper on it is crossing from the small fleet to the big one.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A small fleet of block ships tucked inside a narrow strait, a huge one outside, and one tiny boat crossing between them at night; then the huge fleet closing both ends of the strait and pouring into it until its ships jam. The jam reads without words; the message needs them. A map table: the silent test passes only softened.
- **What grabs them:** A commander secretly tells the enemy what his own side is planning, and how to trap his own fleet.
- **What's at stake:** The Greek fleet, about 370 ships and their crews, and the families of Athens sheltering on the island right behind it.
- **The answer you watch:** The tiny boat crossing in the dark; the big fleet splitting and sliding round to close both ends of the strait; the small fleet staying where it is, in line across the narrow water; the big fleet coming in, its rows bunching, turning sideways and catching on each other where the strait narrows; the small fleet's line moving forward into them; big-fleet ships tipping and being lifted away, and the rest backing out to open sea.

## How it goes

**War:** The Persian invasion of Greece in 480 BC: an alliance of Greek cities, led by Sparta and Athens, against the Persian Empire under King Xerxes. The sea battle of Salamis, in late September of that year.

**Problem:** Beat a fleet about twice the size of yours, or more, when half your own commanders want to sail away.

**How known:** Two early accounts. Aeschylus's play The Persians was staged in Athens eight years after the battle (472 BC: general knowledge, the page read doesn't give the date), by a man who, Wikipedia's article says, fought at Salamis. Its messenger says 'A Hellene, from the Athenian host, came to your son Xerxes and told this tale', that Xerxes saw through 'neither the cleverness of the Greek' nor the gods' ill will, and that he set his ships 'in triple line, to bar the exits and the sounding straits' (lines 355 to 368, Herbert Weir Smyth's translation, read at Perseus). It names neither Themistocles nor the messenger. Herodotus, writing about fifty years after (general knowledge), gives the names, the debate and the words of the message (book 8, chapters 40 to 89, read in G. C. Macaulay's translation on Wikisource). So a message from the Athenian side that the Greeks were about to flee is in both, one of them almost of the time; that it was Themistocles's doing, carried by Sicinnus, is Herodotus's, and the short's second line should say 'as Herodotus tells it'. What's argued, from Wikipedia's article on the battle (read; the books it cites were not opened): the Persian numbers (1,207 in both ancient accounts; 600 to 800 in J. F. Lazenby and others; 400 to 700 in another); whether the Persians entered the strait in the night or at dawn; and how real the Greek quarrel was (Tom Holland suggests it 'may have simply been a ruse'). Losses are estimates: the article has 'something in the range of 200-300' Persian ships and gives 40 Greek ships with no source. Its date is 26 or 27 September 480 BC. The Greek count is Herodotus's 378, or 380 with two that came over; Aeschylus has 310. Three things in the steps were first written from memory of Herodotus, then checked in Macaulay's translation: that Themistocles's 'opinion was like to be defeated by the Peloponnesians' when he sent the message (8.75); that Sicinnus was 'a servant of Themistocles and tutor to his children' (8.75); and that the Persian ships behind ran into their own ships as the front ones turned to flee (8.89). The crowding in the narrows is Aeschylus's, lines 413 to 416: 'none could render another aid, and each crashed its bronze prow against each of its own line'. The article does cite Diodorus for the Persians becoming 'disorganised and cramped' in the strait. That in open water the bigger fleet could surround the smaller is my paraphrase of the argument, not a quotation. That the Spartan Eurybiades held overall command is general knowledge. Left out: Themistocles's own later life, which ended in exile at the Persian court (general knowledge).

**Left today:** The strait itself, between the island of Salamis and the mainland west of Athens (general knowledge: what marks the battle there today wasn't checked). Aeschylus's play, which survives and is still staged.

**Shown as:** Ship-shaped blocks on a map of a strait: no figures, no hand. Themistocles and Xerxes are named on tags and never drawn. Ships that are rammed tip and are lifted off the table: no crews, nobody in the water. Herodotus says most of the Persian dead drowned, 'not being able to swim': the short neither shows nor says it.

**People:** Themistocles, commander of Athens's ships, the largest part of a Greek fleet that was the smaller side: he wanted the battle fought in the strait, with Athens's own families on the island behind it. Eurybiades of Sparta, who held the fleet's overall command, and the commanders from the Peloponnese: they wanted to fall back to the Isthmus of Corinth and guard their own land. Xerxes, king of Persia, with the bigger fleet: he wanted to catch the whole Greek fleet at once and end the war. Sicinnus, a slave in Themistocles's household, who carried the message.

**Lesson:** So when you're outnumbered, think of Themistocles: don't meet them in the open. Get the fight into the narrow place, where their numbers get in each other's way. The saying is his own argument to the other commanders, as Herodotus gives it: 'to fight a sea-fight in a narrow space is for our advantage'.

1. 480 BC: the Persian king Xerxes has marched into Greece. Athens has been emptied, its families shipped across to the island of Salamis and beyond, and the city is taken. The Greek fleet, about 370 ships, sits in the narrow strait beside the island. The Persian fleet outside is far bigger: both early accounts say 1,207 ships, and modern historians put it nearer 600 to 800.
2. The other Greek commanders want to pull back to the Isthmus of Corinth, nearer their own homes. Themistocles of Athens argues the opposite: 'to fight a sea-fight in a narrow space is for our advantage'. The argument is going against him.
3. So that night he sends a slave of his household, Sicinnus, across by boat with a message for the Persians: the Athenian commander is secretly on the king's side, the Greeks are frightened and 'planning to take flight', so block them in now.
4. Xerxes believes it. In the dark his fleet puts to sea and bars the ways out of the strait. By morning the Greeks are told: 'we are encompassed round by the enemy'. Nobody can leave now. They will have to fight in the strait, where Themistocles wanted to.
5. At dawn the Persian ships come on into the narrows. There are too many for the space. As Aeschylus tells it, crowded in the narrows, none can help another, and they crash their bronze prows into their own line, while the Greek ships close round and ram them. When the front ships turn to run, Herodotus adds, the ships behind, still pushing forward, run into them.
6. Herodotus: 'the greater number of their ships were disabled at Salamis'. Xerxes takes much of his army back to Asia, and the army he leaves behind is beaten on land the next year.

**The ending:** Morning over the strait: the big fleet's ships backing out to open water with gaps in their rows, the small fleet still in line across the narrows, and the island behind it untouched. Said: 'So when you're outnumbered, think of Themistocles: don't meet them in the open. Get the fight into the narrow place, where their numbers get in each other's way.'
