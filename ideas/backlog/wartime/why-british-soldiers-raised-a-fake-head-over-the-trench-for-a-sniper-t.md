# Why British soldiers raised a fake head over the trench for a sniper to shoot

## The first second

**Heard:** A sniper is watching this trench, so they raise a head for him.

**Seen:** Inside a First World War trench: a soldier crouched below the sandbags, pushing a stick upward so that a painted papier-mache head in a British cap rises slowly above the parapet, a lit cigarette in its mouth.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A soldier crouched in a trench, pushing a fake head on a stick up over the sandbags, a cigarette smoking in its mouth.
- **What grabs them:** Putting a head above the parapet is the one thing every man in that trench has learned never to do, and he's doing it on purpose, slowly.
- **What's at stake:** Every man in the trench who has to look over the top. Of German snipers early in the war, the book says: 'One of them might easily cause thirty or forty casualties.'
- **The answer you watch:** The bullet punching in at the front of the hollow head and out at the back; the head set back on its board, one periscope lower; an eye at the back hole looking through both holes and up the periscope, straight back along the bullet's path to a dark slot in the far sandbags.

## How it goes

**War:** First World War, the Western Front: the British Army's sniping schools, 1915 to 1918. Written down by Major H. Hesketh-Prichard, who ran the First Army's school.

**Problem:** Find one hidden rifleman somewhere in a long line of sandbags, when looking over the top for him is the very thing that gets you shot.

**How known:** Documented at first hand. Major H. Hesketh-Prichard, D.S.O., M.C., set up and ran the First Army's sniping school, and describes the method step by step in his own book, Sniping in France, chapter II. His figure, sixty-seven snipers found out of seventy-one, is from 'trials at First Army Sniping School', where the snipers were men posted for the test: the book gives no count from the front line. He writes of it as his own party's ('we found a means by which we were able at once to find the position of any German sniper'). He says the idea came from looking back along the path of a German sniper's bullets (the sniper he calls Ernst, 1915); the papier-mache heads themselves came from the French camouflage works at Amiens. The Western Front Association's article (2008, unsigned, no sources given) says the heads came from the London costumier Willy Clarkson; the book mentions 'dummy heads purchased at Clarkson's' only in passing.

**Left today:** Papier-mache dummy heads survive in museums. York Castle Museum holds one (YORCM 2013.73, 'World War One papier mache dummy head used to draw out sniper fire'), readable on Google Arts and Culture, as the fact-check found. One made by the Royal Engineers' camouflage section is among the Royal Engineers Museum's objects in Kent, and the Imperial War Museums hold a photograph of such heads being made (IWM 205183881): those two were not opened, their pages wouldn't load. The book is free to read on Project Gutenberg.

**Shown as:** The only thing ever hit is the papier-mache head: one neat hole in, one neat hole out. The sniper is never seen, only the dark slot he fires from, and the short stops the moment it's found. Nobody is shown shot, on either side.

1. A hidden sniper has the trench covered, and nobody can tell where he fires from.
2. Two stakes go into the trench's own parapet with a board fixed to them. In the board is a groove, and in the groove slides a stick with a hollow papier-mache head on top.
3. The head is pushed slowly up over the sandbags. Some men put a cigarette in its mouth and smoked it from below through a rubber tube.
4. The sniper fires. The bullet goes in at the front of the hollow head and out at the back, and leaves two holes.
5. The head is pulled down as a real man would drop, and its stick is put back in the same groove, lower by exactly the distance between the two glasses of a trench periscope.
6. The periscope's lower glass goes against the front hole, its upper glass above the parapet. An observer puts his eye to the back hole and looks along the bullet's own path, the other way: at the centre of the two holes is the place the sniper fired from.

**The ending:** Seen through the two holes and up the periscope: the far line of sandbags, and one dark slot in it ringed with light. The fake head sits on its board with a hole through it. Said: 'The bullet had drawn a straight line back to the man who fired it. In trials at the army's sniping school, the trick found sixty-seven hidden snipers out of seventy-one.'

## Where it's known from

Major H. Hesketh-Prichard, D.S.O., M.C., Sniping in France (first published 1920; the Project Gutenberg copy, eBook 68964, is E. P. Dutton's New York edition, 'Printed in Great Britain'; the year is from library catalogues, not from that page), chapter II, 'The Sniper in the Trenches': 'We selected a good place opposite to him, and drove two stakes into our own parapet until only about a foot of them remained uncovered'; a board with a groove nailed to the stakes; the head 'slowly pushed up above our parapet'; 'The entry and exit of the bullet made two holes, one in the front, and one in the back of the hollow dummy head'; pulled down in 'as natural a manner as possible'; the stick put back 'exactly the height between the two glasses of a periscope lower'; the observer 'looking along the path of the bullet, only in the opposite direction to that in which it had come'; some men 'placed a cigarette in its mouth, and smoked it through a rubber tube'; 'In trials at First Army Sniping School, we were able by this invention to locate sixty-seven snipers out of seventy-one'; 'One of them might easily cause thirty or forty casualties'. Chapter I: the French Camouflage Works at Amiens 'had made a number of papier-mache models of the heads and shoulders of British soldiers'. Second source: Western Front Association, 'Hesketh Vernon Hesketh-Prichard and the British School of Sniping on the Western Front', 21 Oct 2008. The card leaves out the book's measurements. That the head is dropped 'as a real man would drop' is the book's 'as natural a manner as possible'.

## What has to be made

- a trench with a sandbag parapet, and a far line of sandbags across open ground
- a hollow papier-mache head in a British cap, on a stick, cut open once to show it's hollow
- two stakes and a board with a groove, fixed in the parapet
- a cigarette and a rubber tube
- a box periscope with a glass at each end
- the bullet's path as one thin line of light through both holes
- one dark loophole in the far sandbags, ringed with light at the end
- two British soldiers in 1916 kit, crouching (invented stand-ins)
