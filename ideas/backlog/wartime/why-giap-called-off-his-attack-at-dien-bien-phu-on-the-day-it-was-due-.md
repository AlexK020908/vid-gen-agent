# Why Giap called off his attack at Dien Bien Phu on the day it was due, and hauled his guns back out

## The first second

**Heard:** For nearly ten days and nights his soldiers dragged their guns over a mountain by hand. Giap orders them dragged back.

**Seen:** A model of a long green valley on a table, ringed by steep jungle hills, stamped January 1954. On the valley floor, round an airstrip, a cluster of blue blocks under a paper tag on a pin: 'FRENCH FORTRESS, Dien Bien Phu'. Up in the hills round it sit small gun-shaped blocks, each on a thin rope line trailing back over the ridge, under a tag: 'GIAP'. One by one the guns begin to slide backwards, away from the valley.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** Small guns on rope lines sitting in a ring on the hills above a fortress, and then sliding backwards, away from it. A viewer sees a siege being undone, and why needs words. A model on a table: the silent test passes only softened.
- **What grabs them:** An army that has just dragged heavy guns over a mountain by hand is told, on the day of the attack, to drag them all back.
- **What's at stake:** Giap's whole army, in its first assault on a fortress this size, when the other choice was a fast attack he didn't believe it could win; and the French garrison in the valley, 10,800 men on the day the guns opened.
- **The answer you watch:** Gun-shaped blocks inching along rope lines over a ridge under a counter of days; a clock set to 5 pm, 25 January, with the attack arrows drawn and waiting; the arrows fading and the guns sliding back out; a counter of weeks while the guns go back in one by one, each sinking into a shelter in the hillside, and trench lines creep down toward the fortress; then flashes all round the rim, bursts on the airstrip, a model transport plane turning away, and the trench ring closing until the fortress's blocks turn pale.

## How it goes

**War:** The First Indochina War: the Viet Minh, led by Ho Chi Minh, against France. The battle of Dien Bien Phu, in the hills of north-west Vietnam near Laos, 13 March to 7 May 1954.

**Problem:** Take a fortress that can be fed from the air, with an army that has never tried anything like it, and not lose that army trying.

**How known:** Documented, mostly from the Vietnamese side and from Giap's own later telling. The timetable and his reasons are from the entry 'Dien Bien Phu: cancellation of first attack' in the historical dictionary of the Indochina War kept at the Universite du Quebec a Montreal (the historian Christopher Goscha's project; the entry is unsigned and lists no sources): the attack set for 17:00 on 25 January 1954, a soldier of the 312th Division captured on the 24th so that the French learned the hour, a delay of 24 hours and then the cancellation, his three reasons, the pressure on him to go ahead, and the new rule. The date 26 January is from Nhan Dan (7 May 2025, by a retired major general of Vietnam's National Defence Academy), which also says the troops pulled the artillery into position and 'then pulled it out again', and gives 61,800 Viet Minh soldiers, more than 261,000 labourers and a French garrison of 16,200 at its peak. The hauling by hand, the guns 'each weighing over two tonnes', the 15 km stretch, 'nearly ten days and nights', and that pulling them out 'proved to be even more daunting' are from Viet Nam News (7 May 2024). These are Vietnam's state papers writing about its own victory. 'The hardest decision of his military career' is Wikipedia's sentence, footnoted there to Giap, whose memoir I did not open. A state news agency piece (VNA, 7 May 2019) dates his change of mind to 23 January, on a visit to the front. The French side's confidence is from Air and Space Forces Magazine (Rebecca Grant, 1 August 2004): Navarre was 'convinced that China would not give Giap heavy guns', the Viet Minh were not expected to get them onto the hills, and French counter-battery fire was expected to silence them; the same article says daylight use of the airstrip stopped after the first bombardment, and that France 'realized that it had lost Indochina and made clear that it would fight no more'. French numbers are from Wikipedia's article on the battle with its citations: about 10,800 in the valley on 13 March, and 11,721 taken prisoner at the end. Told level, four things belong beside the win. It cost Giap's army far more men than it cost France (Vietnamese figures there: 4,020 dead and 9,118 wounded; French estimates: 8,000 dead). Many of the prisoners died in captivity: 'Only 3,290 were returned four months later'. How much of the plan and the guns came from China is argued: Navarre blamed Chinese advice and supply, and the dictionary names the chief Chinese adviser, Wei Guoqing, as agreeing with the decision to cancel. And at the battle itself Giap had far more men and guns than the fortress: 'the weaker side' is true of the war, not of this valley. A soldier, To Vinh Dien, died stopping a gun that was sliding during the haul out: Vietnam honours him, and the short doesn't show it. Not opened: Giap's memoir, Bernard Fall's or Martin Windrow's histories, or any French army record.

**Left today:** At Dien Bien Phu: a group of statues on Bo Hom hill, about 20 km north of the city, to the gun crews, and a cannon-hauling monument with a small museum beside it showing things used in the haul (Viet Nam News, 7 May 2024). The Dien Bien Phu Victory Museum is named on another card here.

**Shown as:** A model valley with blocks: gun-shaped blocks on rope lines, the fortress as blocks round an airstrip, trenches as lines that creep. No figures, no hand. Giap and Navarre are named on tags and never drawn. The guns' fire is small flashes on the hills and bursts on the empty airstrip: nothing lands on a person. The fall of the fortress is its blocks turning pale and its tag lifting away. The soldier who died under a gun, and what became of the prisoners, are not shown.

**People:** Vo Nguyen Giap, commander of the Viet Minh's army, the side France expected to outgun in any set battle: he wanted a victory he could be certain of, and the dictionary's entry says he was under orders to attack only if total victory was sure. Ho Chi Minh, the Viet Minh's leader, who (same entry) insisted the battle could not be lost. General Henri Navarre, France's commander in Indochina: he wanted to block the road into Laos and draw Giap's army into a set battle in the valley. Wei Guoqing, head of the Chinese advisers with Giap's army: the entry says advisers pressed for the quick attack, and names him as agreeing with the decision to call it off.

**Lesson:** So when everything's ready and you still aren't sure, think of Giap: calling it off costs less than losing. Be sure first, then strike. The saying is the four words of his new order, in Vietnamese 'đánh chắc, tiến chắc': attack surely, advance surely.

1. November 1953: France drops paratroops into the valley of Dien Bien Phu and builds a fortress round an airstrip, to block the road into Laos and pull Giap's army into a big battle it expects to win. France's commander, Navarre, is sure Giap can't get heavy guns onto the hills round the valley, and his officers that if he does, French guns will silence them.
2. Giap's army brings the guns anyway: along roads cut for lorries, and then, for a stretch of about 15 kilometres over a mountain, by hand, each gun weighing over two tonnes. It takes nearly ten days and nights. The attack is set for 5 pm on 25 January 1954, and the plan is to win fast.
3. Giap isn't sure. His army has never attacked a fortress this big, never fired this many guns together, never fought on through daylight for days. The French have dug in deeper, and a captured soldier has given them the hour. Advisers and staff press him to go, the men are keyed up, and tens of thousands of porters are worn out.
4. 26 January: he calls it off and has the guns hauled back out, which proves harder than hauling them in. He later called it the hardest decision of his military career. The new order: attack surely, advance surely.
5. Nearly seven weeks of digging follow. The guns go back in, into hidden shelters dug into the slopes facing the valley, and trenches creep down toward the fortress.
6. 13 March 1954, 5 pm: the guns open fire. From that first bombardment no aircraft can use the airstrip by day, and soon the fortress is living on parachute drops. The trenches close in for eight weeks. On 7 May it falls, and France decides to fight no more in Indochina.

**The ending:** The valley on 7 May 1954: trench lines closed tight round the airstrip, the fortress's blocks gone pale and its tag lifting away, the guns still hidden in the hillsides they were hauled into twice. Said: 'So when everything's ready and you still aren't sure, think of Giap: calling it off costs less than losing. Be sure first. Then strike.'
