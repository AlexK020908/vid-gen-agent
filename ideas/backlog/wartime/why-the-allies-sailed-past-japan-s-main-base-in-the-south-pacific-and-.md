# Why the Allies sailed past Japan's main base in the South Pacific and never stormed it

## The first second

**Heard:** This is the enemy's great fortress in the South Pacific, and the fleet sails straight past it.

**Seen:** A map table of the South Pacific. On one island sits a fortress piled high with red blocks, guns pointing out to sea, a harbour full of model ships. A hand moves a model troopship right past it, toward a small bare island beyond.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A hand moving a model troopship straight past a fortress island bristling with guns and blocks, to land on an empty island behind it. A map table, not a bizarre sight: what makes a viewer stop is only that the ship goes past.
- **What grabs them:** Leaving the enemy's great fortress, full of soldiers, unbeaten behind your own advance.
- **What's at stake:** The men who would have had to storm it. The official history's last word on it: the Allies 'were able to use the bypass technique with its concomitant savings in lives and time'.
- **The answer you watch:** Model ships landing on small islands round the fortress, one after another; an airstrip appearing on each; lines from the airstrips closing into a ring; the fortress's own ships and planes lifted off the table; the pile of blocks left standing and turning grey while the hand moves the fleet on up the map.

## How it goes

**War:** Second World War, the South-West Pacific: Operation Cartwheel, June 1943 to March 1944. The United States, Australia and New Zealand against Japan's base at Rabaul, on the island of New Britain.

**Problem:** Get past a fortress held by tens of thousands of soldiers without paying what it would cost to storm it.

**How known:** Documented in the US Army's official history: John Miller, Jr., CARTWHEEL: The Reduction of Rabaul (United States Army in World War II, The War in the Pacific; Office of the Chief of Military History, Department of the Army, Washington, 1959), chapter XII, section 'The Decision To Bypass Rabaul', and chapter XVIII, both read. Every quotation in the steps is from those two chapters. The surrender is from the Australian War Memorial's photograph P02592.001, read: the ceremony 'aboard aircraft carrier HMS Glory' on 6 September 1945 'signified the surrender of all Japanese Forces in New Guinea, New Britain and the Solomans'. What is softer. The numbers: the history's 'some 100,000' is for the whole bypassed area, not Rabaul alone; Wikipedia gives about 110,000 for Rabaul with a 'citation needed' tag, and a search summary of an Australian collection gave 139,000 for the surrender; so the card says 'tens of thousands' for the garrison and keeps the history's phrase for the area. That Rabaul was Japan's 'main base' in the South Pacific is the whole premise of the official history, not a sentence I can quote from the pages read. The dates of the other landings (Bougainville from 1 November 1943, Arawe 15 December, Cape Gloucester 26 December) and of the last planes leaving Rabaul (19 February 1944) are from Wikipedia's 'Neutralisation of Rabaul', not opened further. What is argued. Whose idea it was: 'leapfrogging' is often credited to MacArthur (general knowledge, not opened here), but the official history has him arguing in 1943 that Rabaul must be captured, and Marshall and the Washington planners pressing to go round it. And whether the Australian campaigns of 1944 to 1945 against bypassed garrisons were needed is argued by Australian historians (general knowledge, not opened here). It was not bloodless: the landings round it were fought for, and Wikipedia has Rabaul bombed until 8 August 1945. The phrase 'wither on the vine' is commonly used of it; it isn't in the pages I read, so the card doesn't quote it.

**Left today:** Where the surrender can be seen: the Australian War Memorial's photograph of the surrender party off Rabaul on 6 September 1945 (P02592.001), in the public domain. It shows named officers, so it is not shown in the short. At Rabaul, the tunnels the garrison dug against the bombing ('many kilometers of tunnels', Wikipedia; what a visitor can see there now wasn't checked). And the words 'island hopping', which came into English from this campaign and the ones after it (general knowledge, not checked).

**Shown as:** A map table: blocks, model ships and planes, a hand. The fortress is cut off by lines closing round it, not by anything seen hitting people. No bombing in close-up, no hunger, no bodies. The garrison is a pile of blocks that stays to the end.

1. Rabaul is Japan's main base in the South Pacific: a deep harbour, airfields and a garrison in the tens of thousands. General MacArthur wants it taken. It 'would have to be captured rather than just neutralized'.
2. In Washington in June 1943, planners argue that cutting it off would be a 'considerable economy of force' beside storming it. On 21 July General Marshall proposes seizing islands beyond it instead, with the 'purpose of isolating Rabaul'.
3. That August the Combined Chiefs of Staff approve: Rabaul is to be 'neutralized, not captured'.
4. So the landings go in around it, where the defence is thinner, and airfields follow. The last is Emirau, on 20 March 1944, where the Marines went peacefully ashore.
5. From those fields and from carriers, aircraft 'reduced Rabaul to impotence'. Its ships and planes go. Its soldiers stay, with nothing in reach to attack and no way out.
6. The US Army's history counts some 100,000 Japanese bypassed and, for practical purposes, out of the war.

**The ending:** The table on 6 September 1945, the date on the picture: a ring of small islands with airstrips, the fleet long gone north, the fortress still standing with its blocks in place, and one model carrier, HMS Glory, lying off it, where the surrender is signed. Said: 'They never stormed it. They went round it, cut it off and left it standing. The war ended with the fortress still full.'
