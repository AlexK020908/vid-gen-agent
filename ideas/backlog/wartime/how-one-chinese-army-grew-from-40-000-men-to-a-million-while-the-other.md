# How one Chinese army grew from 40,000 men to a million while the other fought Japan's big battles

- **Kind:** wartime, built as `true_story`
- **Your answer so far:** "the chinese 40k into 1 million one" (2026-11-13)

## The first second

**Heard:** This army entered a war with 40,000 men. It left with a million.

**Seen:** A war-room map table of China, 1937. Along the coast and the big rivers, a tall stack of blue blocks stands face to face with a line of white blocks marked with a red disc. Far off in the dry hills of the north-west sits one small red block, alone. A hand lifts a blue block off the table.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A hand taking blocks off a tall blue stack at a front line, while far behind the enemy's line a single red block turns into a pile. It reads without words as one shrinking and one growing, but it is a map table, not a bizarre sight: the silent test passes only that far.
- **What grabs them:** Armies shrink in wars. This one fights as guerrillas, away from the big battles, and comes out twenty-five times the size it went in.
- **What's at stake:** China, and the soldiers of both armies. The Nationalist government's own tables count over 3.2 million of its men killed, wounded or missing in the eight years.
- **The answer you watch:** A hand taking blue blocks off the table battle after battle while the tall stack shortens; a red stain spreading through the spaces between the railway lines Japan holds, with small red raids flickering along those lines year after year; one big burst of red arrows at the railways in 1940, and no second one; the red stack growing to a million; then red arrows crossing the whole map.

## How it goes

**War:** The war between China and Japan, 1937 to 1945, and the Chinese civil war that followed, 1946 to 1949. Three armies: Japan's, the Nationalist government's under Chiang Kai-shek, and the Communist Party's under Mao Zedong.

**Problem:** Fight an invader with an army too small and too poorly armed to meet it in open battle, and still be standing when the war ends. In Mao's words of 1938: 'this large and weak country is being attacked by a small and strong country'.

**How known:** What is documented, and what is a reading. Documented: who fought the big battles. Taiwan's government counts '22 major battles, over 1,000 large-scale campaigns, and over 38,000 smaller engagements' fought by the Nationalist government's forces (President Ma Ying-jeou, 29 Aug 2015, Office of the President, read), and that government's own tables give 1,319,958 killed, 1,761,335 wounded and 130,116 missing (Hsu Long-hsuen, as cited on Wikipedia, not opened; Ma's speech says over 3.22 million 'lost their lives'). Wikipedia's article on the war says the Nationalists were 'the main combatants opposing the Japanese in each of the 22 major battles' and, in a sentence that carries a footnote, that Communist forces 'usually avoided pitched battles with the Japanese, in which their guerrilla tactics were less effective, and generally limited their combat to guerrilla actions', with the Hundred Regiments Offensive and Pingxingguan as 'notable exceptions'. Beijing's own statement agrees on who fought at the front: Hu Jintao's speech of 3 September 2005 says 'As the main force on frontal battlefields, the KMT army organized a series of major campaigns... which dealt heavy blows to the Japanese army', and the same speech says the CPC waged 'extensive guerrilla warfare' in the rear and 'became the mainstay' (found and read by the fact-check, not by me: https://www.mfa.gov.cn/eng/xw/zyjh/202405/t20240530_11339773.html). Documented: the plan. Mao Zedong, 'Problems of Strategy in Guerrilla War Against Japan', May 1938 (Selected Works, volume II, read at marxists.org), quoted in the steps. Documented, by the Party's own count: the growth, from 'just over 40,000 men' in 1937 to 'a great army of one million' by 1945 (the editors' note to that text; Wikipedia's table has 1.2 million for 1945). Because the hook's two numbers are the Party's own, the second line heard says 'by its own count'. The readings. Taiwan's government holds that Mao's communists 'only used the war to strengthen their own forces': those words are Reuters' paraphrase of its position, not a quotation of an official. Beijing's position is that communist guerrillas were the 'backbone' of the fighting in China, again in Reuters' words. Both are from Reuters, 5 Sep 2025, read as carried by The Standard. Among historians, Rana Mitter's China's War with Japan (2013; Forgotten Ally in America), as two reviewers give it: 'While the communists were quite effective in guerrilla warfare', 'Mao preferred to keep his forces in reserve for the post-war battle for supremacy in China' (Sir Hugh Cortazzi, Japan Society review, read); 'the Communist side fought exactly one major battle against the Japanese, and a number of limited guerrilla actions.' (Jonathan Mirsky, The Spectator, 20 June 2013, read). Those are reviewers' words about the book, which I did not open. Cortazzi adds a sentence of his own, not Mitter's: 'The war enabled the Chinese Communist Party to seize power and destroy the Chinese Nationalists'. Pulling the other way, Hans van de Ven is cited on Wikipedia's article on Operation Ichi-Go (his books not opened) for the view that Japan's 1944 offensive, about 500,000 men, did damage to the Nationalists that shaped the post-war order: that puts the weight on what Japan did to one army, more than on what the other army chose. Mao's own later words are on record: to Japanese visitors on 10 July 1964, that the Japanese Imperial Army 'has been a good instructor for us' (Mao Zedong sixiang wansui, 1969, as translated on Wikipedia's article on these remarks, which cites the Chinese collections; whether he meant it as dark humour is argued there). Left out on purpose: the line often quoted as Mao's, '70 percent for self development, 20 percent for compromise, and 10 percent against Japan'. Wikipedia carries it with a 'citation needed' tag and I found no document for it. The owner's word 'hiding' is one side's reading and the card doesn't use it: what the 1938 text orders is guerrilla war behind the lines, and whether that was the only way a small army could fight or a way of saving itself for the next war is exactly what's argued. The 1940 offensive is 105 regiments and about 200,000 troops on Wikipedia, in an article that carries a neutrality-dispute tag; other accounts say 400,000, so the card gives regiments only. That 'nothing on that scale is tried again' follows Mirsky's 'exactly one major battle' and Wikipedia's 'notable exceptions'. A claim seen only in a search summary: that Communist Party historians have called the Nationalists' role 'important' and their own the 'cornerstone'. Not opened: Mitter's and van de Ven's books, Yang Kuisong's work on the guerrilla war, Chalmers Johnson's Peasant Nationalism (1962), Lloyd Eastman's Seeds of Destruction, or any Japanese army record (a Japanese defence-institute paper on the fighting in north China was found but couldn't be read).

**Left today:** The argument itself, every year. On the war's 80th anniversary, in September 2025, Beijing called its guerrillas the 'backbone' of the fight and Taiwan's government answered that Mao's communists 'only used the war to strengthen their own forces' (both in Reuters' words, not quotations of officials). The two governments this story ends with still face each other across the Taiwan Strait, which makes this a live political quarrel as well as history.

**Shown as:** Blocks on a map. No battle is shown, no city, no civilians, nobody hurt: losses are blocks lifted off a table. No leader's face: Mao and Chiang Kai-shek are named, never drawn. Neither army is called brave or cowardly, the word 'hiding' isn't used, and the short says who holds each reading.

1. In 1937 Japan's army comes down the coast and up the rivers. The Nationalist government's army, the blue blocks, meets it in the big battles and is pushed back with heavy loss, year after year.
2. The Communist army is small: just over 40,000 men, by its own count. In May 1938 Mao writes its plan down: Japan 'does not have sufficient soldiers and has to leave many gaps in the occupied areas'. So go into the gaps behind its lines and build bases there: guerrilla units 'must preserve themselves and, moreover, they must expand', and his next sentence speaks of 'preserving and expanding ourselves and destroying the enemy'.
3. Japan holds the cities and the railways. In the countryside between them the red blocks fight a guerrilla war, raid by raid, and spread. How much that cost Japan is what the two sides still dispute.
4. In August 1940 that army fights its one big offensive in the open: the Hundred Regiments Offensive, 105 regiments against the railways of the north. Japan's answer falls hard on the base areas, and nothing on that scale is tried again.
5. In 1944 Japan sends about half a million men against the Nationalist armies, the largest land operation it ever ran. The blue stack loses hundreds of thousands more, and the red moves into the country left open behind.
6. 1945: Japan surrenders. The blue stack is worn down and the red one is about a million. Four years of civil war later the red blocks hold the mainland, and the Nationalist government is on Taiwan.

**The ending:** The table in 1949: red blocks across the mainland, and what is left of the blue stack on the island of Taiwan. Said: 'Taiwan's government says that army used the war to build its strength. Beijing says its guerrillas were the backbone of the fight. What both governments' records agree on: one army fought the big set-piece battles, and the other fought as guerrillas behind the lines and was still growing when they ended.'

## To settle before it's written

- The silent test passes only softened: coloured blocks on a table need words.
- The ending touches a quarrel between Beijing and Taipei that is still live, and the civil war it ends on never closed by treaty: the owner judges whether that's inside the line on wars still being fought.
