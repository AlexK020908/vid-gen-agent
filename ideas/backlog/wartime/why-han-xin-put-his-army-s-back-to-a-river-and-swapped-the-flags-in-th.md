# Why Han Xin put his army's back to a river, and swapped the flags in the enemy's empty camp

- **Kind:** wartime, built as `true_story`
- **Your answer so far:** not shown to you yet

## The first second

**Heard:** Han Xin lines his army up with its back to a river. The enemy laughs.

**Seen:** A map table of north China, stamped 205 BC: a mountain range with one narrow pass through it, opening east onto a plain with a river. Out on the plain a small row of red blocks stands with the river right behind it, under a paper tag on a pin: 'HAN XIN, general of Han'. Facing it, a walled camp packed with tan blocks, tan flags along its walls: 'CHEN YU, army of Zhao, said to be 200,000'.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A small row of blocks set with a river at its back, facing a walled camp many times its size; the big army pours out after it, and behind it every flag on the camp's wall turns red. The flags changing colour read without words; why the small row is at the river doesn't. A map table: the silent test passes only softened.
- **What grabs them:** An outnumbered army drawn up with a river behind it, so that it cannot retreat: the one position a general is taught never to take. The record says the enemy laughed.
- **What's at stake:** Han Xin's whole army, with the river behind it and an army said to be many times its size in front: if the line breaks, there is nowhere to go.
- **The answer you watch:** A short line of small red flags going round through the hills in the dark; a red row stepping out to the river's edge; the camp gates opening and tan blocks pouring out; the red general's block running back to the river, and the rest of the tan blocks leaving the camp empty to chase it; the row at the river holding; red flags popping up along the empty camp's walls, one after another; the tan blocks turning, stopping and scattering.

## How it goes

**War:** The war between Chu and Han, 206 to 202 BC, out of which China's Han dynasty was founded. Han Xin, a general of Liu Bang, the king of Han, against the kingdom of Zhao, at the Jingxing pass (in today's Hebei) in 205 BC.

**Problem:** Beat an army many times the size of yours, waiting in a walled camp at the far end of a mountain pass, with soldiers who are new and likely to run.

**How known:** One source, told in detail: Sima Qian's Shiji, chapter 92 (the biography of the Marquis of Huaiyin), written about a hundred years after the battle and read in Chinese on Wikisource. Every step and quotation in the card is from it, in my own translation. In his closing note Sima Qian says he went to Han Xin's home town, Huaiyin, and heard about him from the people there. I know of no second account that doesn't come from his (the later Han history is generally said to follow him: not opened). So this is 'as Sima Qian tells it', and the short's second line should say so. The numbers are soft, and the text itself shows it: Zhao's army is 'said to be 200,000' (號稱), and Han Xin's is called tens of thousands but, in Chen Yu's mouth, 'really no more than a few thousand'. The bait, in his telling, is the general's flags and drums dropped in a pretended flight (趙見我走，必空壁逐我; 詳弃鼓旗); nothing says Han Xin wanted Zhao to laugh. Only the 2,000 riders and the 10,000 at the river are given as plain counts. The year 205 BC, and the month, October, are from Wikipedia's article on the battle, which cites the Shiji and William Nienhauser's translation (2019), not opened. I found no historian who disputes that the battle went this way, and read none who has tested it either. What Han Xin told his officers afterwards is in the same chapter: they asked why he had broken the rule of keeping hills behind and water in front, and he answered that it is in the Art of War ('throw them where they must die, and they live'), and that his men were untrained, 'like people driven from the market to fight', so on ground with a way out they would all have run. The chapter says Chen Yu was killed and Zhao's king captured, which the short doesn't show.

**Left today:** The idiom 背水一战, 'a battle with your back to the water', used in Chinese for any fight with no way back, and its Japanese twin 背水の陣 (both in Wikipedia's article on the battle, read). The pass is at Jingxing in Hebei: what a visitor can see there wasn't checked.

**Shown as:** Blocks, flags on pins and a river on a map: no figures, no hand. Han Xin and Chen Yu are named on tags and never drawn. The fighting is two rows of blocks pressed together with small bursts of light between them. Zhao's defeat is its blocks scattering off the table. The deaths the record names are not shown.

**People:** Han Xin, general of Han, on the smaller side: he wanted to take the kingdom of Zhao with a small and mostly raw army. As a poor young man, Sima Qian says, he crawled between a bully's legs in the market of Huaiyin sooner than fight him, and 'the whole market laughed at him and thought him a coward'; years later he made that man an officer, saying that killing him would have won him no name, 'so I bore it, and got here'. Zhang Er, his fellow commander. Chen Yu, lord of Cheng'an, commanding Zhao's army: he wanted an open fight and said a righteous army has no use for tricks. Li Zuoche, lord of Guangwu, his adviser: he wanted to hold the walls and starve Han Xin in the pass, and was refused (after the battle Han Xin had him found and treated him as his teacher). Zhao Xie, king of Zhao.

**Lesson:** So when they laugh at your move, think of Han Xin: what looks like a blunder can be the plan. His own lesson to his officers was the other half: his men were raw, so he put them where running wasn't possible. He quotes the Art of War for it: 陷之死地而后生, 'throw them where they must die, and they live'. The idiom 背水一战, 'a battle with your back to the water', comes from this day. The Art of War line is in Sima Qian's chapter; his words for the river line are 背水陳, 'drawn up with their backs to the water', and the four-word idiom came later. The owner's 先当孙子，后当爷 isn't tied to Han Xin in any source I found, though the market story is its picture.

1. Zhao's army waits in a walled camp where the Jingxing pass opens onto the plain, 'said to be 200,000'. Han Xin has a few tens of thousands at most, many of them raw. An adviser tells Zhao's commander, Chen Yu, to hold the walls and cut Han Xin's supplies in the pass, where 'carts cannot go two abreast'. Chen Yu refuses: a righteous army does not use tricks. Han Xin's spies bring him the news, and only then does he dare to enter the pass.
2. In the night he sends 2,000 light horsemen round by hidden paths to watch the camp, each carrying one red flag. Their orders: when Zhao sees me run, it will empty its walls to chase me. Ride in, pull down Zhao's flags and raise Han's red ones.
3. Before dawn he sends 10,000 men out first and draws them up with their backs to the river, where an army has nowhere to fall back to. 'The army of Zhao saw it and laughed out loud.'
4. At dawn Han Xin marches out under his general's flags and drums, and Zhao opens its gates and attacks. Then he pretends to abandon the flags and drums, and runs back to the line at the river.
5. Zhao empties its camp to seize the flags and chase him. At the river Han's men have no way out, so they 'all fought to the death and could not be beaten'. Behind Zhao's back, the 2,000 riders gallop into the empty camp and raise 2,000 red flags.
6. Zhao's soldiers give up on the river line and turn for home. Every wall of their camp is red. They think their king has already been taken, and the army breaks and runs.

**The ending:** The walled camp with a red flag on every wall and nothing inside it; between the camp and the river the tan blocks turn, stop, and scatter off the table. The red row at the river is whole. Said: 'So when they laugh at your move, think of Han Xin: what looked like a blunder was the plan. With the river at their backs, his raw soldiers couldn't run, so they held. In Chinese, a fight with no way back is still called a battle with your back to the water.'

## Where it's known from

Sima Qian, Shiji, chapter 92, 淮陰侯列傳, read on Chinese Wikisource. The camp: '趙王、成安君陳餘聞漢且襲之也，聚兵井陘口，號稱二十萬'. Li Zuoche: '今井陘之道，車不得方軌，騎不得成列'; '願足下假臣奇兵三萬人，從閒道絕其輜重'. Chen Yu: '成安君，儒者也，常稱義兵不用詐謀奇計'; '今韓信兵號數萬，其實不過數千'. '韓信使人閒視，知其不用，還報，則大喜，乃敢引兵遂下'. The riders: '選輕騎二千人，人持一赤幟，從閒道萆山而望趙軍，誡曰：趙見我走，必空壁逐我，若疾入趙壁，拔趙幟，立漢赤幟'. The river: '信乃使萬人先行，出，背水陳。趙軍望見而大笑'. The flight: '於是信、張耳詳弃鼓旗，走水上軍'. '軍皆殊死戰，不可敗'. The flags: '則馳入趙壁，皆拔趙旗，立漢赤幟二千'; '壁皆漢赤幟，而大驚，以爲漢皆已得趙王將矣，兵遂亂，遁走'. After: '斬成安君泜水上，禽趙王歇'; '兵法不曰「陷之死地而後生，置之亡地而後存」'; '此所謂「驅市人而戰之」，其勢非置之死地，使人人自爲戰；今予之生地，皆走'; of Li Zuoche, '東鄉坐，西鄉對，師事之'. The market: '信能死，刺我；不能死，出我袴下'; '俛出袴下，蒲伏。一市人皆笑信，以爲怯'; later, '此壯士也。方辱我時，我寧不能殺之邪？殺之無名，故忍而就於此'. Sima Qian's note: '吾如淮陰，淮陰人爲余言'. Wikipedia, 'Battle of Jingxing', for the date and the idiom: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Jingxing 'A few tens of thousands at most' puts the chapter's two figures together.
