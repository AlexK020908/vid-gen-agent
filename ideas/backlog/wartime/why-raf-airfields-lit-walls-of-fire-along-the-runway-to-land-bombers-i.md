# Why RAF airfields lit walls of fire along the runway to land bombers in fog

## The first second

**Heard:** A bomber is coming home, so they set the runway's edges on fire.

**Seen:** Night, and thick fog. A long runway with a wall of orange flame down each side, the fog glowing above it, and a four-engined bomber dropping out of the murk between the two lines of fire.

## Why someone would stop

- **With the sound off:** A runway at night with a wall of fire down each side, and a big bomber coming down between them through fog.
- **What grabs them:** A bomber is trying to land, and they've lit fires down both sides of the runway.
- **What's at stake:** The crew in each bomber, seven men in a Lancaster or a Halifax, short of fuel, with fog over the airfields they could reach.
- **The answer you watch:** Fog thinning and opening into a clear slot over the runway as the two lines of flame burn; the glow showing through the fog from above; the bomber coming down into the clear air between the fires.

## How it goes

**War:** Second World War: Royal Air Force airfields in Britain, most of them Bomber Command's, 1943 to 1945.

**Problem:** Get a bomber down safely when fog hides the runway and it hasn't the fuel to go anywhere else.

**How known:** Documented. The RAF Museum's own page (object X001-4342) gives the order from Churchill to the Petroleum Warfare Department in 1942, the pipes and petrol burners, 100,000 gallons (456,000 litres) an hour, 15 airfields in Britain, 2,500 aircraft landed safely in fog and the lives of 10,000 aircrew. Those last two are the museum's figures, not traced here to a record of the time, and the airmen figure varies (RAF Museum 10,000; Wikipedia 'possibly 11,000'; Invention and Technology '15,000 men in 2,500 planes'). Accounts give different dates for the first real landing in fog, so the card gives none. That the flames 'evaporated the fog' is from Jeffrey W. Miller, 'Fogbusters', Invention and Technology, Fall 1993 (https://www.inventionandtech.com/node/85580), read by the fact-check: the RAF Museum says only 'sufficient heat to lift fog'. The standard histories (Sir Donald Banks, Flame Over Britain, 1946; Geoffrey Williams, Flying Through Fire, 1995) were not opened.

**Left today:** Nothing in use: it burned too much petrol for peacetime. The last installation, at RAF Manston, was available for emergencies as late as 1952 (Wikipedia) and was removed in 1959 (RAF Museum). The museum holds an air diagram of the system, and the Science Museum Group holds FIDO papers from 1943. Airliners now land in fog by radio beam and autopilot instead, which is general knowledge and wasn't checked here.

**Shown as:** No harm on screen. The danger is a bomber circling in fog with nowhere to land. The fire is beside the runway and never on the aircraft, and no crash is shown.

1. Bombers come back from a night raid short of fuel, and fog has closed in over the airfields. A crew that can't see the runway can't land on it.
2. Along both sides of the runway run pipes with rows of burner jets. Petrol is pumped through them and a man lights the first burner: each stretch catches with a whoosh, until there's a wall of flame down each side.
3. The heat warms the air over the runway. Fog is tiny drops of water hanging in the air, and in the warmed air they dry up, so a clear slot opens in the fog above the tarmac.
4. From above, the glow shows through the fog and marks the runway like a flare path. The pilot lines up on it and comes down into the clear air between the two fires.
5. It burned 100,000 gallons of petrol an hour. But by the RAF Museum's count, 2,500 aircraft landed safely in fog this way between 1943 and 1945.

**The ending:** The bomber rolls to a stop between the two lines of fire. The burners die down, one stretch after another, and the fog closes back over the dark runway. Said: 'The fire never touched the plane. It burned the fog away. The RAF Museum counts 2,500 aircraft landed like this, and the lives of 10,000 airmen.'

## Where it's known from

Royal Air Force Museum, 'Worth a Thousand Words: Air Diagrams', Fog dispersal (collection ref X001-4342, 1943 to 1945): FIDO, 'a network of pipes and petrol burners capable of clearing fog from runways'; ordered by Churchill from the Petroleum Warfare Department in 1942 because fog on home airfields endangered aircraft returning from bombing operations; burned petrol at 100,000 gallons (456,000 litres) per hour; installed at 15 airfields around the UK; between 1943 and 1945, 2,500 aircraft landed safely in fog, 'saving the lives of 10,000 aircrew'; the last installation, at RAF Manston, removed in 1959. How it was lit and what it looked like: the RAF Pathfinders Archive's FIDO page ('a jet of petrol spurted forth when the pumps were in operation'; a man lit the first burner and the section caught 'with a whoosh'; 'the glow of the flames provided a flarepath'): https://raf-pathfinders.com/fido/ Papers on FIDO, 1943: Science Museum Group: https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/documents/aa110067108 That the flames 'evaporated the fog' is from Jeffrey W. Miller, 'Fogbusters', Invention and Technology, Fall 1993, read by the fact-check: https://www.inventionandtech.com/node/85580 The RAF Museum says only 'sufficient heat to lift fog'. Seven men to a Lancaster or Halifax is general knowledge, not from these pages. The hook's 'set the runway's edges on fire' means the burners along both sides: the runway itself didn't burn.
