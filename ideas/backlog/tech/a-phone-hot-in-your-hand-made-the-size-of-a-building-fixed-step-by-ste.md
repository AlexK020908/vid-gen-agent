## How it goes

**Everyday:** Feeling your phone get hot in your hand while it plays a game or charges.

**Goal:** Keep a building full of computers from cooking itself.

**Invention:** the liquid cooling of an AI data centre

**Seen it:** Every time you ask an AI chatbot a question.

**Cycle:** chip heats, water carries, tower cools

**Inside:** The hall with its roof off: cold air as blue balls, hot air as red; then one AI rack made glass, water running through plates on each of the big chips, glowing warmer as it picks up the heat, then handing that heat to the building's own water, which runs up to the towers, where a mist rises off and the rest runs back cool.

1. All the power the computers use turns to heat, so the hall heats like an oven and the chips slow down and shut off (on Britain's hottest day, 19 July 2022, Google switched off part of a London data centre when its cooling failed). So giant air conditioners blow cold air into the hall: the computer-room air conditioners.
2. With every row facing the same way, the hot air blown out of the back of one row is sucked into the front of the next, so you turn the rows round, fronts facing across a cold aisle, backs across a hot aisle: hot and cold aisles.
3. Chilling all that air burns a lot of power, so water carries the heat up to towers on the roof, where some of it evaporates and cools the rest, the way sweat cools you: the cooling towers.
4. AI chips are so hot that one rack of them gives off about 120 kW, against under 8 kW for an average rack, and air can't carry it away, so water is piped through a metal plate on each of the big chips, the GPUs and CPUs: liquid cooling.

**The ending:** A rack of AI chips glowing hot, cooled by water running over each big chip, its heat handed to the building's water and carried up to the towers on the roof, a plume rising into the night as a phone below gets its answer: "Congratulations, you just built the liquid cooling of an AI data centre."

## Where it's known from

Apple Support (118431): if an iPhone gets too hot, charging slows or stops, the display dims or goes black, some apps slow down, and it can show 'iPhone needs to cool down'. APC White Paper 25 (Neil Rasmussen, 'Calculating Total Cooling Requirements for Data Centers'): the power consumed from the AC mains is 'essentially all converted to heat' (read through copies, since APC's own page now redirects). Google Cloud's incident report for 19 July 2022: 'a simultaneous failure of multiple, redundant cooling systems' in a London building, 'combined with the extraordinarily high outside temperatures', and 'We powered down this part of the zone to prevent an even longer outage or damage to machines'; the Met Office verified 40.3°C at Coningsby that day, the UK's record. ENERGY STAR: the hot aisle/cold aisle layout 'was originated by IBM in 1992'; without it, 'The hot exhaust air from the first row of racks gets drawn into the fronts of the second row'. Google (Joe Kava, 19 November 2012, 'Getting the heat out: A look at evaporative cooling'): two loops of water, meeting at heat exchangers, carry the heat from the floor to cooling towers on the roof, which use evaporation 'In the same way that perspiration removes heat from the human body'; chillers are a backup because they're 'not as efficient'. Uptime Institute, Global Data Center Survey 2024: average rack density remains below 8 kW. NVIDIA DGX GB200 user guide: 'The rack power consumption is approximately 120kW', and the compute trays are cooled by liquid through manifolds and cold plates attached to the CPUs and GPUs, while 'The rest of the components like networking and storage devices are air cooled'. Microsoft (John Roach, 6 April 2021): Christian Belady, 'Air cooling is not enough.'
