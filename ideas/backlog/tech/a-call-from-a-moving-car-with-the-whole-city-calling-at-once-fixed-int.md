## How it goes

**Everyday:** Making a call or scrolling on a phone in a car or on a train.

**Goal:** Let everyone in the city call at once, even on the move.

**Invention:** the mobile phone network

**Seen it:** Every time you call or scroll in a car or on a train, and on every three-faced mast beside the road.

**Cycle:** talk, fade, hand over, talk

**Inside:** The city from above with each tower's patch drawn as a hexagon, coloured by its channels; the call as a stream of balls hopping from tower to tower as the car crosses the patches; one tower's three panels each throwing its own beam.

1. One giant tower in the middle of the city has only a handful of channels: in 1960s New York, 2,000 people with car phones shared twelve and waited half an hour to make a call. So you put up many small, weak towers, each covering a patch, and reuse the same channels in patches that don't touch: the cells.
2. Small patches mean the car drives out of one mid-call and the call dies, so the network hands your call to the next tower without dropping it: the handoff.
3. Downtown, a patch's channels fill up at rush hour, so you split the busy patches into smaller ones, each reusing the channels again: cell splitting.
4. Smaller cells mean far more towers to build, so each tower gets three faces pointing three ways, each covering its own slice as a cell of its own: the sectors.

**The ending:** A three-faced tower on a hill above the motorway, the call hopping from tower to tower as the car drives on, thousands of other calls flowing beside it: "Congratulations, you just built the mobile phone network."

## Where it's known from

IEEE History Center (ETHW), 'The Foundations of Mobile and Cellular Telephony': before cellular, 'A single transmitter on a central tower provided a handful of channels for an entire metropolitan area'; '2,000 subscribers in New York City shared just twelve channels, and typically waited thirty minutes to place a call' (in its paragraph on the improved service of 1965); Bell Labs' Donald Ring proposed in 1947 many low-power transmitters, with different frequencies in adjacent cells reused within a city; Amos Joel devised a way to switch the call automatically from one cell to the next; AT&T and Illinois Bell opened the first modern cellular system in Chicago in October 1983. Joel's patent US 3,663,762 (filed 21 December 1970, granted 16 May 1972) keeps calls going as mobiles pass from one transmitter's coverage into another's. V. H. MacDonald, 'Advanced Mobile Phone Service: The Cellular Concept', Bell System Technical Journal 58(1), January 1979: frequency reuse and cell splitting are the essential features of the cellular concept (its abstract; I didn't read the full paper). Sectors: Motorola's patent US 4,128,740 (Graziano, filed 1977): each site has several sectored antennas, the corner-lit layout it improves on uses three antennas of 120°, and one of its stated objects is 'to minimize the number of antenna sites in an RF communications system for a given number of channels in a given area'.
