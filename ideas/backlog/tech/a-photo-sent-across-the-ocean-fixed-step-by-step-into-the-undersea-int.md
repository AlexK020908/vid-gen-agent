## How it goes

**Everyday:** Sending a photo, a message or a video call to someone on another continent.

**Goal:** Get the photo across the ocean in the blink of an eye.

**Invention:** an undersea internet cable

**Seen it:** Every time you message, call or send a photo to another continent.

**Cycle:** flash, fade, boost, flash, land

**Inside:** A cut through the cable: hair-thin glass fibres in the middle, the copper sleeve around them glowing with power, steel wires and plastic outside; a repeater on the seabed made glass, a dim flash going in and a bright one coming out.

1. You beam it up to a satellite, but that's 36,000 km up, so a message and its reply take over half a second, and satellites carry only a sliver of the traffic between continents, so the photos queue at the dish. So you lay a cable along the seabed: the submarine cable.
2. Make it copper, as the first across the Atlantic was in 1858, and it took almost 16 hours to send 98 words, then failed within weeks. So you send flashes of light down glass as thin as a hair, carrying ten times what the last copper cable could: the optical fibre, first across an ocean in 1988.
3. The light fades in the glass until it's too dim to read, so every 50 to 100 km a box on the seabed boosts it: the repeater.
4. A box at the bottom of the sea has nothing to plug into, so a copper sleeve inside the cable carries up to about 15,000 volts from the beach to every repeater: the power feed.
5. Near the shore, ships' anchors and fishing trawls drag across it and snap it, two-thirds of about 200 breaks a year, so there it's wrapped in steel wires and a plough towed by a ship buries it in the seabed: the armour.
6. It still breaks now and then, so a cable ship drags a hook along the seabed, lifts the broken ends aboard and splices in new cable, while your photo goes round by another cable: the repair ship.

**The ending:** The photo as a flash of light racing along the seabed, boosted at each repeater, up a beach near New York and onto the friend's phone: "Congratulations, you just built an undersea internet cable."

## Where it's known from

TeleGeography, Submarine Cable FAQs: satellites account for just 0.37% of all US international capacity; a cable is typically as wide as a garden hose, its glass fibres about the width of a hair; roughly 200 faults a year (ICPC data), fishing vessels and ships dragging anchors causing two-thirds; traffic moves to other cables while one is repaired. ESA ('Types of orbits'): geostationary satellites are 35,786 km up. Viasat ('Satellite internet latency: What's the big deal?', 30 Nov 2021): latency is how long a signal takes to get from A to B and back, and from a geostationary satellite it 'might be 600 ms', because of the distance. IEEE Spectrum (Allison Marsh, 31 Oct 2019): the 1858 Atlantic cable took almost 16 hours to send the queen's 98-word greeting, and within a few weeks the line failed entirely; the inquiry blamed Whitehouse's high voltages (up to 2,000 volts), but the article also cites later analysis that impurities and poor winter storage made its failure inevitable, so the card names no single cause. IEEE Milestone, TAT-8: the first fibre-optic cable to cross an ocean entered service on 14 December 1988, across 5,846 km of seabed between North America and Europe, with ten times the capacity of the last copper cable. Repeaters: an arXiv paper (2401.16997) puts them about every 50 km to make up the fibre's loss; Neptune Subsea's patent US 11,271,654 (2022) works an example of 6,000 km with a repeater every 90 km, all fed in series with the same current, and calls 15 kV 'typically an upper limit' on the cable's voltage; Xtera's engineers (Lightwave, 10 Jan 2022) describe each repeater as erbium-doped fibre amplifiers pumped by lasers. The European Subsea Cables Association ('Submarine Telecommunication Cables', escaeu.org): glass fibres not much thicker than a hair, 'a copper core (needed to transmit power to signal boosting equipment)', and steel armour wires depending on where the cable lies; burial is preferred on the shallow continental shelf, most often by a 'cable plough' towed by a cable ship, which lifts a furrow of the seabed and lays the cable in; 'The overwhelming majority' of faults around the UK shelf are caused by fishing trawlers or ships' anchors; to repair a cable it must be in two pieces, each end is brought to the surface and tested, stock cable from the ship is spliced in, and an ROV reburies it with a water jet. Lightwave (Stephen Hardy, 3 July 2017) confirms a real repair: MainOne's cable broke at 3,400 m off Senegal on 18 June 2017, a repair vessel came from the Atlantic Cable Maintenance Agreement, and the final splice was made on 2 July. The hook dragged along the seabed (a grapnel) and the ship's name, Pierre de Fermat, are from TeleGeography's write-up of that repair, which I read only through a search summary.
