## How it goes

**Kind:** real

**The break:** At Paddington the two needles flick out K, W, A, K, E, R, a letter at a time on the dial, while on the map the marker for the train is still crawling up the line. The word is puzzled out and sent again until it lands, and a sergeant in a plain coat is waiting on the platform when the train pulls in.

**Convicted:** John Tawell, of the murder of Sarah Hart, at the Buckinghamshire assizes in Aylesbury, March 1845; hanged at Aylesbury on 28 March 1845.

**People:** Sarah Hart, who's killed (named, as the record names her; a mother of two); the man in the Quaker's coat, John Tawell (named, convicted); the telegraph clerks at Slough and Paddington (roles); Sergeant William Williams of the railway police (named). Invented stand-ins, no likenesses.

**Shown as:** Told in one line and never shown: her cottage window at dusk, its lamp going out with a soft ring of light. No body, and the poison is never named.

**The ending:** The telegraph at Paddington, its two needles falling still under the letters KWAKER, the wire running off beside the empty line. Said: 'Tried and hanged that March, he was caught by a message that couldn't even spell Quaker.'

## Where it's known from

Science Museum Group, object 1876-1276: the Slough instrument 'used to send the message which identified John Tawell as a murder suspect on 1 January 1845'. Sir Francis Head's Stokers and Pokers (1849, ch. XIII) prints the message, with the 7h. 42m. train and 'a brown greatcoat on, which reaches nearly down to his feet', and tells of the omnibus and the lodging in Scott's Yard, Cannon Street; Head spells the word 'Quaker'. The KWAKER spelling and Paddington's requests to repeat it come only from later histories (the University of Salford's page; Carol Baxter, The Peculiar Case of the Electric Constable, Oneworld, 2013). The trial is reported in the Annual Register for 1845 (as Wikipedia cites it; I haven't read it). That no rider could beat the train is my own step.
